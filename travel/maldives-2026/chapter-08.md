The water was cloudy with plankton, hardly the ideal conditions for underwater photography. But to the manta rays it was exactly the attraction. On 15<sup>th</sup> October we had one of those snorkelling encounters that remains vivid long after getting out of the water: Reef mantas moving gracefully through the murk, feeding as they went.

## A Shape Emerging from the Blue

A manta has a way of making even a sizeable stretch of ocean feel suddenly occupied. At first there is little more than a shadow in the water, an indistinct shape gradually resolving itself into something sizeable and unmistakable. Then the broad triangular fins become apparent, moving in a measured rhythm, and the shadow becomes an animal of wonderful precision and grace.

These were reef manta rays, *Mobula alfredi*, one of the two generally recognised species of manta ray. Although smaller on average than their oceanic relatives, reef mantas can still reach a disc width of several metres. Seeing one from above, suspended in the water rather than displayed in an aquarium or on a screen, gives a rather different appreciation of its size.

The contrast between its apparent effortlessness and my own clumsy efforts to keep position in the water was considerable. Watching from the surface, the important thing was to leave room and let the animal carry on with what it was doing.

Their appearance was spectacular, but the behaviour was at least as interesting as the sight itself.

## Flying Through Water

One of the most striking things about a manta ray is the way it swims. Most fish propel themselves primarily by moving their tails from side to side. Mantas do something quite different. Their greatly enlarged pectoral fins form broad, flexible wings, which they move up and down in a form of underwater flight. The movement is sometimes described as *mobuliform locomotion*, a specialised swimming style characteristic of manta and devil rays.

The fins don't simply flap like rigid paddles. They flex and change shape during each stroke, generating thrust and lift as they move through the water. The result is a remarkably smooth form of propulsion, well suited to an animal that spends much of its life travelling through open water in search of food.

Watching the footage afterwards, I was particularly struck by how much of this movement is easy to miss in real time.

Three successive images from the encounter capture different stages of a swimming stroke. In the first, one of the manta's pale undersides is prominently visible as its fin rises. In the next, the fin changes position, revealing more of the dark upper surface. By the third, the broad fin has swept downwards, creating an entirely different silhouette.

Seen together, the images reveal the extraordinary flexibility of the manta's body and the continuous, flowing nature of its movement.

There is something almost birdlike about it, although the comparison only goes so far. A bird flies through air, a manta through water, and the physical demands are very different. Yet the resemblance is sufficiently strong that watching a manta can feel less like watching a fish swim and more like watching something fly.

Perhaps that's why the word *gliding* seems so appropriate.

## The Value of Murky Water

Plankton can make the water look disappointingly cloudy through a camera. Suspended particles scatter the light, reducing detail and contrast. Much of my footage from the encounter was affected by precisely that problem. But the cloudiness wasn't just photographic bad luck. It was part of the story.

Mantas are filter feeders. Rather than pursuing individual fish, they consume small planktonic animals suspended in the water, including copepods and other tiny crustaceans. Water enters through the mouth and passes across specialised filtering structures associated with the gills, allowing food particles to be retained. The two fleshy projections on either side of the mouth, known as *cephalic fins*, help guide water towards the mouth during feeding. When not in use, these structures can be rolled up, giving the front of the animal a rather different appearance.

Where plankton becomes concentrated, feeding can be particularly rewarding. Currents, tides and local underwater topography all influence the distribution of plankton, creating temporary patches of abundant food. This helps explain why manta encounters can be so unpredictable. The animals aren't visiting a particular location for the benefit of snorkellers. They're responding to an ever-changing underwater environment.

The very thing that made the encounter difficult to record helped explain why it happened.

## An Animal Worth Watching

There is a temptation during a wildlife encounter to concentrate on obtaining the perfect photograph, particularly when the opportunity might not come again. But the mantas were a reminder of how much can be gained simply by watching. The movement of the fins, the subtle adjustments of the body, the way an animal changes direction without any apparent urgency — these are details that reveal themselves when attention shifts from capturing an image to observing behaviour.

The video captures something of that experience. The manta's movement is continuous and fluid, and its shape changes dramatically as the fins rise and fall. Even through the plankton-clouded water, the grace of the animal is unmistakable.

Afterwards I spent time reviewing and improving the GoPro clips. Some were simply too murky to rescue meaningfully. A few captured enough of the encounter to keep. I rather like that imbalance - the experience was far greater than the images I brought back.

## A Visitor at the Villa

The mantas weren't confined to an organised outing.

On 16<sup>th</sup> October, a reef manta swam gracefully past the deck of our water villa. There was no boat trip, no search, and no particular expectation of seeing anything extraordinary. The animal simply happened to be passing through the waters surrounding the island. It was a wonderful privilege to see such a creature without having to do anything more elaborate than look towards the water.

That unexpected appearance also reinforced something which became increasingly apparent throughout our stay. The reef, lagoon and surrounding ocean are not separate from Moofushi. They are the living environment of which the island is only a small part.

For us, the manta's appearance was an exceptional event. For the manta, it was presumably just another moment in its ordinary life.

## Curiosity That Continues

The encounter also strengthened my interest in manta conservation.

Reef mantas are long-lived animals with relatively slow reproductive rates. Females generally give birth to a single pup after a lengthy gestation, and reproduction is infrequent. This means that populations cannot quickly replace animals lost through fishing or other pressures.

Their dependence on productive feeding areas also makes the protection of important habitats particularly significant. In the Maldives, researchers have been able to identify individual mantas through the distinctive patterns of dark spots on their pale undersides, building up long-term records of their movements, associations and use of particular locations.

It's a fascinating example of how repeated observations of individual animals can contribute to a much larger understanding of a species.

The encounter made me want to know more about where mantas travel, what they depend upon and how their future can be protected and I subsequently adopted a Maldivian manta named _Cheeky Monkey_ through the [Manta Trust](https://www.mantatrust.org), a small personal connection to that wider story of monitoring and protecting these remarkable animals.
