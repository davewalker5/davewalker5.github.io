Not all the wildlife at Moofushi is swimming over the reef. Some of the most enjoyable observations occurred within a few steps of the restaurants, villas and paths.

A tiny island can contain a surprising number of small lives, most of which are easy to overlook if you're always looking towards the sea.

## The busy beach

On 18<sup>th</sup> October, a ghost crab provided a delightful little demonstration of engineering.

It was excavating a burrow in the sand near the Manta Restaurant. Down the hole it went, then out it came carrying material, which it deposited on a growing spoil heap a foot or so away from the entrance to its burrow before disappearing underground again.

Nothing particularly dramatic happened. That was precisely what made it interesting. Watching an animal carry out an ordinary task reveals a world of activity that continues whether anyone happens to notice or not.

Ghost crabs appeared frequently in the diary, alongside land hermit crabs and swift-footed rock crabs. On another evening, we were treated to a rather different display of crab behaviour.

We were sitting on the decking at the Manta Restaurant, enjoying our evening meal, with the sand and water's edge below us gently illuminated by the lights beneath the decking. It provided an unexpectedly good vantage point from which to observe the crabs going about their business on the beach.

What initially looked like a collection of crabs wandering about apparently at random gradually revealed something rather more organised. There seemed to be an informal system of right of way, with smaller crabs generally giving way to larger ones as they encountered one another. Occasionally there were little confrontations, particularly when a morsel of food was involved, with individuals tussling briefly over whatever prize they'd discovered before one eventually prevailed.

Another curious detail was that they all seemed reluctant to venture into the water. Despite moving about quite freely across the sand, they appeared to treat the water's edge as a boundary, keeping clear of the advancing sea.

I wouldn't want to read too much into what we were seeing. Whether the apparent pecking order reflected size, dominance, simple avoidance or something else entirely would require rather more systematic observation. But there was something fascinating about watching these little encounters unfold. What might easily have passed unnoticed as background activity became, once we started paying attention, a small and surprisingly intricate world of its own.

## In the vegetation

The trees and undergrowth held a different assortment of inhabitants. We saw house geckos, oriental garden lizards and the large Indian flying foxes (*Pteropus medius*).

The oriental garden lizards were daytime residents, occasionally spotted among the vegetation or resting on tree trunks, where their colouring could make them surprisingly difficult to distinguish from their surroundings. The geckos were rather less particular about the time of day, appearing both during daylight hours and in the evenings, around the villas and other buildings.

The flying foxes were perhaps the most conspicuous of the island's non-human mammals, and they provided an interesting comparison with our earlier visits. Previously, we'd largely noticed them returning to the island in the evening, their distinctive silhouettes crossing the sky as the light began to fade. This time, however, we saw them flying back towards the island at almost any time of day. Whether this represented a genuine change in their behaviour or simply reflected when and where we happened to be looking, I couldn't say.

The two photographs of flying foxes accompanying this chapter actually date back to our visit in 2015, when I had a particularly memorable encounter with one of these remarkable animals. I watched it arrive in a coconut palm beside our beach villa, settle itself among the fronds and gradually make itself comfortable. It then hung upside down, apparently looking down towards our outdoor shower. Quite what it made of the arrangements below is anybody's guess, but it provided a wonderful opportunity to observe and photograph an animal that we'd more usually seen only in flight.

Seen close up, flying foxes are extraordinary creatures. Their great leathery wings and impressive size contrast rather wonderfully with their fox-like faces, large eyes and russet-coloured fur. There's something particularly appealing about seeing one at rest, hanging apparently effortlessly from a palm tree, rather than passing overhead against the evening sky.

Together, these small inhabitants added another dimension to the island's wildlife. The lizards moving through the vegetation, the geckos appearing around the buildings and the flying foxes travelling overhead were reminders that Moofushi's natural world extends well beyond the reef and the shoreline. There was always something else going about its business, often quite independently of whether anyone happened to be watching.

## The beetle and the palm

The island's insect life provided plenty of opportunities for observation, including coconut rhinoceros beetles, yellow oriental paper wasps, moths and dragonflies.

The coconut rhinoceros beetle (*Oryctes rhinoceros*) was particularly memorable. A substantial, heavily built insect, its flight is accompanied by a remarkably distinctive buzzing, quite unlike the more delicate sounds one might associate with smaller flying insects. There's something almost improbable about watching such a bulky creature take to the air, its wings somehow managing to carry that heavily armoured body from one place to another, and at some speed.

Identifying it was an interesting exercise in its own right. Our initial encounters didn't provide enough detail for a confident identification, although its size, shape and distinctive flight suggested that we were dealing with a fairly substantial beetle. Subsequent sightings gradually revealed more of its characteristics, each adding another piece to the puzzle, until the unmistakable horn projecting from its head provided a particularly convincing clue. What had initially been little more than an unfamiliar buzzing insect became a recognisable member of the island's wildlife.

Coconut rhinoceros beetles are an interesting presence on a tropical island, not least because their relationship with coconut palms is rather less benign than their fascinating appearance might suggest. Adults bore into the crowns of palms to feed, potentially damaging developing fronds and sometimes the growing point itself.

On one occasion, we watched a member of the resort staff drilling into the trunk of a palm near reception. We'd seen something very similar in Lanzarote the previous year, where palms were being treated against beetle infestation by injecting insecticide directly into their trunks. This technique, known as *endotherapy*, allows a treatment to be carried through the tree's internal tissues. It's also used against coconut rhinoceros beetles and other palm pests, so I wondered whether we were witnessing a similar effort to protect Moofushi's palms. I couldn't confirm what treatment was being carried out, but the resemblance was certainly striking.

It was another reminder that even creatures we find fascinating can have a complicated relationship with their surroundings, and that maintaining the island's lush vegetation may involve rather more active management than is immediately apparent.

## Other small lives

Other insects were less conspicuous but no less deserving of attention. Yellow oriental paper wasps appeared around the island, while moths and other small flying insects were encountered among the vegetation and around the resort lighting. These were the sorts of animals that could easily pass unnoticed against the more spectacular wildlife of the reef, yet each had its own place in the island's ecology.

The dragonflies were especially interesting. The wandering glider (*Pantala flavescens*) deserves a chapter of its own because its extraordinary migrations connect this tiny island to a much wider world, far beyond the horizon visible from its shores.

Not every encounter resulted in an immediate or certain identification. Some questions were resolved through repeated observations, others through photographs examined afterwards, and a few remain provisional. But that's part of the pleasure of keeping a sightings diary: an initial observation needn't be the end of the investigation. Sometimes it's only the beginning.

## Making room for the ordinary

Resort life and wildlife coexist in interesting ways. A heron may use a foot-wash jar, crabs may excavate near the restaurant, and insects appear beneath lights or among the plants. These small encounters brought us closer to the quieter, less conspicuous rhythms of island life.

The mantas, sharks and turtles understandably dominate the photographs and the memories but I suspect that, years from now, I'll still remember that ghost crab methodically building its little spoil heap on the beach.
