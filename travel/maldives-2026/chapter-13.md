Not all the wildlife at Moofushi is swimming over the reef. Some of the most enjoyable observations occurred within a few steps of the restaurants, villas and paths.

A tiny island can contain a surprising number of small lives, most of which are easy to overlook if you're always looking towards the sea.

## The busy beach

On 18 October, a ghost crab provided a delightful little demonstration of engineering.

It was excavating a burrow in the sand near the Manta Restaurant. Down the hole it went, then out it came carrying material, which it deposited on a growing spoil heap before disappearing underground again.

Nothing particularly dramatic happened. That was precisely what made it interesting. Watching an animal carry out an ordinary task reveals a world of activity that continues whether anyone happens to notice or not.

Ghost crabs appeared frequently in the diary, alongside land hermit crabs and swift-footed rock crabs. On another evening, crabs on the beach seemed to be eyeing one another up beneath the restaurant lighting.

## In the vegetation

The trees and undergrowth held a different assortment of inhabitants. We saw house geckos, oriental garden lizards and the large fruit bats that I habitually refer to in my notes as *bfrats*.

Geckos appeared at night, while the lizards offered opportunities for observation in daylight. Their shapes, colouring and movements are very different from those of the birds, and their presence gives the island vegetation another layer of interest.

The fruit bats, moving overhead, added something entirely different again: mammals in a landscape where our attention was so often fixed on marine life.

## The insects

There were coconut rhinoceros beetles, including the unmistakable buzzing of one in flight; yellow oriental paper wasps; moths; and dragonflies.

The wandering glider deserves a chapter of its own because its migrations connect this small island to a much wider world. But there was no shortage of other invertebrates to investigate.

Not every identification was straightforward. Some emerged from photographs examined afterwards; others are still best treated as provisional. That's one advantage of keeping a sightings diary: it preserves the original observation without demanding that every question be answered immediately.

## Making room for the ordinary

Resort life and wildlife coexist in interesting ways. A heron may use a foot-wash jar, crabs may excavate near the restaurant, and insects appear beneath lights or among the plants.

Sometimes the human environment creates opportunities for animals; sometimes it poses difficulties. I wouldn't want to assume that proximity always means the relationship is beneficial.

What I do know is that these small encounters made the island feel more alive.

The mantas, sharks and turtles will understandably dominate the photographs and the memories. But I suspect that, years from now, I'll still remember that ghost crab methodically building its little spoil heap on the beach.
