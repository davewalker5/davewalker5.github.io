On 19 October we swapped our familiar stretches of the Moofushi house reef for a different sort of marine encounter: snorkelling with nurse sharks and rays.

It's one thing to glimpse a shark passing below a walkway or along the edge of a reef. It's quite another to enter the water where several large animals are moving around you.

## Into the water

The encounter was organised through Blue Tribe. Once in the water, the sharks' movements quickly became the centre of attention: large bodies passing with a calm, assured economy that is difficult to appreciate from photographs alone.

The animals were identified to us as nurse sharks. I'd avoid assigning a precise scientific name from the trip notes alone, particularly because common-name and species identifications can become confused between oceans. The photographs and video are a better starting point for checking the identification properly.

Alongside the sharks were large rays, later identified from our observations and imagery as pink whiprays, *Pateobatis fai*.

## Two very different designs

Watching sharks and rays together is a striking reminder of how varied the shapes of cartilaginous fishes can be.

The sharks' bodies are recognisably streamlined, with fins and tails that immediately signal their familiar form. The rays seem almost to flow through the water, broad bodies and long tails creating a very different silhouette.

Neither design is better understood simply by calling the animals graceful. The interesting part is watching how their bodies work in the environment they inhabit.

I was fortunate enough to take photographs and video, although being in the middle of an encounter inevitably changes what can be recorded. Sometimes there are simply too many things to look at.

## A view from above

Blue Tribe also provided drone footage of the excursion, photographed by Tiger. We purchased the footage and received permission to share it.

The aerial perspective is a revelation. The relationship between the swimmers, the sharks, the rays and the surrounding water becomes much clearer. Moments that felt close and immediate in the sea can be seen as part of a much larger scene.

I've used the footage and stills to create a photographic montage, which seems a particularly appropriate way to introduce the experience.

## After the encounter

The afternoon didn't end with the snorkelling. We returned to Moofushi and later joined a sunset cruise, the boat bobbing gently home as the first stars appeared.

It's a lovely contrast: the concentration of watching enormous animals in the water, followed by the quiet pleasure of drifting back towards the island under an emerging night sky.

Experiences like this tend to get described as thrills, and there's certainly excitement involved. But for me the lasting memory is rather different. It's the opportunity to see these animals as living creatures, moving with complete assurance in a world where I am only an awkward, temporary visitor.
