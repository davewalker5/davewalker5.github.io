Some wildlife encounters are unforgettable because they're spectacular. Others stay with you because of how they make you feel. Our dolphin-watching excursion from Moofushi on 13<sup>th</sup> October managed to be both.

## Not Quite Unicorns

We were fortunate enough to encounter dolphins during our visit to Moofushi in 2015, and the possibility of seeing them again was one of the pleasures of returning to the island. But dolphins are wild animals, travelling freely through an enormous ocean, and there's no guarantee that they'll appear simply because we've come looking for them.

We got friendly with some other guests during our stay, and dolphins became something of a running joke between us. Despite their own efforts, they hadn't been fortunate enough to see any. We began joking that perhaps dolphins were mythical creatures, rather like unicorns, whose existence was supported by enthusiastic accounts but frustratingly little firsthand evidence.

It was all good-natured fun, of course, but there was a grain of truth beneath the joke. Wildlife watching is a curious mixture of anticipation, patience and chance. You can be in exactly the right place, at what seems to be exactly the right time, and see nothing at all. On another occasion, the ocean offers something extraordinary.

Having been lucky in 2015, we were hoping our good fortune might hold.

## Out on the Water

The trip took us away from the island and out into the surrounding ocean, where the scale of the atoll feels rather different. From the water villa, the reef and lagoon define a familiar little world. Beyond them is a much larger one.

The Maldives consists of scattered islands and reefs surrounded by deep ocean, and the waters around the atolls are home to several species of dolphin.

We went hoping to see them, but with the understanding that there might be nothing to see. There's never a guarantee with wildlife, and I rather like that. The animals are not part of a timetable.

On this occasion, however, we were fortunate.

## A Pod, and a Youngster

We encountered a pod of dolphins, including a youngster travelling with the group. Subsequent examination of a photograph supported their identification as spinner dolphins, *Stenella longirostris*.

Spinner dolphins are relatively small, slender dolphins, recognisable by their long, narrow beaks and distinctive colouration. Their common name comes from their spectacular habit of leaping clear of the water and rotating around their longitudinal axis, sometimes several times in a single jump.

Not every encounter involves these aerial displays, however. Much of a dolphin's life takes place at or beneath the surface, and simply watching a group moving together through the water can be captivating.

Spinner dolphins are highly social animals, often travelling in groups that can number dozens or even hundreds of individuals. Their social relationships and group composition can be complex, and the presence of younger animals is a reminder that these aren't merely collections of individuals sharing the same stretch of ocean.

I can't claim to know what the dolphins were thinking or how they experienced our presence. What I can say is that watching them together was deeply affecting. My diary entry uses the word *emotional*, and it's the right word.

## A Life Beneath the Surface

There's something particularly interesting about spinner dolphins in the tropical Indian Ocean.

Studies of spinner dolphins have revealed a fascinating daily rhythm. Research in the Maldives and Chagos archipelagos has found that the animals frequently enter atoll lagoons during the morning, spend much of the day resting in relatively sheltered waters, and depart again in the afternoon to feed offshore at night [1].

This pattern has also been documented in other parts of the world, notably Hawaii, where decades of research have helped establish our understanding of spinner dolphin behaviour [2].

Their prey includes small fish, squid and crustaceans associated with deeper waters, some of which migrate towards the surface at night. The dolphins' movements are therefore connected to the daily rhythms of life far beneath the ocean's surface.

Like other dolphins, they rely heavily on sound to navigate and interact with their surroundings. Their acoustic world includes echolocation clicks and a variety of whistles and other vocalisations.

It's tempting, when watching dolphins at the surface, to imagine that we're seeing a substantial part of their lives. In reality, we're catching occasional glimpses of animals whose activities are largely hidden from us. A dorsal fin breaks the surface, a back rolls through the water, and then the animal disappears again. Beneath that brief visible movement is a much richer existence, taking place in an environment we can only visit for short periods.

Perhaps that's part of their fascination.

## What the Moving Image Remembers

Cameras are useful, and I wouldn't willingly have left mine behind. They help identify species and recover details that pass too quickly to register at the time. But photographs rarely reproduce the character of an encounter. They don't fully capture the anticipation, the movement of the boat, or the repeated excitement of dolphins appearing and disappearing around us.

The video from this excursion preserves something different.

Watching the dolphins surface and move through the water gives a better impression of the encounter's rhythm. One moment there's only the ocean; the next, a fin or a curved back appears, followed by another. Then the surface closes over them again. It's not the spectacular spinning leaps for which the species is named that make this footage valuable to me. It's the opportunity to watch the animals simply being dolphins, moving through their own environment.

And, of course, the recording preserves a little of the experience for a time when the holiday itself has become a more distant memory.

## The Privilege of Seeing Them

There's a distinction between knowing that an animal exists and encountering it in the wild. We can read about dolphins, study their behaviour, watch documentaries and admire photographs. All of those things have value, and they can deepen our understanding of the animals enormously.

But an encounter in the wild adds something that is difficult to describe. The animal is there independently of us, pursuing a life that has nothing to do with our holiday, our expectations or our cameras. We are, for a little while, observers.

And perhaps that's also why the joke about unicorns was so apt. Our friends' experience was no less authentic because they hadn't seen dolphins. They had simply been reminded of something that wildlife watching teaches repeatedly - nature doesn't owe us an appearance.

When it does offer one, it's worth appreciating.

## Back at Moofushi

We returned to the island and celebrated with Prosecco at the Manta Bar. It seems an entirely fitting way to mark a rather extraordinary outing.

By evening the usual rhythms of the resort resumed - dinner, the lagoon, the familiar sounds of the island. But something had been added to the experience of being there.

We had now encountered dolphins on two separate visits to Moofushi, eleven years apart. The experiences weren't identical, and there was no reason to expect them to be. That, in itself, is part of what makes returning to a place so rewarding.

The ocean beyond Moofushi was no longer merely a vast, beautiful expanse. For a brief time, it had offered us another glimpse into the lives of the animals moving through it. And I suspect I'll remember the youngster for a very long time.

As for the unicorns? Well, we have the video to prove they exist!
