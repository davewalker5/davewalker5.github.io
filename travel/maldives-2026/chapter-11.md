Blacktip reef sharks have become a bit of a feature of our visits to Constance Moofushi. Our previous visits had been earlier in the year, around March time, so we were fortunate enough to encounter the young sharks. At little more than 12 inches long, we affectionately christened them "BBTs", "Baby Blacktips".

They were delightful animals, unmistakably sharks despite their diminutive size, already possessing the characteristic shape, markings and movements of their larger relatives.

This time, visiting in October, we weren't treated to quite such tiny youngsters, but nevertheless we'd barely arrived at Moofushi before the sharks began appearing. On our first day, a blacktip reef shark passed the water villa. Soon there were further sightings - one beneath a walkway, juveniles patrolling the shallows, and occasional larger individuals moving through the lagoon.

It became something of a familiar pleasure to look into the water and discover one passing by. Sometimes there would be a shark, sometimes several, and sometimes nothing at all. They were simply going about their lives, apparently indifferent to our fascination with them.

## A shark perfectly at home

The blacktip reef shark (*Carcharhinus melanopterus*) is a characteristic inhabitant of tropical coral reef environments throughout the Indo-Pacific. Its distinctive dark-tipped fins, particularly the prominent black tip on the first dorsal fin, make it one of the more readily recognisable reef sharks.

It is also a species particularly associated with shallow coastal waters, reef flats and lagoons.

For young blacktips, these environments are especially important. Very shallow water can provide a degree of refuge from larger predators, including larger sharks, which may be less able to manoeuvre effectively in such confined spaces. The complex structure of coral and the changing depth of the reef flats can offer further protection.

There is something rather appealing about the idea that the shallow water in which we enjoy paddling and snorkelling is also a place where young sharks can find relative safety.

Of course, the lagoon isn't merely a nursery. It forms part of a much wider living reef environment, used by sharks of different sizes for movement, feeding and other activities. The reef and lagoon are not separate worlds but connected parts of a single ecosystem.

Watching blacktips of different sizes moving through the waters around Moofushi gives a small glimpse into that wider life.

## Grace in motion

One of the things that has struck me most strongly about blacktip reef sharks is just how extraordinarily graceful they are in the water.

Sharks are so often presented through the language of danger, aggression and predation that it's easy to overlook their sheer elegance.

Watching one swim is a rather different experience.

The body moves with a smooth, economical rhythm, the tail sweeping from side to side while the pectoral fins hold the animal beautifully balanced. There is no obvious sense of effort, no frantic movement. The shark seems to glide through the water with a quiet assurance, changing direction through subtle adjustments of its body and fins.

One evening, we watched and filmed a blacktip patrolling the shallows in the darkness. The illuminated water revealed the shark moving steadily through the lagoon, its pale underside occasionally catching the light as it changed direction.

It was mesmerising.

Without the distractions of a busy reef scene, the movement itself became the focus. The shark seemed almost to flow through the water, its streamlined body and measured tail movements revealing an animal superbly adapted to its environment.

It is one of those occasions when video captures something that a still photograph cannot quite convey. The beauty lies not simply in the animal's appearance, but in the way it moves. And, of course, the shark's presence after dark was a reminder that the lagoon's inhabitants continue their lives long after we've finished our own day's activities.

## Something unusual on the way to breakfast

Most of our encounters involved sharks swimming individually, or simply passing through an area without any particularly obvious interaction.

Then, on the morning of 14th October, we saw something rather different. Walking to breakfast, we noticed a group of blacktip reef sharks, each roughly a metre long, interacting in the shallow water. Rather than simply cruising past one another, the animals were actively engaged with each other. We watched several individuals swimming closely together, pursuing one another and occasionally twisting and rolling in the water. There was also what appeared to be biting behaviour, with one particular individual seemingly the focus of much of the attention.

It was quite unlike the relatively unhurried, solitary patrols we'd become accustomed to seeing.

My immediate impression was that the behaviour might have been related to courtship or mating. In some shark species, including blacktip reef sharks, mating can involve males pursuing and biting females, sometimes holding them with their teeth during the process. There were certainly elements of what we observed that seemed consistent with such an explanation but consistency is not confirmation.

Without being able to identify the sexes of the animals, establish the complete sequence of events or obtain a specialist interpretation of the footage, it would be premature to describe the encounter definitively as courtship.

The interactions might have had another explanation altogether.

I wrote in the diary that I felt privileged to witness it. I still do.

We had happened upon a small episode in the lives of these animals, something that was clearly significant to them but whose meaning we could only begin to speculate about.

## Familiar sharks, unfamiliar lives

There's an interesting distinction between recognising an animal and understanding its behaviour.

After several visits to Moofushi, blacktip reef sharks are becoming familiar to us. We know what they look like, we recognise their characteristic movements, and we know some of the places where we're most likely to encounter them but that familiarity can be deceptive.

A shark swimming along the edge of the lagoon might be travelling between feeding areas, searching for prey, or simply moving through part of its usual range. We can observe the movement, but we cannot necessarily know its purpose. Likewise, a group of sharks swimming together may be engaged in social interaction, competition, courtship or some other activity that is not immediately apparent to an observer.

Even the simplest questions can be surprisingly difficult to answer.

- Why were those particular sharks together at that particular time?
- Why was one animal apparently receiving so much attention?
- Was the interaction unusual, or merely something that happens regularly when nobody is watching?

The more closely we observe, the more questions emerge and that's one of the great pleasures of wildlife watching. An encounter doesn't have to produce a definitive explanation to be worthwhile.

Sometimes the most valuable outcome is simply a better question.

## Looking again

One of the advantages of taking photographs and video is the opportunity to revisit an encounter after the initial excitement has passed. A fleeting observation can become something to examine more carefully. Movements that were difficult to follow in real time can be watched again, slowed down and compared with descriptions of known behaviour.

The photographs from our 14<sup>th</sup> October encounter is particularly interesting in this respect, since it allows us to examine the pursuit, the close interactions and the apparent biting more carefully.

It also provides a useful reminder of the limitations of observation. A camera records what happens within its field of view, not everything happening around it. Important behaviour may have occurred before filming began or outside the frame. Nor can a photograph or video necessarily reveal the motivation behind an animal's actions.

The challenge is to distinguish what we actually saw from what we think it might mean.

That distinction doesn't diminish the encounter. If anything, it makes it more interesting.

## Wildlife on the doorstep

Perhaps the most remarkable thing about our encounters with blacktip reef sharks at Moofushi is how unremarkable the circumstances often are. We weren't on an organised wildlife excursion, or searching for something unusual. We might be walking to breakfast, standing on the steps of our villa, crossing one of the jetties or simply looking into the lagoon. And there, sometimes just a few metres away, would be a shark.

It is easy, surrounded by the comforts of a beautiful resort, to forget that we are visitors in an extraordinarily rich natural environment.

The lagoon isn't a decorative feature of the island. It is a living habitat, connected to the surrounding reef and the wider Indian Ocean. The sharks, rays, turtles, fish and countless smaller creatures that inhabit it are not there for our entertainment, although observing them gives us enormous pleasure - they are simply living their lives.

Over our visits to Moofushi, the blacktip reef sharks have become something approaching familiar neighbours. From the tiny BBTs of our earlier visits to the graceful night-time patrols and the intriguing group interactions of this trip, each encounter has added a little to our appreciation of them.

Perhaps that is what I value most about returning to the same place. Familiarity doesn't diminish the sense of wonder. Quite the opposite. Each visit provides another opportunity to notice something that escaped us before, to recognise a behaviour or to ask a new question.

The blacktips may have become familiar, but their lives remain largely a mystery.
