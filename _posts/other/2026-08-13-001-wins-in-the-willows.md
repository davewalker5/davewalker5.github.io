There are days when the Thames feels less like a river running through the landscape and more like the landscape itself: a long, slow thread connecting fields, villages, wildlife, pubs, locks, boats and people.

Today was one of those days.

I walked from Abingdon to Oxford along the Thames Path, following a route that has become familiar enough that I no longer feel any great need to hurry along it. The pleasure is increasingly in the interruptions.

Birds. Butterflies. Plants. Things moving in the margins of vision that make you stop, look again and, sometimes, reach for a notebook.

By the time I reached Oxford the accumulated list of sightings had become gratifyingly long. There is something about making such lists that changes a walk. The countryside ceases to be simply green and begins to resolve itself into individuals: this bird, that flower, a butterfly crossing the path, something calling unseen from the reeds. Attention gives the landscape texture.

Eventually, though, there are other necessities.

A pint at The Head of the River, for one.

Lunch presented a small and unexpected test of principle. Octopus was on the menu.

A few weeks ago I might have ordered it without much thought. But I’m currently reading Peter Godfrey-Smith’s Other Minds, and once you have spent time thinking about an animal possessing a nervous system distributed through its arms, exploring its world with extraordinary curiosity and displaying behaviour uncomfortably suggestive of individual character, ordering one for lunch becomes rather more complicated.

So: no octopus.

I’m not yet quite sure where that line of thought leads. But perhaps one of the tests of serious reading is whether, occasionally, it follows you out of the book and into the world.

And then came the return journey.

Rather than retracing the Thames Path, I boarded a Salters boat for the slow trip back to Abingdon.

Walking beside a river and travelling on it are surprisingly different experiences. On foot, the Thames is something you accompany. From a boat, you become temporarily subject to its pace.

The afternoon was warm and bright. The engine settled into a steady thrum beneath us and Oxford began to slip slowly away.

At Iffley Lock we descended almost imperceptibly.

The gates closed behind us and there came that peculiar watery slushing sound as the lock emptied. The river level crept visibly down the stone walls beside the boat. Inches became feet. The outside world seemed briefly to rise around us.

And then the downstream gates opened.

For a moment they framed the Thames beyond them like a theatre curtain being drawn back: water, trees and sky suddenly extending away ahead.

The boat moved on.

Somewhere along the bank came the unmistakable smell of someone enjoying a rather different kind of riverside vegetation.

At Sandford Lock the air was full of swallows.

Masses of them.

They swooped low over the water, climbed, turned and dived again, stitching invisible patterns through the warm afternoon air with an agility that made the boat beneath them seem almost geological in its slowness.

In the fields, cattle grazed with the magnificent indifference of cattle everywhere: brown and white, black and white, heads buried in the grass and bottoms presented squarely towards the river.

A small armada of Canada and greylag geese headed downstream.

Water lilies lay flat upon the surface, their yellow flowers held just above it.

Around me came the gentle background noise of the boat itself: conversations overlapping, laughter, children talking, the occasional change in note from the engine.

And gradually even observation became optional.

Warmth.

Sunlight.

The rhythmic throbbing of the engine through the hull.

I dozed.

There is a particular luxury in travelling slowly towards somewhere you already know. Nothing needs to be accomplished. There is no uncertainty about the destination. The landscape arrives when it arrives.

Eventually Abingdon did too.

The appropriate conclusion seemed obvious: a pint of Weston’s and a pint of Diet Coke at The Nags Head, beside the river from which I had just stepped ashore.

A walk to Oxford.

A boat home.

Birds, butterflies, cattle, geese, swallows and water lilies.

Locks filling and emptying.

A lunch choice quietly changed by a book.

Sunshine, beer, river water and an afternoon doze to the pulse of a boat engine.

A thoroughly Wind in the Willows sort of day.

And perhaps that is as good a description as any.
