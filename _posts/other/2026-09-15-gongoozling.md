There is something stupidly comforting about the sound of ducks quacking.

I discovered — or perhaps rediscovered — this today sitting on a bench beside the Thames at Abingdon, with a small crowd of ducks gathering around me and some rye bread to distribute amongst them.

It is not exactly a sophisticated pastime. Tear off a little piece of bread. Throw it. Watch several ducks immediately decide that this particular morsel is the most important object in the universe. Repeat.

All accompanied by quacking.

Lots of quacking.

And somehow it is enormously relaxing.

Part of the appeal, I think, is that quacking is communicative, but not communicative to me. I am surrounded by a little society carrying on with its business, but there is no demand that I respond, interpret, decide or perform. I can simply sit there and listen to it happen.

And ducks are such immediate creatures. They paddle, fuss, forage, squabble over bread, then settle again. Watching and hearing them pulls attention into the present because their world is so conspicuously about what is happening right now.

Perhaps that is part of why it feels so grounding.

I also think part of the reason is that everything was moving at the correct speed.

Nothing was hurried. Nothing needed improving. Nothing was demanding that I get on with it.

For a while the world contracted to ducks, bread and noise. Their concerns were admirably immediate: *bread has appeared; bread must be acquired.*

I could learn something from ducks.

Around that small centrepiece, the rest of the afternoon seemed to fall naturally into the same rhythm.

Boats made their way lazily through Abingdon Lock, providing the opportunity for a little entirely legitimate **gongoozling** — that wonderfully specific word for standing around watching activity on canals and locks while contributing absolutely nothing to it.

They too were moving at the correct speed.

The swifts and swallows initially seemed to have disappeared. I found myself wondering whether they had already begun to leave, then the swallows suddenly appeared again overhead and corrected me.

Further along the bank, a group of greylag geese were making equally leisurely progress towards the river, cropping the grass as they went. No urgency there either. Eat a bit. Walk a bit. Eat another bit.

Again: the correct speed.

The sun was out. It was warm.

And eventually the afternoon ended with a glass of wine beside the river at the Nags Head.

None of this amounts to very much when written down: ducks, boats, birds, sunshine and a glass of wine.

But perhaps that is precisely the point.

So much of life seems to be organised around moving faster, getting somewhere, finishing something, making progress. This afternoon was a reminder that there are times when the better thing is simply to fall into step with what is already happening around you.

The ducks were quacking.

The boats were easing through the lock.

The geese were grazing their way towards the water.

And, for a little while, everything — including me — was moving at the correct speed.

It was lovely.
