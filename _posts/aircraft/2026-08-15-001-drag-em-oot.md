There are aircraft that first announce themselves on a screen, and there are aircraft that announce themselves by sound.

This morning I heard the latter.

A remarkably deep, throaty piston-engine note overhead prompted me to look at the aircraft tracking data. The source turned out to be rather more interesting than the usual light aircraft crossing Oxfordshire: N473DC, a Douglas C-47A/DC-3C Dakota known as _Drag-’em-Oot_.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.flight_path %}

## The aircraft

N473DC is Douglas construction number 19345, originally delivered to the United States Army Air Forces on 28 December 1943 as 42-100882.

It subsequently joined the 87th Troop Carrier Squadron, operating from Greenham Common in England. Equipped for glider retrieval, the aircraft acquired the name Drag-’em-Oot — essentially, “drag them out”.

Its wartime history includes operations associated with D-Day and later airborne operations in Europe. More than eight decades later, the aircraft remains airworthy.

That makes the rather ordinary act of hearing an aeroplane pass overhead distinctly less ordinary.

## Those engines

The sound was what caught my attention before anything else.

N473DC is powered by a pair of Pratt & Whitney R-1830 Twin Wasp radial piston engines. Each is a two-row, fourteen-cylinder air-cooled radial with a displacement of approximately 30 litres.

Across the aircraft, that means:

* 2 engines
* 28 cylinders
* approximately 60 litres total displacement
* around 1,200 horsepower per engine

Modern turbine-powered aircraft can be surprisingly unobtrusive at altitude. The Dakota is anything but.

Even before identifying the aircraft, the character of the sound was striking: low, resonant and distinctly mechanical. Once the aircraft type was known, the source became obvious. Two large radial engines turning large propellers produce a sound belonging to a very different period of aviation.

## A track becomes a record

One reason I have been developing the flight-path recording tools is precisely for encounters like this.

A passing aircraft is normally ephemeral. It appears, crosses the sky and disappears. At most there is a memory of having seen — or in this case primarily heard — it.

Recorded telemetry changes that.

The flight-path plot preserves the geometry of the encounter: where the aircraft travelled, its relationship to the observation point and, depending upon the available data, its altitude and progress through the local airspace.

That data can then provide the starting point for a much wider investigation.

In this case a few seconds of unusually throaty engine noise led from:

> sound → aircraft track → registration → aircraft type → engines → individual airframe history

That sequence is very much what Field Notes is about.

The observation itself was small. The trail of questions behind it was considerably larger.

And somewhere in that chain is the rather wonderful fact that an aircraft built during the Second World War was still flying over Oxfordshire on an otherwise ordinary Saturday afternoon in 2026.
