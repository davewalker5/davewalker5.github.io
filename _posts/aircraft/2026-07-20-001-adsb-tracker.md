Some projects move steadily towards a clear destination. Others develop in fits and starts. The ADS-B Tracker, or _ADS-B BaseStation Reader_, is one of the latter.

Over time it accumulated a capable collection of components: a console tracker, an ADS-B simulator, a SignalR-based Tracker Hub, a database of observations, browser-based live tracking, and a set of Jupyter notebooks for analysing and visualising flight paths.

Each part was useful, but they never quite felt like a single application.

Recently, I returned to the project and gave it some long-overdue attention.

The result is an integrated user interface that brings much of that work together for the first time.

## An older strand of Field Notes

Aircraft observation predates much of what Field Notes has since become.

My interest in wildlife and conservation goes back considerably further still, but aircraft tracking was one of the earlier areas in which I began systematically combining observation, software and data.

Over time, the centre of gravity of Field Notes has shifted: Wildlife recording, seasonal ecology, palaeontology, computational natural history and the questions emerging from them now occupy much more of my attention, and that feels entirely right.

Aircraft data now has a quieter place on the site: another observational dataset, with tools and reports available through the reference material rather than a major section in its own right.

But quieter does not mean unimportant.

Returning to the Tracker reminded me that there was still good work there — and that some of it had never quite been finished.

## The pieces were already there

The Tracker had gradually evolved into several related applications and workflows:

- The console application could receive and process ADS-B messages
- The Tracker Hub could expose live tracking data through SignalR
- A browser interface could display aircraft being tracked in real time
- The observation database retained historical records
- Separate Jupyter Notebooks could reconstruct and visualise aircraft flight paths

None of these pieces was particularly lacking on its own. The problem was that they remained pieces.

To move from live tracking to historical records or flight-path analysis meant moving between different tools and workflows.

The recent work has largely been about changing that, not by replacing the existing architecture, but by bringing together the parts that naturally belong together.

## An integrated view

The Tracker Hub now includes an integrated user interface built around several related views of the same observational system:

- The live tracker provides a visual counterpart to the console tracker, showing aircraft currently being observed and the data associated with them
- The radar view places currently tracked aircraft in spatial context around the receiver, making it easier to see how individual observations relate to the wider pattern of movement through the local airspace
- A database browser allows historical tracking records to be explored directly
- Aircraft tracks can be examined using a two-dimensional altitude-versus-distance plot

The existing flight-path rendering work has also been incorporated, allowing historical tracks to be visualised geographically without leaving the application.

More recently, the interface has gained support for selecting **tracking profiles**.

These profiles define the observational criteria used when deciding which aircraft should be tracked, including constraints such as:

- Altitude limits
- Distance from the receiver
- Whether an aircraft is climbing, descending or level

That is a small feature in interface terms, but an important one conceptually. At it's simplest, a tracking profile is just a filter but it also describes what kind of observation I am interested in making:

- One profile might focus on low-level aircraft approaching or departing nearby airports
- Another might be concerned with higher-altitude traffic crossing the area

The same incoming ADS-B data can therefore support different observational questions.

That feels much closer to the way I think about the rest of Field Notes.

## Integration rather than reinvention

One thing I am particularly pleased about is that the integrated UI has not required abandoning the existing architecture.

- The console tracker still exists
- The simulator, used to generate a dummy aircraft message feed when no receiver is available, still exists
- The Tracker Hub remains a separate component
- Lightweight tracking can still run independently — including on a Raspberry Pi — while a richer interface can run elsewhere
- The database remains a durable record of observations rather than being tied to the user interface

That separation has proved useful over time.

The new UI simply provides a better way of working with the system as a whole. Not another major capability or a complete rewrite. Just a coherent surface over work that already existed.

In retrospect, that was what the project had been missing but an attempt to implement a solution using a desktop technology stack, a year or two ago, was such a painful experience that I'd set the idea of a rich UI aside until now.

## Is it finished?

Probably not!

I am increasingly suspicious of the word *finished* when applied to hobby projects as there will almost certainly be more observations to make, reports to build, bugs to fix and ideas to explore.

That said, the project now feels complete in a way that it did not before:

- The main pieces fit together
- The architecture makes sense
- The historical data can be explored
- Live observations can be followed
- Flight paths can be analysed and visualised
- Different observational intentions can be expressed through tracking profiles

It feels less like a collection of experiments and more like a coherent tool.

And, importantly, I am pleased with it.

## A quieter place does not mean an abandoned one

The ADS-B Tracker is unlikely to become one of the central strands of Field Notes again but, crucially, that is not a judgement on its value. It is simply a reflection of where curiosity has led.

Wildlife, ecology, palaeontology and computational natural history currently generate more questions for me, and therefore more work.

But one of the pleasures of a long-running personal project is that interests do not have to compete for survival:

- They can move into the background
- They can remain useful
- They can wait

And sometimes, years later, they can receive a little attention and become better than they were before.

That is what has happened here.

The ADS-B Tracker has had some TLC.

And I think it deserved it.
