The position-density replay added something important to my ADS-B BaseStation Reader.

Instead of seeing only the final spatial pattern left behind by an observation session, I could watch that pattern develop.

Individual aircraft became tracks. Tracks began to overlap. Repeated movements formed corridors, and those corridors gradually became the larger observational footprint visible to the receiver.

That raised another question.

> Once an observation session has finished, how should I describe it?

A density plot captures an important part of the answer, but only part of it.

A session also has a duration, a population of aircraft, an altitude structure, a pattern of activity through time, a spatial extent and a particular degree of reference-data coverage.

If repeated sessions are eventually going to be compared, each one first needs to be characterised in its own right.

## From replay to session report

The reporting suite for the ADS-B application already contained several historical reports.

Those deliberately aggregate data across many completed observation sessions: which aircraft have appeared most often, which types are commonly observed, where positions tend to accumulate, and how activity varies over longer periods.

That aggregation is useful precisely because it suppresses some of the detail of individual sessions.

For the observational work I am now carrying out, however, the individual session is itself an important unit of study, so I've added a session-level reporting layer organised around three questions:

- **Session Characterisation** — what made up this observation?
- **Session Spatial Structure** — what shape did the observed airspace take?
- **Session Data Context** — how complete is the information available for interpreting it?

The reports are still produced outside the live application in Jupyter notebooks.

The tracker remains the observing instrument. The notebooks examine what that instrument recorded after the observation is complete.

## The temporal heartbeat of a session

One of the new visualisations is a five-minute activity timeline.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.session_activity_timeline %}

This particular session remained active throughout the observing period.

The number of simultaneously active aircraft moved up and down, but generally remained between roughly thirty and fifty. New aircraft continued to enter the observation throughout the hour, while others left reception.

The position-record count tells a similar story.

The session was not one short burst of intense activity surrounded by long quiet periods. Thousands of position records continued to arrive during successive five-minute intervals.

That is useful information which the final density plot cannot contain: The density plot says where observations accumulated while the activity timeline says something about **when the observations contributing to it occurred**.

## What was being observed?

The session contained **228 distinct aircraft**.

Where aircraft could be identified from the local reference database, Boeing and Airbus aircraft dominated the observation.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.aircraft_by_manufacturer %}

The most numerous individual type was the Boeing 737-800, followed by several Airbus A320-family and Boeing 737 variants.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.aircraft_by_type %}

There is an important distinction here from the historical reports: Across many sessions, it makes sense to ask which particular aircraft are **frequently observed** but within one session that is usually not very meaningful. An individual aircraft normally contributes one passage through the observation.

The useful question instead becomes:

> What was the composition of this particular sample of airspace?

The manufacturer and aircraft-type distributions provide one description of that composition.

## The completed spatial pattern

The session accumulated **49,206 position records**.

Those records ultimately occupied **503 cells** in the persisted position-density grid.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.final_position_density %}

The final pattern is already highly structured.

Several concentrations and extended corridors can be seen, with the strongest density forming through the central part of the observed region.

It is tempting to start explaining those structures but at this stage, I am deliberately not doing so.

This is one observation session within a larger baseline study. A line on the plot may represent a recurring feature of the surrounding airspace, or it may simply reflect the aircraft which happened to pass through during this particular hour.

That distinction can only be addressed through repetition.

For now, the plot is a description:

> This is the spatial footprint produced during this observation

## How quickly did that footprint appear?

Persisting density snapshots makes it possible to go beyond the completed picture. For every snapshot I can calculate how many of the cells present in the final density grid had already appeared and for this session:

- Approximately **50%** of the final occupied cells had appeared after **5 minutes**
- Approximately **75%** after **15 minutes**
- Approximately **90%** after **35 minutes**
- Approximately **95%** after **50 minutes**

That is perhaps the most interesting new measurement produced by the session-level reports. Not because those particular numbers are yet significant, but because they make a previously qualitative question measurable.

When I first began replaying density snapshots, one of the questions was:

> How quickly does a recognisable traffic pattern emerge?

The reports can now ask a more precise version:

> How long does it take an observation to acquire a given proportion of its final spatial coverage?

One session cannot answer whether the result is typical but repeated sessions can.

## Observation envelope and observation duration

This also links directly to another question in the current observational study.

> How long does an ADS-B observing session need to be?

A longer observation almost inevitably records more aircraft and more positions but the spatial envelope may behave differently.

If the broad shape regularly appears early and later observation principally adds density to already occupied regions, a relatively short observation may be sufficient to characterise spatial structure but if important parts of the envelope continue appearing late in sessions, longer observations may be required.

The distinction is between accumulating **more observations** and discovering **more of the observable space**.

The persisted snapshots now provide a way of separating those two things.

## Knowing what I do not know

The session reports also include information about reference-data coverage.

Of the 228 distinct aircraft observed during this session, **199 could be identified locally**, giving aircraft-reference coverage of approximately **87%**.

Flight identification is currently very different. There were **195 distinct callsigns**, but none could yet be resolved through the local flight-reference data.

That does not invalidate the observation.

The position, altitude, range and density measurements come from the received ADS-B-derived data. Knowing the aircraft registration, airline, origin or destination adds useful context, but the spatial observation does not depend upon every aircraft being identified.

Indeed, recording the coverage explicitly is useful in its own right as it distinguishes:

> What the receiver observed

from:

> How much additional meaning my local reference data can currently attach to those observations

## A session as an analytical object

The earlier density-replay work made me think of an observation session as a field record and the new reporting has strengthened that idea. A session is now more than a container holding aircraft and position rows in a database: It has several complementary descriptions:

- A population of observed aircraft
- An activity pattern through time
- An altitude and range structure
- A completed spatial footprint
- A sequence through which that footprint developed
- A record of how completely the observations can be identified

None of those replaces the others. Rather, together they describe one bounded period during which an instrument observed a dynamic system.

## One observation is not a baseline

There is an obvious danger in producing attractive plots this early - they invite interpretation!

The current _Airspace Weather Baseline Study_ is deliberately based on repeated, methodologically consistent observations. Its purpose is to discover which features recur, which vary, and whether different times of day or days of the week produce meaningfully different observable structures.

So I am treating these first reports as **instrument and method development**, not as findings.

- I now know that a completed session can be described consistently
- I know that its final spatial envelope can be measured
- I know that the development of that envelope can be quantified rather than merely watched

What I do not yet know is whether another comparable observation will look the same.

That is the interesting part.

One session can show what the measurements look like.

Twenty sessions may begin to show which parts of the airspace persist.
