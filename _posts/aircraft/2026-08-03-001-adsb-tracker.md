A few days ago I wrote about how historical reporting had changed the way I thought about the ADS-B Tracker.

A position-density report had combined thousands of recorded aircraft positions into something that felt unexpectedly personal.

> That's my airspace!

The report showed the accumulated structure of the airspace visible from my receiver over a number of completed observation sessions.

What I hadn't expected was that the same idea would soon find its way back into the live application — and become something rather different.

## From report to observation

My approach to reporting has deliberately been to keep it outside the application.

The ADS-B Tracker's integrated UI is intended to support the immediate observation workflow. Historical reporting is implemented separately using Jupyter notebooks, where it can remain exploratory, flexible and unconstrained by the needs of the operational interface.

That separation still feels right. But it doesn't mean that an idea discovered through reporting can never belong in the application.

The position-density report proved visually striking and genuinely useful. It revealed where aircraft positions had accumulated and began to show the recurring structure of the airspace around the receiver.

The question was whether that same visual approach could serve a useful purpose during an active observation session.

Not:

> Where have aircraft been observed across many sessions?

But:

> Where is activity accumulating during this session?

That shift in focus was enough to turn a historical report into a live observational view.

## Watching the tracks grow

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.position_density_early %}

The live position-density plot groups recorded positions into hexagonal spatial bins. Each occupied cell shows somewhere an aircraft has been observed during the current session. The colour indicates how many position observations have accumulated within that area.

With a broad tracking profile and many aircraft, the result would gradually become a conventional density plot. But while testing it with my **Home – Level Flight** profile, something more immediate appeared.

The profile deliberately prunes the received traffic, retaining only a relatively small number of level-flying aircraft passing through the local airspace.

With so few aircraft present, their individual paths remain visible. They look rather like caterpillars!

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.position_density_middle %}

With each refresh, the chains of hexagons extend across the plot. The chart is not showing a conventional line drawn between positions. It is showing the accumulation of observations in the areas through which the aircraft have passed.

Even so, the flight paths are unmistakable. The aircraft progress through the local airspace and leave a temporary trace behind them.

## A spatial memory of the session

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.position_density_later %}

The Radar view answers an immediate question:

> Where are the aircraft now?

The Position Density view answers a different one:

> Where have they been during this observation?

That distinction gives the live application a new temporal dimension. The aircraft visible on the radar are transient. They enter the receiver's range, move across it and eventually disappear. The density plot retains the shape of their passage.

As the session develops, it acquires a kind of spatial memory.

The tracks lengthen. Frequently observed areas become more prominent. Separate movements begin to reveal corridors and patterns.

The resulting image belongs only to that observation session. It is shaped by:

- The receiver and its location
- The selected tracking profile
- The time and duration of the session
- The aircraft that happened to pass through
- The limitations of what the receiver was able to detect

It is not a global picture of aviation - it is a record of what was observable from one place during one bounded period of attention.

## The tracking profile as an observational lens

The tracking profile has always been an important part of the application. It controls which aircraft remain of interest according to criteria such as altitude, distance and behaviour.

But the density plot made its role feel more concrete.

The profile is not merely filtering records from a table - it is defining the phenomenon being observed.

A Heathrow-arrivals profile might reveal one set of paths. A home level-flight profile reveals another. A broader all-traffic profile would produce a denser and less individually legible picture.

Changing the profile changes the character of the observation and in that sense, it acts rather like an observational lens. It does not change the airspace itself, but it determines which part of its activity is brought into view.

## When the application became an instrument

The ADS-B Tracker began as a collection of software components. It could receive BaseStation messages, maintain aircraft state, store observations, publish live updates and support later analysis.

All of that work was necessary, but it did not by itself make the application feel like an observational instrument.

That changed with the integrated UI. The Live Tracker now supports a deliberate workflow:

- prepare an observation session;
- choose the receiver and tracking profile;
- begin tracking;
- monitor aircraft through complementary views;
- watch the session develop;
- stop deliberately;
- review what was recorded;
- return later for further analysis.

The Position Density view fits naturally into that workbench.

- **Tracking** shows the aircraft as live entities
- **Radar** shows their current spatial relationship to the receiver
- **Position Density** shows the traces accumulating behind them
- **Summary** describes the session as a completed or developing whole

Together, those views do more than expose the underlying data. They support the act of observing.

## Incomplete, but richer

My original ambition for the Tracker placed much more emphasis on complete flight identification.

I imagined taking it somewhere such as Heathrow and having it automatically record fully resolved sightings while I concentrated on photographing the aircraft.

That remains technically imaginable, but I no longer think it is the most valuable use of the application.

The Tracker can record what the receiver observes, using a carefully chosen profile to focus the session. I can concentrate on photography or simply watch the traffic. Afterwards, I can research and resolve particular aircraft or flights when they interest me.

The result is not an end-to-end-complete data pipeline. It is something richer:

- An electronic observation session
- A photographic record shaped by my own choices
- An opportunity for later research and interpretation

Incomplete enrichment does not make the observation incomplete. An unresolved aircraft still passed through the receiver's range. Its position, altitude, direction and movement remain part of the session.

The observation can be valid even when some of its interpretation remains open.

## Letting the observations lead

There is also a satisfying development lesson here.

The position-density view was not invented because the application needed another chart. It emerged from using the historical reports, noticing that one visualisation changed how I understood the data, and then asking whether the idea could be adapted to support live observation. The report acted as an exploratory space.

The useful insight was then brought back into the application, but with its purpose changed and that suggests a healthy relationship between the two parts of the project:

> Reports explore the accumulated data while the application adopts only those ideas that genuinely improve the current observation

It prevents the integrated UI from becoming a warehouse of every chart that can be produced while still allowing worthwhile discoveries to become part of the observing workflow.

## Watching my airspace

A position-density plot sounds analytical but in use, it feels unexpectedly immediate.

Aircraft move through the local airspace and their tracks slowly appear behind them. The plot grows with the session. For a while, it holds the shape of where they have been.

- The receiver detects the signals
- The software records and organises them

But through the observation workbench, I can now watch those signals become a visible trace of activity across the sky around my home.

The Tracker is no longer simply software that processes aircraft messages.

It has become one of the instruments through which I observe a place.
