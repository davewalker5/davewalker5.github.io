One of the pleasures of collecting aircraft observations is that an apparently ordinary entry in a database can unexpectedly open a door into history.

While looking at the ages of aircraft detected during one of my local observation sessions, I applied a fairly arbitrary filter for aircraft at least sixty years old. Among the results was this:

At around 85 years old, G-APLU was already an interesting outlier.

Then I started looking into its history.

What emerged was an aircraft that appears to have begun life as an RAF trainer built only a few miles from where I now live, subsequently passed through the French overseas civil register, spent part of its life in Aden or Yemen, returned to Britain, was restored to flight, survived a substantial accident in the 1990s and is still flying today.

My flight-track plot shows its passage through the local airspace during the observation session.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.flight_path %}

The track, however, captures only a few minutes in an aircraft history extending across eight decades.

## From Cowley to the RAF

G-APLU is a de Havilland DH.82A Tiger Moth II, construction number 85094. The surviving de Havilland production list records the aircraft as:

> 85094 — DH82A TIGER MOTH — T6825 — G-APLU — F-OBKK — VR-AAY — G-APLU

It also associates the aircraft with R. A. Bishop and M. E. Vaisey. ([AirHistory](https://www.airhistory.org.uk/dh/p850.html))

The first identity in that sequence, T6825, was its RAF serial.

Although it was a de Havilland design, G-APLU was built not at Hatfield but by Morris Motors at Cowley, Oxford, in 1941. The RAF Museum confirms that wartime Tiger Moth production was subcontracted to Morris Motors at its Cowley car factory; its detailed history of another Cowley-built Tiger Moth notes that Morris eventually built some 3,500 of the type. ([RAF Museum](https://www.rafmuseum.org.uk/research/archive-exhibitions/de-havilland-the-man-and-the-company/world-war-ii/))

That gives this particular observation a pleasing local connection. An aeroplane built at Cowley during the Second World War was still flying through Oxfordshire airspace approximately 85 years later.

Exactly where T6825 served during its RAF career is something I have not yet been able to document from a primary service record. It is routinely described in later sources as an RAF trainer, which would of course be entirely consistent with the principal wartime role of the Tiger Moth, but a complete unit-by-unit service history remains something to find rather than assume.

## G-APLU appears

After military service, T6825 entered the British civil register as G-APLU.

Here the dates become less well documented in readily accessible primary material. A later aircraft-history description gives April 1958 for its initial registration as G-APLU and says that by July 1958 it had been exported and become F-OBKK. That precise chronology should therefore be regarded as provisionally sourced rather than established from the original register itself. ([eBay UK](https://www.ebay.co.uk/itm/195415361663))

The identity change itself, however, is considerably firmer: both the de Havilland production record and other aviation archives associate construction number 85094 with F-OBKK. ([AirHistory](https://www.airhistory.org.uk/dh/p850.html))

That raises the first intriguing question.

> Where had it gone?

## F-OBKK: into the French overseas register

The F-O registration series was used for French aircraft based outside metropolitan France. Consequently, F-OBKK tells us that the Tiger Moth entered the French overseas civil registration system, but not by itself which territory it operated in.

One secondary description specifically places F-OBKK in French Guiana. That is certainly plausible, but I have so far been unable to locate the contemporary French civil-register entry that would independently establish either its owner or its operating base.

This distinction matters.

We can say with considerable confidence that:

> G-APLU became F-OBKK

We cannot yet say with the same confidence that:

> F-OBKK was definitely based in French Guiana

French Guiana is therefore best regarded as a strong lead awaiting confirmation rather than as settled fact.

There is an additional reason for caution. Looking at nearby F-OBK* registrations does not reveal a simple territorial allocation pattern: neighbouring registrations can be associated with quite different parts of the French overseas world. The letters themselves therefore cannot be used to infer French Guiana.

For now the safest description is:

> F-OBKK — French overseas register; reported as French Guiana, but the contemporary registration record remains to be located

And then the aircraft changed identity again.

## VR-AAY: Aden, not Anguilla

The de Havilland production record gives the next registration as VR-AAY. ([AirHistory](https://www.airhistory.org.uk/dh/p850.html))

This initially produced a confusing trail because one later aircraft description identifies VR-AAY as an Anguillan registration. That appears to be wrong.

Historical aircraft-registration records associate the VR-A prefix with Aden, and aviation-history sources specifically describing construction number 85094 identify it as having been VR-AAY in Yemen. ([AirHistory](https://www.airhistory.net/airframe/1162/85094))

Air-Britain’s photographic database independently records G-APLU as construction number 85094 and lists VR-AAY among its former identities. ([ABPic](https://abpic.co.uk/pictures/view/1310562))

The Aden/Yemen part of the story is therefore considerably firmer than the French Guiana identification.

At some point after its period as F-OBKK, this 1941 Cowley-built Tiger Moth became:

> VR-AAY — Aden/Yemen

Exactly when remains uncertain.

The old VR-A registration allocation belongs to the period of British Aden and survived briefly into the political changes surrounding independence and the creation of South Yemen. The aircraft must therefore have received VR-AAY while that registration series was still active.

That potentially places its Aden/Yemen career somewhere between the late 1950s and the end of the 1960s.

## What was a Tiger Moth doing in Aden?

This is where fact starts to give way to reasonable inference.

I have found no evidence that VR-AAY was operated by Aden Airways, despite that airline having aircraft in the same general VR-AA* registration sequence. We should therefore not assume an airline connection merely from the registration.

A Tiger Moth would in any event have been an unlikely airline aircraft.

By this stage in its life it might plausibly have been used for any of several purposes: private flying, club flying, training, liaison work or another form of inexpensive light aviation.

But at present I do not know.

The most important missing document is therefore an Aden civil aircraft register entry for VR-AAY. Such an entry might identify the owner, registration date and perhaps the aircraft’s operating base.

Until that turns up, the responsible conclusion is simply that c/n 85094 genuinely spent part of its civilian career registered in Aden/Yemen as VR-AAY; its precise owner and role there remain unresolved.

That is still a rather remarkable journey for an RAF primary trainer built in Oxford in 1941.

## A gap in the story

There may then be another documentary gap.

The VR-A registration system disappeared around the end of the 1960s, while secondary histories report G-APLU returning to the British register only in 1977.

If both dates are correct, something remains to be explained between them.

Possibilities include the aircraft being stored or inactive, remaining in the region without an active registration, being exported and awaiting restoration, or carrying another identity that is absent from the abbreviated production records.

There is currently no evidence strong enough to choose between those possibilities.

Rather than hiding that hole in the chronology, I think it is one of the more interesting parts of the aircraft’s history: there is still research to do.

## Back to Britain

By the late 1970s the Tiger Moth was once again G-APLU.

Its later British history becomes much easier to trace photographically and through contemporary aviation reporting.

G-APLU was photographed at Old Warden on 28 May 1989 and at the PFA Rally at Cranfield on 2 July 1989, both times identified as DH.82A construction number 85094. ([Airport-Data](https://airport-data.com/aircraft/photo/000405692))

So by the summer of 1989, the aircraft that had begun life as RAF T6825 and subsequently travelled through at least two overseas civil registers was once again flying in Britain.

That might have been a satisfying end to the story.

It wasn’t.

## The 1994 accident

On 2 July 1994, G-APLU was involved in an accident at Rush Green Airfield in Hertfordshire.

Unlike some of the more obscure parts of the aircraft’s earlier life, this event is documented by the Air Accidents Investigation Branch. ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/aaib-reports/dh82a-tiger-moth-g-aplu-2-july-1994))

The Tiger Moth was departing from grass strip 34 in calm conditions with a pilot and one passenger aboard.

According to the AAIB report, it became airborne approximately halfway along the strip at around 42–45 knots. The pilot lowered the nose to accelerate to climbing speed, but instead the aircraft’s airspeed began to decay.

The pilot closed the throttle and landed again.

There was, however, insufficient strip remaining to stop. G-APLU struck the airfield boundary fence at approximately 30 knots and came to rest on its wheels around twenty yards into the adjoining field.

Neither occupant was injured. ([Digital Cabinet Office](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5422ff2140f0b61342000909/DH82A_Tiger_Moth__G-APLU_08-94.pdf))

The aircraft was less fortunate.

The AAIB recorded substantial damage to both lower wings, the landing gear, engine cowling and propeller. Subsequent examination reportedly revealed a lack of compression in one engine cylinder, which the pilot considered the probable explanation for the aircraft’s degraded take-off performance. ([Digital Cabinet Office](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5422ff2140f0b61342000909/DH82A_Tiger_Moth__G-APLU_08-94.pdf))

There is an interesting anomaly in the report. The AAIB form lists the aircraft’s year of manufacture as 1950.

That is incompatible with the construction-number evidence identifying c/n 85094 as wartime Tiger Moth T6825 and with other records placing its construction in 1941. The AAIB date therefore appears to be an administrative error rather than evidence of a different aircraft. ([Digital Cabinet Office](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5422ff2140f0b61342000909/DH82A_Tiger_Moth__G-APLU_08-94.pdf))

Most importantly, G-APLU was repaired again.

## Still flying

Later photographs trace G-APLU through the British vintage-aircraft scene for decades afterwards.

Air-Britain records it at the International de Havilland Moth Rally at Belvoir Castle in 2011. ([ABPic](https://abpic.co.uk/pictures/view/1310562))

It was photographed at the de Havilland Moth Club’s International Moth Rally at Woburn Abbey in 2013. (Airport-Data)

Another photographic record shows it participating with the Diamond Nine Tiger Moth formation at Shoreham and explicitly identifies it as the former VR-AAY in Yemen. ([Airplane Pictures](https://airplane-pictures.net/photo/240111/g-aplu-private-de-havilland-dh-82-tiger-moth/))

Modern flight-tracking data still identifies G-APLU, Mode-S 4014E6, as a de Havilland DH.82 Tiger Moth. ([Flightradar24](https://www.flightradar24.com/data/aircraft/g-aplu))

Which eventually brings the story all the way back to my receiver.

On an otherwise ordinary local observation session in 2026, 4014E6 appeared in the data.

The database row said:

> G-APLU — Tiger Moth — 1941 — age 85

Behind those few fields was something rather richer.

## Eighty-five years in one line of data

The history can currently be reconstructed approximately like this:

| Period                  | Identity        | Commentary                                                                   |
| ----------------------- | --------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1941                    | T6825           | DH.82A Tiger Moth, c/n 85094; Morris Motors/Cowley wartime production        |
| Post-RAF, 1958 reported | G-APLU          | Entered British civil register                                               |
| From 1958 reported      | F-OBKK          | French overseas civil registration                                           |
| Unknown                 | French Guiana?  | Reported by a secondary source; still requires confirmation                  |
| Late 1950s/1960s        | VR-AAY          | Aden/Yemen registration; well corroborated                                   |
| c. late 1960s–1970s     | ?               | Documentary gap                                                              |
| By late 1970s           | G-APLU          | Returned to Britain                                                          |
| 1989                    | G-APLU          | Restored to flight by Dick Bishop and Mike Vaisey                            |
| 1994                    | G-APLU          | Substantially damaged in aborted take-off at Rush Green; occupants uninjured |
| Post-1994               | G-APLU          | Repaired and returned to flight                                              |
| 2026                    | 4014E6 / G-APLU | Detected flying through my local observation area                            |

The distinction between documented fact and inference is important here:

- The identities T6825, G-APLU, F-OBKK and VR-AAY are all supported by airframe histories tied to construction number 85094
- The association of VR-AAY with Aden/Yemen is independently corroborated
- The association of F-OBKK with French Guiana is plausible and explicitly reported, but I have not yet found the contemporary French register necessary to treat it as proven
- Likewise, I do not yet know the owner or purpose of VR-AAY in Aden, nor what happened to the aircraft during the apparent gap between the end of the VR-A registration era and its return to the British register

Those are questions rather than conclusions.

## From aircraft age to aircraft biography

This started because I filtered my observations for aircraft more than sixty years old.

That now seems a much more interesting operation than I initially realised. For an ordinary contemporary aircraft, age is principally another descriptive variable. Once aircraft become sufficiently old, however, age begins to select for an entirely different kind of population: survivors.

An 85-year-old aircraft that remains active has necessarily accumulated a history of maintenance, ownership, preservation, restoration and often reinvention and G-APLU demonstrates that beautifully.

Its construction year doesn’t merely tell us how old it is. It connects an aircraft passing through Oxfordshire today with wartime manufacturing at Cowley, RAF training, the post-war disposal of military aircraft, overseas civil aviation, the remains of the British colonial registration system in Aden, the vintage-aircraft preservation movement and the practical work required to keep an elderly wooden-and-fabric biplane airworthy.

And somewhere within that history are still some unanswered questions.

I would particularly like to find:

1. The original French civil-register entry for F-OBKK, establishing its owner and operating territory
2. The Aden civil-register entry for VR-AAY, establishing its owner, registration date and perhaps its role
3. The detailed RAF service record of T6825
4. Documentation explaining the aircraft’s whereabouts between the end of its overseas career and its eventual return to Britain

So the investigation isn’t quite finished.

But there is already something rather satisfying about the result.

> A signal received locally in 2026 turned out to belong to an aeroplane built locally in 1941.

After eighty-five years, several registrations, at least one journey halfway around the world, a restoration and a fairly serious encounter with a Hertfordshire boundary fence, it had come back through the neighbourhood.

Not bad for one row in a SQLite query.
