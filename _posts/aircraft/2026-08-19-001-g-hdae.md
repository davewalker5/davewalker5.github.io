Another old aircraft appeared overhead today.

At 11:42 on 19 August 2026, G-HDAE, a de Havilland DHC-1 Chipmunk, passed nearby at around 2,300 feet and 90 knots, tracking northwest. It had departed Benson at 11:37 BST.

Built in 1951, the aircraft is now 75 years old.

And, rather pleasingly, there is a direct connection with another elderly de Havilland aircraft I spotted recently: the Tiger Moth.

## Replacing the Tiger Moth

The DHC-1 Chipmunk was de Havilland Canada’s first indigenous aircraft design. Work began shortly after the Second World War, with the new aircraft intended as a modern replacement for the de Havilland Tiger Moth that had trained enormous numbers of wartime pilots.

The prototype Chipmunk first flew at Downsview, Toronto, on 22 May 1946.

Although its purpose was much the same as the Tiger Moth’s — elementary pilot training — visually and technically it belonged to a different generation. The biplane wings and open cockpits of the Tiger Moth gave way to a low-wing monoplane, an enclosed tandem cockpit, flaps, wheel brakes and radio.

It retained some decidedly traditional features, however. The Chipmunk was still a tailwheel aircraft, powered in British-built versions by a 145 hp de Havilland Gipsy Major engine, and its two seats remained arranged in tandem, with the student in front and instructor behind.

The RAF began using the Chipmunk in 1950. More than 700 were eventually built for the RAF, and the type became a familiar sight with flying-training units and University Air Squadrons. Remarkably, Chipmunks continued to provide Air Experience Flights for cadets until 1996.

So the Tiger Moth and Chipmunk I have now watched flying overhead aren’t simply two old de Havilland aeroplanes separated by a decade.

They represent successive generations of British flying training.

## G-HDAE: from Chester to Portugal

The particular aircraft I saw today has a history extending well beyond Britain.

It was built by de Havilland at Chester in 1951, with manufacturer’s serial number C1/0280. It was one of a batch of ten British-built Chipmunks supplied to Portugal, where it entered military service as Portuguese Air Force 1304.

Portugal would become an important Chipmunk operator. After the first British-built aircraft arrived, further examples were manufactured under licence by OGMA in Portugal.

For 1304, the job was the one for which the Chipmunk had been designed: training pilots.

It remained in Portuguese military service for decades before finally being declared surplus in 1998.

## Madeira and CS-DAE

Retirement from military service was not the end of the aircraft’s flying career.

In 1998 it passed to a civilian flying club on Madeira and acquired the Portuguese civil registration CS-DAE.

There it underwent an extensive overhaul. The original fabric covering was replaced with Dacron and the aircraft received a complete repaint.

Its new colour scheme was strikingly yellow — reportedly chosen as a reference to one of Madeira’s best-known crops, bananas — while its former Portuguese Air Force markings and the number 1304 were retained. It also acquired distinctive nose art during this period.

Five years later the aircraft moved again.

## Becoming G-HDAE

In 2003, CS-DAE was bought by a British-based pilot and imported into the United Kingdom. It joined the British civil register as G-HDAE, the registration it still carries today.

Although subsequently based for a period in Belgium, it remained British registered.

Today G-HDAE wears another identity on the outside. It is painted to represent WP964, a Chipmunk formerly operated by the British Army Air Corps at Middle Wallop during the 1970s and 1980s, where Chipmunks were used in the training of forward air controllers.

The actual aircraft beneath those markings, however, remains C1/0280 — Portuguese Air Force 1304, later CS-DAE, and now G-HDAE.

Recent photographs still show the aircraft carrying the WP964 Army colour scheme.

## Today’s flight

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.flight_path %}

There is something slightly extraordinary about watching an aircraft like this on an ordinary day.

The dot moving across a modern flight-tracking app represents an aeroplane built in Chester in 1951; one that trained Portuguese military pilots, spent time flying from Madeira, crossed into civilian ownership, and eventually returned to Britain.

Seventy-five years after it was built, it is still doing what it was designed to do.

Flying.

And my growing, entirely accidental chronology of old aircraft passing overhead has acquired another entry:

|||
|---|---|
|1941|de Havilland Tiger Moth G-APLU|
|1942|North American Harvard IIB|
|1945|Supermarine Spitfire T.IX|
|1951|de Havilland DHC-1 Chipmunk G-HDAE|

The Chipmunk makes that sequence particularly satisfying. The first and last aircraft on the list aren’t merely separated by ten years of aviation development.

One was designed to replace the other.
