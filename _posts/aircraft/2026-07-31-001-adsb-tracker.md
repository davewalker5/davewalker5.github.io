A couple of weeks ago I wrote about returning to the ADS-B Tracker and finally bringing together several years of work into something that felt like a coherent application.

At the time, that seemed to be the story but looking back, I think it was only half of it.

Since then I've continued the work to turn the application into an integrated observation workbench and, most recently, have been working on historical reporting. Not dashboards inside the application, but a separate suite of Jupyter notebooks that analyse completed observation sessions.

The reports themselves are useful enough. They summarise observation sessions, aircraft activity, flights, position histories and temporal patterns.

But while building them I realised something I hadn't expected:

> The project had quietly changed ...

## From tracking aircraft...

Like many ADS-B applications, the Tracker began with a straightforward objective.

- Receive aircraft transmissions
- Decode them
- Display them
- Store them
- Identify them

Over the past week or so that has grown into observation sessions, reference-data management, historical browsing and live operational reporting.

Each addition made the software more capable, but it still felt as though the project was fundamentally about tracking aircraft.

That isn't quite how it feels now.

## ...to observing a place

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.position_density %}

One of the first reports I wrote produced a simple position-density plot.

Every recorded aircraft position from every completed observation session was plotted on the same axes.

I expected an interesting visualisation.

What I didn't expect was my immediate reaction.

> That's my airspace!

Not Heathrow. Not the United Kingdom. Not European aviation. The airspace visible from one particular place. Observed repeatedly over time.

Thousands of individual aircraft positions had combined into something quite different.

Instead of showing where one aircraft had flown, they were beginning to reveal the structure of the sky above my home.

## Patterns rather than aircraft

That small shift changes the kinds of questions worth asking.

Instead of:

- Where is this aircraft now?
- What route is it flying?

I find myself asking:

- Which manufacturers dominate here?
- Which airlines appear most frequently?
- Which corridors are consistently busy?
- How does activity change during the day?
- Which flights return again and again?

Those are observational questions rather than operational ones.

They describe the place being observed rather than the individual objects moving through it.

## The value of accumulation

At the moment the database is still small so many of the reports reflect only a handful of observation sessions.

Even so, they have already started to hint at longer-term patterns.

One chart currently suggests that Boeing aircraft dominate my observations.

That is almost certainly because the dataset is still small and my location is overflown by large numbers of Ryanair Boeing 737s and, interestingly, that doesn't match my much larger collection of manual aircraft observations, where Airbus and Boeing appear much more evenly balanced.

That isn't a contradiction:  It's exactly the kind of comparison that historical reporting makes possible.

As more observation sessions accumulate, it will be interesting to see whether the ADS-B data gradually converges on the same picture.

## Another branch of Field Notes

Aircraft have occupied a quieter place within Field Notes in recent months.

Wildlife, palaeontology and computational natural history have naturally become much larger parts of the site.

Working on the reporting suite reminded me that the underlying approach hasn't really changed.

Whether I am recording birds, fossil specimens or aircraft, the process is remarkably similar.

- Observe carefully
- Keep good records
- Accumulate evidence
- Look for patterns
- Allow those patterns to suggest better questions

The subject matter changes but the practice does not.

## Looking upwards

The reports are still young and as the observation database grows they will become richer without changing very much themselves.

More sessions will simply reveal more of the airspace around me.

That, I think, is the real lesson from this latest round of development.

I certainly was writing software to track aircraft — and I still enjoy doing exactly that. Watching an interesting aircraft appear overhead hasn't become any less enjoyable. But somewhere along the way the project acquired another purpose that I hadn't anticipated. The individual aircraft were beginning to reveal something larger: the character of the airspace around my home.

Instead, I was slowly building a way of observing a place.

And I suspect there is still a great deal more to learn from looking upwards.
