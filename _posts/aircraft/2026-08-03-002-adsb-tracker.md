Earlier today I wrote about watching aircraft leave traces across the live position-density plot in the ADS-B Tracker.

At first, the display showed a handful of short chains of hexagons. They looked rather like caterpillars moving through the local airspace. As the session continued, those tracks lengthened. More aircraft appeared. Paths crossed one another and frequently occupied areas became increasingly prominent.

Then, after several hours, the plot changed character again.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.position_density_envelope %}

The individual aircraft tracks had begun to merge into an oval. That was not something I had expected to see.

## From tracks to footprint

By this point the session had recorded:

- 169 aircraft with positions
- 10,410 individual position observations
- 849 occupied spatial bins
- and a peak density of 46 observations within a single bin

The plot still contained traces of individual routes, but they were no longer its most obvious feature.

Instead, the accumulated observations had started to reveal the overall region within which aircraft were being detected and retained by the active tracking profile.

What began as a view of aircraft movement was becoming a view of the observation itself.

## Not simply receiver range

It is tempting to describe the oval as the receiver's range.

That is probably part of what is visible, but the shape is more complicated than that.

The plot is the product of several interacting factors:

- The physical reception characteristics of the SDR receiver and antenna
- The altitude and position of the aircraft
- Surrounding terrain, buildings and local obstructions
- The routes aircraft happened to follow during the session
- The criteria imposed by the selected tracking profile
- The geometry of plotting latitude and longitude directly

The result is therefore not a pure technical coverage map but is better understood as an **observed operational envelope**. It shows the region within which aircraft were both detectable and relevant to this particular observation.

## The profile defines the phenomenon

The active profile for this session was **Home – Level Flight**.

Its purpose is to retain level-flying aircraft passing through the airspace around the receiver while pruning traffic that does not meet the selected criteria.

Earlier in the session, this produced a pleasantly sparse picture in which individual tracks remained easy to distinguish. Over time, however, those filtered tracks accumulated. The profile had not merely reduced the number of records displayed. It had defined the phenomenon being observed.

A different profile would almost certainly produce a different shape. A profile focused on Heathrow arrivals, for instance, might reveal a narrower family of approach tracks. A broader all-traffic profile might fill a larger and denser region. A more restrictive altitude or distance profile would produce a smaller envelope.

The visible footprint therefore belongs not only to the receiver, but to the observational question encoded in the profile.

## A boundary emerging from the data

The most interesting part is that this boundary was not calculated explicitly. I did not ask the application to estimate antenna coverage or draw a receiver-radius circle. The shape emerged from the accumulated positions themselves and that makes it an empirical boundary rather than a configured one, reflecting what was actually observed during the session.

The empty or lightly occupied regions are potentially as interesting as the dense ones. They may indicate:

- Weaker reception in a particular direction
- Fewer established flight paths
- Profile exclusions
- Altitude-dependent coverage
- Temporary gaps in traffic
- Simply parts of the envelope that had not yet accumulated enough observations

Repeated sessions using the same profile may eventually show which parts of the footprint are stable and which are shaped by the traffic of a particular day.

## Several scales of observation

The live density plot now seems to reveal several different things depending on how long the session has been running. Early in the session, it shows **individual movement**. A little later, it reveals **routes and intersections**. After more observations accumulate, those routes begin to form **traffic corridors** and, eventually, the corridors merge into the **spatial footprint of the observing system**.

The same visualisation moves through several scales:

> aircraft → tracks → corridors → observation envelope

That is what makes it more interesting than I anticipated. It is not merely a static heatmap whose values grow larger over time and its meaning changes as the session develops.

## An instrument observing itself

There is another aspect to this that I find especially appealing The ADS-B Tracker is intended to observe aircraft passing through the local airspace but this plot is also beginning to reveal the behaviour of the observing instrument.

It shows not only where aircraft travelled, but where the combination of receiver, antenna, software, profile and local conditions was able to observe them.

In that sense, the instrument is indirectly describing its own view of the world. The boundary is not perfectly circular, clean or theoretical. Rather, it is irregular, contingent and produced through use and that makes it much more interesting.

## The value of leaving it running

When I first added the position-density view, I was fascinated by the growing caterpillar-like tracks.

Had I stopped the session after half an hour, that would have seemed like the feature's main value.

Leaving it running revealed something else: The visualisation moved beyond individual aircraft and began to expose the larger conditions under which those aircraft were being observed.

That is a useful reminder that accumulated data can change not only the amount we know, but the scale at which we are able to see:

- A single position tells me where one aircraft was
- A short chain shows its path
- Thousands of positions begin to show the shape of the receiver's world
