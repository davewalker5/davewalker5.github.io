I recently wrote about the unexpected shape emerging from the live position-density plot in my ADS-B Tracker.

What began as a collection of short, caterpillar-like aircraft tracks gradually became a network of routes and intersections. As more positions accumulated, those routes began to merge into an oval-shaped operational footprint: not simply the theoretical range of the receiver, but the region produced by the receiver, antenna, tracking profile, local conditions and the aircraft that happened to pass through them.

There was, however, something the final plot could not show.

It showed what the observation had become, but not **how it became that way**.

## Preserving the intermediate states

The position-density plot is cumulative.

Each new aircraft position contributes to a spatial bin, increasing the density wherever observations recur. While a session is active, the display therefore changes continuously. Individual tracks appear, lengthen, intersect and gradually become part of a larger pattern.

Previously, only the current state existed. Once the session ended, its aircraft positions remained in the database, but the sequence through which the density plot had developed was not retained as a sequence in its own right.

Version 2.29.0 of the ADS-B BaseStation Reader changes that.

The application can now optionally persist position-density snapshots to the database at a configurable interval. Each snapshot belongs to the active observation session and records the cumulative density state at that particular moment.

A companion Jupyter notebook can then load the stored sequence and reconstruct it as an animation.

The result is the short replay above.

## From final picture to developing process

A static density plot answers a spatial question:

> Where did observations accumulate during this session?

The animation adds a temporal question:

> How did that pattern form?

That distinction turns out to matter.

The completed plot compresses the entire observation into one image. It cannot show whether several routes became active at once, whether one corridor appeared much earlier than another, or whether the final footprint accumulated steadily or through distinct phases of activity.

The replay restores some of that lost process.

Early frames remain sparse. A few tracks enter the display and begin extending across it. More paths appear, often following similar lines. Intersections grow brighter as repeated observations occupy the same bins. Eventually, the individual movements begin to merge into the broader shape visible at the end of the session.

The visualisation moves through the same scales I noticed in the live plot:

> aircraft → tracks → routes → corridors → observation envelope

This time, however, those changes can be watched again after the observation has finished.

## A session as a field record

The animation also reinforces the importance of the application's observation-session model.

A session is not merely a convenient database identifier. It defines one deliberate period of observation, carried out using a particular receiver configuration and tracking profile.

The replay is therefore not an animation assembled from an arbitrary collection of historical positions. It belongs to one bounded observing period. Its frames describe the gradual development of one session's view of the surrounding airspace.

That makes the stored snapshots feel rather like successive entries in a field record.

Each one is incomplete in isolation. Together, and in order, they preserve the development of the observation.

## Derived observations

A density snapshot is not itself an ADS-B transmission.

It is a derived observation: a summary calculated from the collective state of many received positions. It records something that no individual aircraft message contains—the spatial pattern produced by the session up to that point.

A sequence of those snapshots forms a small derived dataset of its own.

That opens up questions which were not available from the final image alone:

- How quickly does a recognisable traffic pattern emerge?
- Which corridors appear first?
- Are some parts of the footprint active only briefly?
- Does the same tracking profile produce a similar progression on another day?
- How much of the final observation envelope is stable, and how much depends upon the traffic of a particular session?

I do not yet know which of those questions will prove interesting. That is precisely why preserving the snapshots seems worthwhile.

The records make it possible to return to the observation later, when the useful questions may be clearer than they were while it was taking place.

## Keeping reporting outside the application

The animation is produced in a Jupyter notebook rather than inside the live tracking interface.

That separation is deliberate.

The application is responsible for the operational work: receiving the messages, maintaining the density state and storing snapshots reliably while the session is running.

The notebook is responsible for the exploratory work: selecting a historical session, reconstructing its snapshots, controlling the animation and exporting the result as a GIF or video.

This keeps the live interface focused on observation while allowing reporting to remain adaptable. The stored snapshots are the durable feature; the animation is one way of interpreting them.

Other forms of analysis can be added later without turning the application itself into a general reporting environment.

## Watching the instrument learn its view

What I enjoy most about the replay is that it shows both the observed aircraft and the observing system.

The tracks are produced by real aircraft movements, but the shape that ultimately emerges also reflects:

- Receiver and antenna performance
- Aircraft altitude
- Local obstructions
- Available traffic routes
- Tracking-profile rules
- Session duration
- The interval at which snapshots were recorded

The finished pattern therefore belongs to neither the aircraft nor the receiver alone. It is the result of their interaction during a particular period of observation.

In the earlier post, I described the density plot as an instrument indirectly observing its own view of the world.

The animation adds another layer to that idea.

It does not merely show the instrument's view. It shows that view being constructed.

## The value of retaining change

There is a broader lesson here that applies beyond aircraft tracking.

A final state is often easier to store and present than the sequence that produced it. Yet the sequence may contain much of what makes the result understandable.

The completed position-density plot is striking because of its shape. The replay is interesting because it reveals that the shape was not simply there waiting to be displayed. It emerged gradually from individual observations, each contributing a little more structure.

- One position records a moment
- A track records movement
- A completed density plot records accumulated space
- A replay records the observation becoming itself
