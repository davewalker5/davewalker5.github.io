It began, as these things sometimes do, with a holiday.

Our trip to the Maldives provided some extraordinary encounters with marine wildlife, including Moorish Idols, Titan Triggerfish and Reef Manta Rays. It also provided rather a lot of video: Approximately 50 Gb of GoPro footage, representing a wonderful collection of memories but also something of a challenge when it came to finding, extracting and examining the interesting bits.

Initially, the solution seemed straightforward enough. A few scripts using the Open Source video media manipulation suite `FFmpeg` would allow me to clarify and enhance the footage and extract frames and clips from the recordings. But as the scripts became more elaborate, the process became increasingly cumbersome. What was needed, I decided, was a simple application to make the whole business easier.

And so _ReefFrame_ was born, a desktop application built around `FFmpeg` that provides a visual interface for clarifying and enhancing footage, extracting individual frames, and trimming and exporting clips. What began as a handful of command-line scripts had evolved into a practical tool for examining and working with my underwater footage, without having to wrestle with `FFmpeg` commands each time.

That might have been the end of the story, except that one evening another thought occurred to me.

> *What if I could get the computer to find the fish for itself? And, having found one, could it follow that individual as it moved through the video?*

It was one of those wonderfully dangerous questions that begins with _"I wonder if..."_ and ends with an entirely new software project.

This one became _FishTrack_.

What I hadn't anticipated was that, in experimenting with different approaches to tracking fish, I would discover something rather interesting about the relationship between the way animals move and the way computers see them.

## From Video Collection to Digital Fieldwork

_ReefFrame_ and _FishTrack_ were never part of the holiday plan. Indeed, neither application was originally planned at all.

_ReefFrame_ developed out of a practical need - making it easier to work with the footage I had brought home. Rather than repeatedly assembling command-line instructions, I wanted a convenient way to inspect recordings, identify interesting sequences, extract still images, and prepare clips for further examination. It was, in effect, a digital workbench for my underwater recordings.

_FishTrack_ represented a different kind of question. Rather than simply helping me inspect the footage, could an application examine the contents of the images and identify the animals within them? The distinction is important.

_ReefFrame_ helps me decide which parts of a recording I want to examine. _FishTrack_ attempts to identify what is happening within those recordings. The first is primarily a tool for managing and extracting observations. The second is an experiment in automated observation.

And that, for someone interested in natural history as well as computing, opens up all sorts of possibilities.

## Teaching a Computer to Follow a Fish

At first glance, tracking a fish through a video might seem a relatively straightforward task. Find the fish in one frame, determine where it moves in the next, and repeat. Unfortunately, fish are not particularly cooperative subjects.

They change direction, accelerate, disappear behind other objects, turn towards or away from the camera, and sometimes alter their apparent shape almost beyond recognition. Meanwhile, the camera itself may be moving, the light may be changing, and the background may be a complicated mixture of coral, rock, shadow and other marine life. What appears obvious to a human observer is not necessarily obvious to a computer.

_FishTrack_ explores two rather different approaches to the problem.

The first uses **conventional computer vision (CV)**. It examines changes between successive frames, compensates for camera movement, identifies regions that appear to be moving independently of the background, and attempts to associate those regions with individual tracks.

The second uses **SAM 2 (Segment Anything Model 2)**, an artificial intelligence model capable of identifying and following the visible extent of objects through video sequences.

Both approaches aim to maintain the identity of a subject as it moves, but they use different kinds of information to do so.

_FishTrack_ also incorporates **Grounding DINO**, a text-guided object detector that can help identify candidate subjects. In effect, it can be asked to look for something described in words, such as a fish, and propose regions of an image that might contain it.

Grounding DINO is used for finding candidate objects, while CV and SAM 2 provide alternative tracking mechanisms. The intriguing question was not simply whether these methods worked. It was *how they would behave when confronted with real animals moving naturally in their environment*. And for that, I already had some excellent test subjects.

The Moorish Idol (_Zanclus cornutus_) is one of the most distinctive fish found on tropical coral reefs. Its strongly contrasting black, white and yellow markings, laterally compressed body and extraordinarily elongated dorsal fin make it immediately recognisable. It is an elegant animal, and one that presents a particularly interesting visual target.

For a human observer, recognising a Moorish Idol is generally straightforward. Its overall appearance is so distinctive that even a brief glimpse may be sufficient. But this does not automatically make it an easy subject for a computer. A visual tracking system must distinguish the fish from the surrounding reef, accommodate changes in its apparent outline as it moves, and avoid confusing changes in the camera's viewpoint with movement by the animal itself.

The Moorish Idol footage offered an opportunity to explore the basic process of finding a subject in a complex natural scene and following its movement across successive frames. It was also a reminder that detection and tracking are two different problems.

**Recognising an animal in an individual image is not the same thing as maintaining its identity through time.**

An algorithm may be quite successful at the former while encountering difficulties with the latter. This becomes particularly apparent when the animal changes its orientation.

The Titan Triggerfish (*Balistoides viridescens*) provided a particularly revealing example. In the footage, the fish rolls and turns, presenting the camera with a dramatically changing view of its body. To a human observer, there is no great mystery. We recognise the movement as a continuous action performed by the same animal. Its appearance changes, but its identity does not.

To a computer vision algorithm, however, that transformation can be rather more troublesome. The conventional CV implementation initially follows the fish, but when it rolls and presents a substantially different outline, the tracker loses it. SAM 2, by contrast, continues to follow the fish through the movement.

This was one of the moments when the project became especially interesting. The conventional method is attempting to associate patterns of movement and detected image regions across successive frames. A sudden change in appearance can disrupt those associations. SAM 2 draws upon a richer representation of the subject and its visual context, allowing it, in this example, to maintain continuity despite the changing shape.

In other words, both systems are looking at the same fish, but they are not interpreting the visual evidence in the same way. And this illustrates something important about automated observation: A change in the apparent properties of an object does not necessarily indicate a change in the object itself. For a human observer, that distinction feels almost trivial. For a machine, establishing it reliably can be surprisingly difficult.

Of all the subjects in the Maldives footage, the Reef Manta Rays (*Mobula alfredi*) were perhaps the most spectacular. Watching one of these beautiful animals move through the water is an extraordinary experience. Their broad pectoral fins rise and fall in sweeping movements, giving the impression of effortless flight.

But it is precisely this movement that makes them interesting from a computer vision perspective. A manta's silhouette changes continuously during each wingbeat. With its fins extended, the animal presents a broad, almost diamond-shaped outline. As the fins rise, that outline narrows and changes substantially. As they descend, it expands again. The animal is, of course, the same individual throughout. But from the perspective of a tracker that relies heavily on the geometry and continuity of moving regions, the changes can be considerable.

I tried _FishTrack_ on two Manta clips. In both cases, SAM 2 successfully maintained the track throughout the sequence. The conventional CV method initially performed reasonably well, but as the wingbeats developed and the apparent shape changed, it eventually lost the subject.

What makes this example so attractive is that the tracking difficulty is not an artefact of poor footage or some unusual technical problem. It arises directly from the animal's natural movement - the Manta is doing exactly what a Manta should do, its swimming motion simply presents the tracking algorithms with different levels of difficulty.

And in that sense, the animal's biology is an essential part of the experiment.

## When Failure Becomes a Useful Result

It would be tempting to regard these experiments as a competition between conventional computer vision and artificial intelligence, with SAM 2 emerging as the winner but I don't think that would be a particularly useful way to interpret them.

The two methods have different assumptions, different computational requirements, and different strengths. There are circumstances in which conventional computer vision is entirely adequate, and it has the attraction of being relatively lightweight and understandable in terms of the image-processing operations being performed. SAM 2 is more sophisticated and computationally demanding, but that sophistication enables it to cope with some changes in subject appearance that prove difficult for the conventional approach.

The important point is that the differences in their behaviour are interesting in themselves.

When the conventional tracker loses a rolling Triggerfish or a wingbeating Manta, that tells us something about the limitations of the particular tracking strategy being used. When SAM 2 maintains the track, that demonstrates the value of a different approach to representing and following the subject. It doesn't follow that one method must be developed until it matches the other.

Indeed, I have no particular intention of attempting to make the conventional CV implementation perform as well as SAM 2 in every situation as that wasn't the purpose of the experiment. The purpose was to explore different ways of following fish through real underwater footage, and to see what happened and in that respect, both approaches have been useful.

A failed track can be just as informative as a successful one, provided we understand what the failure tells us — and avoid drawing conclusions that the evidence doesn't support.

## Observation, Interpretation and the Limitations of our Instruments

There is a broader point here that appeals to the natural historian in me. Whenever we observe the natural world, we do so through some combination of our senses, our knowledge, and the instruments available to us. A pair of binoculars, a microscope, a camera or a computer vision model extends our ability to observe, but each also introduces its own limitations. We can only interpret what the instrument makes available, and the instrument's representation of the world is not necessarily identical to the world itself.

The _FishTrack_ experiments provide a small but rather tangible demonstration of this principle. The conventional CV algorithm does not suddenly encounter a different animal when the Titan Triggerfish rolls. Nor does the Manta cease to be the same individual when its wings rise. What changes is the information available to the tracking method, and the method's ability to maintain a consistent interpretation of that information.

This is an important distinction as it highlights that the failure of an observation method is not necessarily a change in the thing being observed.

It is also a useful reminder that an artificial intelligence system, however impressive its results, is not interpreting the natural world in precisely the same way that we do. Its apparent success needs to be understood in terms of the task it has actually performed.

SAM 2 has demonstrated that it can maintain tracks through movements that defeat my conventional CV implementation. That is an interesting and useful finding. It is not evidence that the system understands the biology or behaviour of the animals it is following.

That remains the job of the observer.

## The Unexpected Rewards of Curiosity

Looking back over the development of _ReefFrame_ and _FishTrack_, one of the most satisfying things is that neither project was planned. The starting point was simply a holiday, some wonderful wildlife encounters, and a collection of video recordings that I wanted to explore. One practical problem led to _ReefFrame_. Then an idle thought — *I wonder whether a computer could follow the fish?* — led to _FishTrack_.

That, in turn, led to experiments with different detection and tracking methods, to comparisons between conventional computer vision and machine learning, and finally to some fascinating examples of how natural animal movement can challenge an automated observer.

At no point was there a grand plan to carry out an investigation into computer vision: The investigation emerged from the questions.

This is, I think, one of the great pleasures of maintaining Field Notes. Observations do not necessarily remain isolated records of things seen or experienced. Sometimes they suggest new questions, and those questions lead into subjects that were never part of the original intention.

In this case, an interest in marine wildlife became an opportunity to explore something of the relationship between biology, visual perception and computation and the technology has, in turn, encouraged me to look more closely at the original wildlife footage. The whole process has been rather circular, in the nicest possible way.

The original purpose of the GoPro recordings was to preserve memories of some remarkable encounters with marine life. They have certainly done that but they have also become the raw material for two software applications, a series of experiments in computer vision, and a thoroughly enjoyable exploration of how computers attempt to make sense of the natural world.

Not a bad return from a holiday.

And now, of course, I'm wondering what else might be hiding in those 50 GB of footage...
