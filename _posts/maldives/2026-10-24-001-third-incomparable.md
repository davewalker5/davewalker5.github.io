We're approaching the end of our third visit to Constance Moofushi, a small island resort in the South Ari Atoll of the Maldives. Soon we'll board a seaplane for the journey back to Malé, followed by flights through Istanbul to London. And I'm feeling rather sad about leaving.

There's nothing particularly unusual about that. We've been fortunate enough to enjoy some wonderful holidays over the years, and reaching the end of an especially enjoyable one often brings a certain melancholy. But Moofushi is different. The sadness I feel about leaving here is of an entirely different order.

I've been reflecting on why.

## Three incomparables

Of all the holidays we've enjoyed together over the past twenty years or so, there have always been two that we've regarded as incomparable with anything else: our honeymoon and our Tanzanian safari. They occupy a category of their own, not simply because they were wonderful holidays, but because their significance goes beyond the ordinary pleasures of travel.

Moofushi has now become the third.

That's not a judgement made in the excitement of discovering somewhere new. We first visited in 2015, again in 2018, and returning after all these years has reinforced, rather than diminished, our affection for the island. Indeed, I think this third visit has helped me understand something I hadn't fully appreciated before.

It's not simply that Moofushi is beautiful, although it certainly is. The white sand, turquoise lagoons, palm trees and seemingly endless Indian Ocean provide scenery of almost improbable beauty. But we've visited plenty of beautiful places. Some have been spectacularly so, without leaving anything like the same emotional impression.

There must be something else.

## The difference is meaning

For roughly fifty years, wildlife and conservation have been among my deepest interests and most enduring values. They aren't simply hobbies or pleasant diversions. They're part of how I understand and engage with the world. And on this tiny island in the Indian Ocean, an extraordinary number of those interests and values come together.

Over the past fortnight, I've swum alongside hawksbill turtles, watching them graze naturally on the reef, seemingly unconcerned by my presence. I've encountered reef manta rays feeding in plankton-rich water, watched sharks and rays moving through their environment, and marvelled at the extraordinary diversity of fish living around the coral. On land, there have been birds, lizards, geckos, fruit bats and insects to observe, identify and learn about.

What matters to me isn't simply seeing these creatures. It's the opportunity to observe them going about their lives, in their own environments, on their own terms. A hawksbill turtle feeding on the reef isn't performing for the benefit of tourists. It's simply being a turtle. For a few extraordinary minutes, I have the privilege of sharing its world.

Conservation adds another dimension.

The animals and ecosystems I've been observing aren't just fascinating. Their continued existence matters deeply to me. Learning about the efforts to protect the marine environment, seeing the coral restoration work around the island, and supporting manta ray conservation have connected the pleasure of observation with values I've held for most of my life.

And then there's the Indian Ocean itself, for which I've had a particular affection for many years. Its colour, warmth, wildlife and vastness have always fascinated me. Here, all these things are present together.

Moofushi doesn't merely provide a collection of things I enjoy. It offers an environment in which an unusual number of the things I value most find expression. It's somewhere that speaks to some very deep parts of who I am.

## A photograph that explains something

While preparing a private journal entry about these reflections, I needed a photograph to accompany it and with 70 Gb of photographs and GoPro footage to choose from I had no shortage of candidates! I've taken photographs of spectacular sunsets, astonishingly beautiful lagoons, palm-fringed beaches and the surrounding Indian Ocean. Any number of them would have illustrated the beauty of Moofushi perfectly.

But the photograph that immediately caught my attention was something rather less spectacular. A wooden sign on a beach, marking a sea turtle nesting site.

The sign records that a turtle laid her eggs there on 7th October 2026, just before we arrived. It was joined by a second nest during our stay. It's an unremarkable scene in purely visual terms: a modest wooden marker standing in the sand. There's no turtle in the photograph, no dramatic wildlife encounter, no particularly striking scenery. Yet of all the photographs available, that was the one I wanted to use.

Reflecting on the choice afterwards, I realised how neatly it illustrated what I'd been trying to understand. That little sign represents the presence of wild creatures living their lives independently of the resort and its visitors. It speaks of a continuing life cycle, of the relationship between the island and the wider ecosystem, and of a human decision to recognise and protect something vulnerable.

It represents wildlife, ecology, conservation and coexistence.

In other words, it represents some of the things that make Moofushi meaningful to me.

There's a particularly lovely contrast here. For us, Moofushi is somewhere extraordinary that we visit for a short time before reluctantly leaving. For the turtle, this beach is part of the natural world in which the next generation may begin its life.

The island is not merely a beautiful destination created for our enjoyment. It's part of a living world, with its own rhythms and purposes, of which we're temporary visitors.

I think that photograph captures something that a spectacular sunset, however beautiful, cannot.

- The sunset shows what Moofushi looks like
- The nesting-site photograph says something about what Moofushi means to me

And perhaps the most interesting thing is that I chose it instinctively, before consciously recognising why it mattered.

## Curiosity as everyday life

There's another aspect of this experience that I've been reflecting on. Throughout our stay, I've explored, snorkelled, photographed, filmed, researched, identified species, kept notes and asked questions. Even things that might otherwise have passed without much attention have become subjects of fascination.

The changing shape of the island's sandbanks. The weather systems bringing spectacular tropical rainstorms. The reef's dramatic descent into deep water. The behaviour of fish, birds and reptiles. The efforts to maintain and protect the surrounding marine environment. Almost everything has offered an opportunity to observe, investigate and learn something.

I think there's something particularly significant about that. At home, curiosity, wildlife observation, photography and writing are interests that I pursue alongside the ordinary responsibilities of daily life. Here, for a couple of weeks, they've largely *been* daily life as the environment has made it possible to engage with those interests almost continuously, without having to seek out opportunities or fit them around other things.

That has been extraordinarily fulfilling.

It also helps explain why leaving feels different from the end of an ordinary holiday. I'm not simply saying goodbye to beautiful scenery, warm weather and a welcome break from routine. I'm leaving an environment that seems unusually well suited to the person I am.

## Impermanence and appreciation

There's a Buddhist dimension to all this that I've found helpful to contemplate.

*Anicca* describes the impermanent nature of things. *Dukkha*, often translated as suffering or dissatisfaction, arises in part from our attachment to things and our desire for reality to be other than it is.

I understand perfectly well that holidays end. Indeed, their finite nature is part of what makes them special. I also recognise how fortunate we are to have enjoyed these experiences, and particularly to have had the opportunity to return to somewhere we loved so much. None of that entirely removes the sadness, but perhaps it doesn't need to.

Gratitude and sadness aren't mutually exclusive. It's possible to be profoundly grateful for an experience while simultaneously regretting that it's coming to an end and the strength of that regret may itself tell us something about the significance of what we've experienced.

The impermanence that makes leaving Moofushi painful is also part of what makes the time we've spent here so precious.

There's no need to resolve that contradiction and perhaps there isn't really a contradiction at all.

## What we take home

Of course, the experience doesn't simply disappear when we leave. We'll return with an enormous collection of photographs and videos, a wildlife diary, many pages of observations, and memories of some extraordinary encounters. There will be plenty to write about in Field Notes over the coming weeks.

But more importantly, the interests and values that made Moofushi so meaningful aren't things we're leaving behind.

My curiosity about the natural world, my concern for conservation and my affection for the Indian Ocean were part of my life long before this holiday. They'll remain part of it long afterwards.

I've always known that I loved this place. What I hadn't fully understood was why. Now I think I do.

And in doing so, it has earned a place alongside our honeymoon and Tanzanian safari as one of our three incomparable travel experiences.

> **The third incomparable**

I'll be sad when we leave.

But I'm enormously grateful that we've returned, and that this third visit has helped me understand why this little island in the Indian Ocean means quite so much to me.
