It would appear that the octopuses haven't finished with me yet!

Having recently completed my [Other Minds](/wildlife/other-minds/) reading project and the [Chromatophore Simulator](/wildlife/chromatophores/), I found myself returning to another aspect of octopus biology that had caught my imagination.

Not their remarkable ability to change colour this time, but their arms. Eight extraordinarily flexible appendages, capable of reaching, exploring, manipulating objects and apparently managing a considerable amount of their own activity. And behind those arms, a nervous system organised rather differently from our own.

Once again, a question emerged and, once again, the obvious response seemed to be to write some software.

## Who's Actually in Control?

One of the things I found particularly fascinating while reading Peter Godfrey-Smith's *Other Minds* was the distribution of neurons throughout an octopus's body.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.octopus_nervous_system %}

A substantial proportion of an octopus's neurons are located outside its central brain, particularly within its arms. This raises an intriguing problem.

An octopus arm is not a conventional limb with a small number of rigid segments and clearly defined joints. It is an extremely flexible muscular structure capable of bending, twisting, shortening and changing shape along its length. 

Controlling such a structure centrally, by specifying the position of every part at every moment, would be a formidable task. But what if the central brain doesn't need to do all of that? What if it can specify a broad objective while much of the detailed movement is managed locally?

The question I wanted to investigate was:

> **How much central control is necessary when the individual parts of a system can make useful adjustments themselves?**

That seemed like a rather interesting computational experiment.

## Building an Arm

The first step was to build something that could move.

My simulated arm is deliberately much simpler than a biological octopus arm. It consists of a chain of connected, fixed-length segments, each able to bend relative to its neighbour.

There are no muscles, no water resistance and no attempt to reproduce the physical properties of living tissue. Instead, the arm is controlled geometrically.

The central controller supplies a destination. The final segment receives that destination, and neighbouring segments exchange requests about where their connections should be positioned. Each joint makes small adjustments, subject to limits on bending and turning speed. This produces a distinction I particularly like:

> **The central controller specifies what the arm should achieve. The local controller determines how the arm moves towards it.**

The resulting movement isn't specified as a complete sequence of predetermined poses. It develops through repeated local adjustments. And once a single arm could do something useful, the next step was rather obvious.

Each has its own target, segment geometry, movement requests and activity state. One arm can reach towards food while another negotiates an obstacle. A third might already be holding something, while others remain idle. They share the same environment, but their individual movements are not choreographed joint by joint by the central controller.

This was the first point at which the simulation began to resemble the question I had originally wanted to investigate. Not because it looked exactly like an octopus — it certainly doesn't — but because it provided a visible distinction between central intention and local execution.

It also suggested further questions:

- What happens when an arm encounters an obstacle?
- How does it know when it has touched something?
- And can contact lead to something more useful than simply stopping?

## Sensing, Avoiding and Grasping

The subsequent development introduced three important capabilities.

- **Local contact sensing** allows individual segments to register contact with objects in the environment. This is deliberately simple: the model detects physical contact rather than sensing food at a distance
- **Obstacle avoidance** allows segments to modify their requested movements when something obstructs their route. There is no centrally calculated path for the entire arm. Local steering attempts to negotiate the obstacle, while a separate geometric check prevents unsafe movements
- **Grasping** introduces an interaction with an object rather than simply reaching a destination

When two neighbouring segments remain in contact with the same food object for a sufficient period, the arm can establish a grip. It can then hold, carry, retract or release the object.

The distinction between reaching and grasping is important:

- Reaching concerns the position of the arm
- Grasping concerns its interaction with the environment

And, as with the Chromatophore Simulator, I found that adding a relatively small number of rules produced a much richer collection of observable behaviours. Not intelligence and certainly not a reproduction of biological octopus cognition but useful, increasingly complex behaviour arising from interacting control mechanisms.

## The Behaviour Showcase

Having developed the individual control mechanisms, I wanted to see how they behaved together in a single experiment. This led to the introduction of **scenarios** — JSON files that define the initial conditions of a simulation, including food, obstacles, arm targets and movement parameters.

Importantly, a scenario doesn't prescribe a sequence of movements. It establishes the starting conditions and leaves the existing controllers to determine what happens.

The first substantial example was the *Behaviour Showcase*. Four arms receive different objectives. Two make relatively straightforward reaches, another negotiates an obstacle, and a fourth moves more slowly towards food, eventually making contact and establishing a grip. The remaining arms stay idle, making the individual activities easier to follow.

What I particularly like is that this isn't a scripted animation. The arms respond to their objectives and environment through their normal control mechanisms. Their movements overlap, and the final grasp results from sustained local contact rather than an instruction to perform a grasp at a predetermined moment. And because the initial conditions are stored in a scenario file, I can change the targets, obstacles or movement parameters and repeat the experiment.

**The same controllers, operating under different conditions, can produce different outcomes.**

That seems to me a much more interesting demonstration than simply showing several animated arms following predetermined paths.

## When Movement Becomes Impossible

Another aspect of the development that I found interesting was learning to distinguish between an arm wanting to move and actually being able to move.

An arm can be given a perfectly reasonable destination that is nevertheless impossible to reach. Perhaps the target lies beyond its total length. Perhaps the joints cannot bend sufficiently. Perhaps obstacles prevent a safe approach.

There's also a more subtle geometric problem. A movement can appear safe when only its starting and ending positions are examined, yet still pass through an obstacle along the way. The simulation therefore checks the movement between poses, rather than simply looking at the final arrangement.

This reinforces a distinction that also emerged from the Chromatophore Simulator:

> **What a control system requests is not necessarily what the system can achieve.**

In the chromatophore model, response speeds and delays affect whether pigment cells can follow changing instructions. Here, bending limits, movement speeds and obstacles constrain whether an arm can fulfil its objective.

Different simulations, but a related underlying idea.

## This Isn't an Octopus

A necessary qualification. Octopus Controller is a computational model inspired by selected aspects of octopus arm control. It is not a simulation of actual octopus neurophysiology:

- The arms are articulated chains rather than muscular hydrostats
- Sensing is simplified to geometric contact detection
- Grasping uses a rigid attachment rather than modelling suction, pressure or friction
- The central controller does not autonomously decide which food objects are worth investigating
- The arms do not possess the full sensory or behavioural capabilities of living octopuses

The model also has limitations. Arms can cross one another, the body remains fixed, and local obstacle avoidance does not guarantee that every reachable destination can actually be reached.

These simplifications are deliberate. They make it possible to isolate and investigate a particular question without pretending to reproduce the much richer biological system. This is another example of what I have increasingly come to think of as **computational natural history**. Use a simplified model to investigate a biological idea, observe what happens, and remain clear about what the model can and cannot tell us.

## The Interactive Explorer

The point of Octopus Controller isn't merely to watch an arm reach towards a target. It's to investigate what happens when the conditions change:

- Move the target
- Alter a joint's maximum bend
- Introduce an obstacle
- Change the number of segments
- Give several arms different objectives
- See what happens

A video can illustrate a particular behaviour, but it cannot provide that freedom. This is where the interactive explorer comes in. It allows anyone to interact with the model directly, without installing Python or downloading the source code and to create and upload their own scenarios.

## And Then There's Charles

I can't really describe this project without mentioning Charles. Charles was one of three common octopuses — Albert, Bertram and Charles — studied by P. B. Dews in a series of operant-conditioning experiments reported in 1959.

The task was relatively straightforward: learn to operate a lever to obtain food.

Albert and Bertram proved reasonably cooperative. Charles was another matter. He operated the lever, but did so with sufficient force to bend it repeatedly and eventually break it. He also developed an interest in the experimental lamp, attempting to pull it into his tank. And, perhaps most memorably, he directed jets of water towards the experimenter.

I find Charles's story both enormously entertaining and scientifically interesting.

He clearly demonstrated the ability to interact with the experimental apparatus but his behaviour extended well beyond the particular task the researchers wanted him to perform. Of course, we cannot know whether Charles was being deliberately mischievous. Describing him as uncooperative is an affectionate human interpretation, not a conclusion about his intentions.

Nevertheless, the contrast between Charles and his more compliant companions is a wonderful reminder that an animal's capabilities are not necessarily revealed entirely by its performance in a narrowly defined experiment. And that makes him a rather appropriate inspiration for a project exploring flexible, locally controlled interaction with the environment.

Octopus Controller is therefore dedicated to Charles.

I suspect he would have been entirely unimpressed by the carefully defined targets and movement constraints. He'd probably have wanted to know how to break the apparatus. And, in the spirit of Charles, perhaps the next question shouldn't be:

> What would I like the controller to do?

Perhaps it should be:

> **What happens when the controller starts doing something I didn't expect?**

I suspect Charles would approve. 🐙
