One of the unexpected pleasures of the computational shell morphology project is that it has quietly changed the way I look at fossils.

A few months ago I would have admired an ammonite simply because it was beautiful. Now, when I look at one, I instinctively start thinking about how it grew.

I notice the logarithmic spiral. I imagine the expanding aperture tracing out each successive chamber. I wonder about the changing shell radius, the spacing of the septa and the path of the siphuncle. Instead of seeing a finished object, I find myself trying to reconstruct the biological process that created it.

That change in perspective led to a small purchase.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.ammonite %}

This split and polished Madagascan ammonite is the first fossil in what I expect will remain a modest personal collection. It wasn’t bought simply because it was attractive — although it certainly is — but because spending time modelling shell growth taught me to appreciate the extraordinary mathematics embodied in its form.

Much of the shell morphology work on Field Notes has involved building mathematical and computational models of shell growth. The aim has never been to reproduce a particular fossil exactly, but to understand how relatively simple rules can generate such a remarkable diversity of forms.

Holding a real specimen after spending so much time working with those models is surprisingly satisfying. The mathematics suddenly has weight. The geometry has texture. Features that previously existed only as arrays of coordinates and triangular meshes become tangible.

There is also something rather grounding about owning the real thing. Computational models are abstractions: useful, informative and often beautiful in their own right, but still abstractions. A fossil reminds us that every model ultimately refers back to an organism that lived, grew and died hundreds of millions of years ago.

This ammonite is therefore more than the first entry in a fossil collection. It marks a point where the computational and physical sides of Field Notes meet.

I suspect it won’t be the last.

An _Orthoceras_ will be joining it shortly, and I’m hoping that a genuine stromatolite specimen will eventually follow. Together they’ll become not just objects to display, but companions to the modelling work: reminders that every equation, simulation and visualisation begins with something that once existed in the natural world.

Looking back, it’s amusing that the modelling came before the fossil. Usually we build models because we already have an object we want to understand. In this case the process ran in reverse: understanding the mathematics first made me want to hold the object that inspired it.
