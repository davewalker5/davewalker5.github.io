One of the recurring themes of Field Notes has become the relationship between observing something in the natural world and trying to understand it by building a model. Sometimes the model comes first. Sometimes the observation does and sometimes, rather satisfyingly, the whole process goes round in a circle.

Earlier this year I visited the Oxford University Museum of Natural History with two broad questions in mind. The first was how life made the transition from relatively simple microbial forms to the recognisable animals of the Cambrian. The second was how the straight shells of early cephalopods eventually gave rise to the familiar coiled forms of ammonites.

Both questions subsequently escaped from the museum and turned into modelling projects.

The cephalopods led me into logarithmic spirals and computational shell morphology: exploring how simple mathematical rules governing growth can generate recognisable shell forms.

The early-life question took me somewhere quite different.

Stromatolites are layered structures produced through the interaction of microbial communities, particularly cyanobacteria, with their environment. As microbial mats grow, trap sediment and continue growing through successive layers, surprisingly complex structures can emerge from relatively simple repeated processes. That seemed almost irresistible from a modelling point of view.

As with the shell work, the aim wasn't to reproduce a particular specimen. It was to ask a simpler question:

> Can a small set of rules reproduce something recognisably stromatolite-like?

So I built a model.

A few months later I went back to the museum but this time I wasn't looking at the displays with quite the same eyes. Having spent time modelling shell growth, an ammonite is no longer simply a beautiful spiral fossil. I find myself looking for the growth process: the expanding aperture, the changing radius, the chambers and the geometry that produced the finished shell.

The same thing has now happened with stromatolites. Except this time the museum had something rather special waiting for me. Among its specimens is a stromatolite from Zimbabwe approximately **2.5 billion years old**.

And visitors are allowed to touch it.

That is quite a difficult experience to describe without resorting to geological superlatives.

The specimen itself is grey, irregular and, at first glance, perhaps rather unassuming. But once I laid my hands on it the surface became the interesting part: ridged, rippled and heavily textured, with curved structures and layers running through the rock.

After spending time representing stromatolite growth computationally, I suddenly had the real thing beneath my fingers.

The model, inevitably, is absurdly simple by comparison. But that's rather the point. A computational model isn't intended to recreate every fracture, mineral, deformation and accident preserved in a particular 2.5-billion-year-old rock. It strips most of reality away deliberately. What remains is an attempt to isolate a process and ask whether relatively simple interactions, repeated over time, can produce some aspect of the structure we observe.

Standing in the museum with my hand on the specimen in front of me made that distinction unusually tangible.

- The model explains something
- The rock contains everything

There was another pleasing connection with the earlier visit. My original exploration of cephalopod evolution began with coiled ammonites and logarithmic spirals before branching out to modelling straight-shelled orthocones. On this visit I found myself looking at belemnites and other fossil cephalopods and almost immediately wrote a question in my notebook:

**Can I model these?**

Apparently the process is recursive:

- Museum specimens generate questions
- Questions become diagrams and notes
- Some of those become mathematical or computational models
- The models then change what I notice when I return to the specimens

And those observations generate new questions.

In an earlier Field Notes post, after buying an ammonite following the shell-modelling work, I wrote that computational models are abstractions and that the real fossil reminds us that every model ultimately refers back to something that once existed in the natural world.

Being up-close and personal with a 2.5-billion-year-old stromatolite brought that thought back rather forcefully.

Only this time the mathematics didn't merely have weight.

Deep time did too.
