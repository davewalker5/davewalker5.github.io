It has been hot.

Not merely "quite warm for Britain" hot, accompanied by somebody optimistically uncovering the barbecue and everybody else complaining about sleeping with the windows open.

The spring and summer of 2026 were genuinely exceptional and after several months of walking around Abingdon recording wildlife, I had begun to develop an impression. Things seemed ... quieter. Not empty. Not devoid of wildlife. Just different.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.all %}

Some of the birds I would normally expect to notice regularly seemed less conspicuous. Swallows and Swifts appeared to be around less often.

The vegetation looked different too. As the summer progressed, stretches of hedgerow and verge which had been green and productive earlier in the year increasingly became brown, dry and depleted of flowers.

Meanwhile, Magpies appeared to be everywhere.

Which left me with an obvious question:

> **Did the exceptional heat leave a visible signature in my wildlife records?**

Fortunately, I had something with which to test the impression.

For a number of years I've been accumulating wildlife observations around Abingdon, including the records used for my **Wildlife Through the Year in Abingdon** project. So I took a subset of species for which I had reasonably useful records and compared sightings during 2025 and 2026. The first result was fairly obvious — for many birds, sightings really were down.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.all_birds %}

Wrens, Great Tits, Blue Tits, Dunnocks, Song Thrushes, House Sparrows and Robins were all recorded less frequently between May and September than during the corresponding period in 2025.

June stood out particularly strongly — across the selected bird species, observations were approximately 30% lower than during June 2025.

The wildflowers showed something interesting too.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.all_flora %}

April had actually produced more observations than the previous year and May was remarkably similar. Then came June. Wildflower observations fell from 308 in June 2025 to 180 in June 2026 — a reduction of roughly 42%.

That wasn't just a change in a spreadsheet - it reflected something that had become very visible on the ground: hedgerows and verges which had been productive earlier in the season were increasingly dry, brown and short of flowers.

But this is where things become more interesting than simply:

> "It was hot, therefore there was less wildlife."

Because there are some gloriously awkward exceptions:

- Blackbird sightings were only slightly lower
- Woodpigeons were essentially unchanged
- Common Poppies actually increased
- And Magpies apparently regarded the entire situation as an opportunity

Between May and September I recorded 95 Magpie encounters in 2026, compared with 69 during the same period in 2025, an increase of around 38%.

Even before looking at the figures, Magpies had been one of the species that felt particularly conspicuous this year and the numbers appear to agree.

The summer migrants were more complicated. Swallows were recorded around 25% less frequently overall and Swifts around 21% less frequently, but neither followed a simple month-by-month decline. Swift observations, for example, were actually higher during May and July than in 2025, while collapsing during June.

Ecology has an unfortunate habit of refusing to arrange itself into a tidy graph merely for my convenience.

There is also an important limitation to all of this:

> These are **sightings**, not population counts

For birds, that matters enormously. If a bird changes where it feeds, when it is active, how often it sings, or how willing it is to spend time in exposed places, then I may record it less often even if exactly the same number of birds remains in the area.

In ecological terms, part of what I'm measuring is **detectability**.

The flowers are slightly different. They cannot move elsewhere or become quieter and during the hottest and driest parts of the summer, much of the flowering vegetation visibly browned, died back or ceased flowering. So although my records still cannot tell me what happened to plant populations, the reduction in sightings was accompanied by a very real change in the above-ground vegetation itself.

That leaves me with two slightly different effects.

- For the birds, the heat may have changed how easily I encountered them
- For the flowers, the heat changed what was visibly growing and flowering in the landscape

So the strongest conclusion I can draw isn't:

> "The heatwaves reduced wildlife populations"

My observations simply cannot demonstrate that.

What they *can* show is that the wildlife landscape I encountered during this exceptionally hot year looked noticeably different.

- Many familiar birds became less conspicuous
- Some remained remarkably stable
- A few became more visible
- And the flowering vegetation itself contracted during the hottest and driest part of the summer

The strongest changes appeared during early summer, particularly June, at roughly the same time that unusually hot conditions became a persistent feature of the year.

In other words, perhaps the most interesting result is not simply that the landscape contained less wildlife. It is that:

> **The character of the observable landscape changed**

That's a much subtler result than the question I started with and, to me, a rather more interesting one.
