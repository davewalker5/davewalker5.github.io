There are some experiences that you look forward to with a certain amount of anticipation, knowing they'll be special. And then there are those that, when they finally happen, turn out to be quite unlike anything you'd imagined.

Snorkelling with nurse sharks in the Maldives was both!

We've been fortunate enough to enjoy some extraordinary encounters with marine life during our time here. Hawksbill turtles moving serenely over the reef, reef manta rays gliding effortlessly through the water, and countless species of fish inhabiting a world of colour and movement beneath the surface.

But this was something different.

## Into Their World

The first impression was one of sheer abundance.

Not a solitary shark emerging from the blue, but sharks everywhere. Large, substantial animals moving through the water, passing above and below one another, sometimes so close that it was difficult to know quite where to look.

Around them, fish darted and gathered in shimmering shoals, flashes of electric blue appearing against the deeper turquoise of the sea. Sunlight, refracted by the rippling surface, painted shifting patterns across the sharks' backs.

It was an extraordinary spectacle.

And amongst the sharks were rays, their broad, flattened bodies moving with a grace that seemed almost improbable. Where the sharks propelled themselves with the steady, powerful movement of their tails, the rays appeared to fly, their great wings undulating gently as they passed.

Two very different ways of moving through the same environment, both seemingly effortless.

## A Different Kind of Shark

Sharks occupy a curious place in our collective imagination. For many of us, their image has been shaped by films, television and the occasional sensational newspaper headline. They are presented as fearsome predators, creatures whose presence in the water should immediately inspire alarm. The reality, of course, is considerably more interesting.

These were tawny nurse sharks, large, generally placid bottom-dwelling sharks that inhabit the warm waters of the Indian and Pacific Oceans. Although capable of growing to several metres in length, they are not normally aggressive towards people unless disturbed or provoked.

Watching them at close quarters, what struck me wasn't aggression or menace, but their extraordinary physical presence. They are unmistakably powerful animals. Broad-headed, muscular, and beautifully adapted to their environment. Yet their movements were unhurried, almost languid at times, as they passed through the water.

Occasionally, one would turn towards us, its blunt head and small eyes coming into view before it swept past and disappeared into the gathering of sharks and fish.

There is something wonderfully humbling about being so close to an animal of that size, entirely at home in an environment in which you are merely a temporary visitor.

## The Beauty of the Unexpected

Photography underwater is a curious business. The light changes constantly. The water is full of suspended particles. Animals move unpredictably, often in several directions at once, and the composition you'd ideally like rarely has much to do with what actually appears in front of the camera. And, of course, you're floating in the sea yourself.

But sometimes those very limitations become part of the story.

Looking back through the photographs and videos, what pleases me most isn't necessarily the individual sharks, impressive though they are. It's the sense of immersion: the overlapping bodies, the sunlight scattered across their backs, the little blue fish threading their way between the larger animals, and the occasional ray appearing almost unexpectedly amongst them.

The pictures are crowded, sometimes chaotic, and all the better for it, because in the best possible way that's what the experience felt like. Not an orderly parade of marine life, but a living, moving world in which we were briefly privileged to participate.

While we were busy getting up close and personal with nurse sharks and rays beneath the surface, Blue Tribe were filming the whole extraordinary spectacle from the air. It gives a superb view of the entire gathering, the sharks weaving around one another and the rays gliding gracefully amongst them.

It's a lovely reminder of how much our perspective shapes an experience. From below, we were surrounded by individual animals, each passing close enough to admire. From above, the whole gathering becomes visible — a remarkable concentration of marine life in the vastness of the ocean.

## A Privilege, Not a Possession

Perhaps that's what will stay with me most.

We spend much of our lives observing the natural world from a comfortable distance. We watch birds in the garden, admire animals through the lens of a camera, or encounter marine life through the glass of an aquarium. All wonderful experiences in their own right. But being in the water changes the relationship.

You're no longer separated from the animals by glass, distance or the reassuring solidity of dry land. You're sharing their environment, subject to its movements, its currents and its uncertainties and you're very conscious that you don't belong there in quite the same way they do.

There is a responsibility that comes with that privilege. These are wild animals, not performers, and the opportunity to encounter them should never come at the expense of their welfare.

But when such an encounter is approached with respect, it can be profoundly rewarding.

I went into the water hoping to see sharks. I came out with something rather more valuable: a renewed sense of wonder at the extraordinary variety of life that exists beneath the ocean's surface, and at how little of it we ordinarily get to experience.

The Maldives has already given us some remarkable memories.

This one will take some beating.
