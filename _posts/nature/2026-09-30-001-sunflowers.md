We have a triffid in our garden!

Every year I grow sunflowers. It is one of those small seasonal rituals that has gradually become part of the rhythm of the year:

- Germinate the seeds indoors in a little window-ledge propagator
- Bring the seedlings on until they are large enough to cope with life outside
- Then plant them out and wait

I grow two varieties.

**Little Dorrit** is the smaller of the two — relatively compact, but wonderfully enthusiastic about producing flowers. **Sunburst**, meanwhile, is much closer to what most of us probably picture when somebody says *sunflower*: tall stems, large leaves and those enormous, gloriously yellow flower heads.

I love sunflowers. There is something almost ridiculously cheerful about them.

> They don't do subtle!

They arrive as great bursts of yellow and gold, standing above the rest of the garden as though somebody has turned the brightness control up slightly too far.

This year both varieties did well. The Little Dorrit plants flowered profusely through the summer before beginning to die back during August. The Sunburst plants also performed nicely, and I kept dead-heading them as the flowers faded to encourage them to keep producing more. Eventually, as summer moved towards autumn, they too began to reach the end of their season.

Except one.

There had actually been nothing particularly promising about this plant when it started life.

Of all the Sunburst seedlings, it was the runt of the litter: the smallest and weakest-looking of the bunch. If I had been asked back in spring which plant was least likely to amount to much, this would probably have been the obvious candidate.

Apparently it took that personally.

While its companions flowered, faded and gradually died back, the runt carried on growing.

And growing.

And growing.

It branched.

It produced more flower heads.

It climbed above the garden wall.

And by late September it had become this:

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.triffid %}
**Good grief.**

And, remarkably, this is not even the plant at its bushiest. It has already shed a couple of substantial branches, and it is now so tall that I have to use a set of steps simply to get high enough to prune and dead-head it and even then I have to stretch to reach it!

The garden chairs provide a useful sense of scale, although I am beginning to suspect they may also eventually provide somewhere for the sunflower to sit.

What makes it particularly entertaining is that it is now the end of September. The other plants have largely finished for the year. Autumn is arriving. Leaves are beginning to turn. The growing season is winding down.

And this thing apparently hasn't received the memo.

There is something rather pleasing about that.

Growing plants inevitably creates expectations. You sow several seeds of the same variety, treat them broadly alike, plant them into the same garden and naturally expect roughly similar results.

Then one individual simply ignores the plan.

The weakest seedling becomes the strongest plant.

The supposed runt outlasts everything around it.

And the familiar annual ritual produces something completely unexpected.

Gardening, like natural history more generally, seems to contain an endless supply of these little reminders that living things are not machines. Patterns are useful. Expectations are reasonable.

But individuals remain individuals.

And occasionally one of them decides that what your garden really needs is a triffid.
