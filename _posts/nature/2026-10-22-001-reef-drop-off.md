There's a point on the reef at Moofushi where the world changes quite suddenly.

For much of the swim, the seabed is reassuringly close. Coral formations rise towards the surface, fish go about their business, and there's always something to catch the eye. It's a busy, colourful, wonderfully complicated place. And then, quite abruptly, it falls away.

On the Totem Bar side of the island, the reef reaches an edge where the shallow turquoise water gives way to a much deeper blue. Swimming along it, you can look down the coral-covered slope and watch it disappear into the depths. Beyond it, there's just water, becoming darker and less distinct until there's nothing more to see.

It's a remarkable sensation.

On one side, there's a landscape that feels almost familiar. Rocks, slopes, ledges, little valleys and outcrops. Fish moving between them. A world that, despite being underwater, has something of the character of a hillside. On the other, an enormous blue emptiness.

Of course, it isn't empty. There will be fish out there, perhaps rays, perhaps sharks, and innumerable creatures too small or too distant to see. But from the surface, looking into that blue, there's no way of knowing what's there. I found myself wondering just how far down it goes.

A little investigation revealed that the resort describes this stretch as a reef cliff. There are various depths quoted for the house reef and nearby dive sites, but nothing that quite answers the question of how deep that particular slope descends before it finally reaches the seabed.

And somehow, I'm rather pleased about that.

There's something wonderful about being able to swim, quite comfortably, along the edge of a landscape whose full extent remains hidden from you. The coral and fish are right there, almost within touching distance, while just a few metres away the view opens into something vast and unknowable.

It's a reminder that the world is considerably bigger than the little portion of it we happen to be able to see and that, sometimes, the most interesting part of a landscape is the bit that disappears beyond the edge of visibility.
