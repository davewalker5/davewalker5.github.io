I have a small ritual that marks the changing seasons.

The display on our coffee table changes through the year: flowers, pot pourri, and various bits and pieces gathered or arranged to suit the season. And autumn means conkers.

Not because they are particularly useful for anything.

Not because I intend to suspend them from pieces of string and challenge the neighbourhood children to mortal combat.

Simply because fresh conkers are **beautiful things**.

- That deep, polished chestnut brown
- The paler circular scar
- The extraordinary gloss they have when they have only recently fallen from the tree

They look almost as though somebody has polished them individually.

Normally, I collect a few around Abingdon and arrange them in a decorated bowl as the centrepiece of the autumn display. Except this year the horse chestnuts around Abingdon haven't cropped terribly well. There have been conkers, certainly, but nowhere near the usual abundance, and for a while I thought one of my little seasonal traditions might have to be abandoned for the year.

A very minor disappointment in the grand scheme of things, but a disappointment nonetheless.

Then this morning I went for a walk around Christchurch Meadows in Oxford. It was one of those lovely early-autumn mornings when the year seems temporarily undecided about what season it actually is: warm sunshine, blue sky, the beginnings of autumn colour, and that distinctive low golden light which somehow makes everything look slightly better than it did a month ago.

And beside the river were horse chestnut trees. Loaded with conkers. Not merely producing the occasional slightly apologetic specimen.

**Loaded.**

The ground beneath them was scattered with split green husks and fresh, glossy brown conkers, and suddenly my supposedly conkerless autumn had become a problem of quite a different sort.

> How many conkers does one reasonably need?

This is, of course, exactly the sort of question to which the correct answer is:

> Probably slightly fewer than I eventually collected

I came home with a bag full. And there is something enormously satisfying about that.

Conkers are such ordinary objects that it is very easy not to notice how extraordinary they actually look. Inside that thick, aggressively spiked green casing is something smooth, rounded and almost jewel-like.

Freshly fallen, they have a richness of colour and shine that doesn't last indefinitely. Like so many seasonal things, part of their appeal is precisely that they belong to a particular moment.

- Soon they will dull
- The leaves will properly turn
- The mornings will become colder

But for a little while there will be a bowl of impossibly glossy horse chestnuts sitting on our coffee table announcing that autumn has arrived.

So the traditional display is happening after all.

Autumn, apparently, was merely waiting for me in Oxford.
