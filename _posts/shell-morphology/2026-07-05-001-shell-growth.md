When I first started the Computational Shell Morphology project, my aim was simply to answer a question that had lodged itself in my mind during a visit to the Oxford University Museum of Natural History.

> Could I model shell growth?

At the time I imagined producing a handful of convincing shell renders before moving on to the next curiosity.

Instead, the project kept asking new questions.

Adding internal chambers naturally led to modelling siphuncles. Exploring ammonites prompted experiments with high-spired gastropods. Straight orthocones were followed by gently curved cyrtocones. Each new shell suggested another variation worth exploring.

Somewhere along the way, something changed. The project stopped feeling like a renderer and started feeling like a laboratory.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.explorer %}

The latest addition to the site is an interactive Morphology Explorer. Rather than simply looking at a collection of finished shell models, visitors can adjust the growth parameters themselves and watch entirely different morphologies emerge.

That shift feels surprisingly significant.

Natural history often begins with careful observation. We examine specimens, compare forms and look for patterns. Computational modelling adds another layer to that process by allowing us to ask a different kind of question:

> What happens if…?

- What happens if the aperture expands a little faster?
- What happens if successive whorls overlap more closely?
- What happens if growth follows a straight axis rather than a curved one?
- What happens if ornamentation is removed entirely?

The explorer does not attempt to simulate the developmental biology of any particular species in detail. Instead, it explores a geometric idea that lies beneath many shell forms: that remarkable diversity can emerge from relatively small changes to a shared growth process.

That idea was the one that caught my attention in the museum all those weeks ago, and it continues to surprise me.

One of the most satisfying aspects of developing the explorer has been discovering just how intuitive these relationships become when they are interactive. Reading about growth parameters is one thing. Moving a slider and watching an ammonite gradually transform into something that resembles a high-spired gastropod is something else entirely.

It becomes less like looking at a collection of specimens and more like exploring a landscape of possible forms and I find that an intriguing way to think about morphology.

The explorer also reflects something broader that has been happening across Field Notes: Increasingly, the site is becoming more than a place to publish observations and finished articles. Where it makes sense, I’d like it to become somewhere that readers can experiment for themselves. Interactive visualisations, computational models and exploratory tools sit quite naturally alongside notebooks, essays and photographs.

After all, one of the pleasures of natural history is not simply discovering answers. It is discovering good questions.

The shell morphology project has already travelled much further than I expected when I scribbled a short note in my notebook that simply read, _"I should model this."_

I have a feeling it still has some distance left to go.
