# An Eclipse, a Skewer and a Folded Piece of Paper

I had rather hoped to photograph today’s partial solar eclipse properly.

The obvious plan was to use my Nikon DX DSLR with the 150–600 mm lens. At 600 mm on a DX body, the Sun would have filled a useful part of the frame and, with the right filtration, the setup should have been capable of producing a nicely detailed record of the eclipse.

The important phrase there is *with the right filtration*.

Pointing a long telephoto lens at the Sun is not something to improvise with. A suitable solar filter has to go over the front of the lens, reducing the incoming light before it enters the optical system. Without that, there is a very real risk of damaging both camera and eyesight.

By the time I had worked out exactly what I needed, however, it was too late to get hold of the filter in time for the eclipse.

So much for the 600 mm lens.

Fortunately, there is another way to observe the Sun which requires rather less specialist equipment.

## The sophisticated apparatus

My solar observing equipment eventually consisted of:

* A folded piece of paper
* A kitchen skewer
* Another surface onto which the image could be projected, in this case the kitchen wall

The skewer made a small hole in the paper, producing an extremely crude pinhole camera.

The principle is beautifully simple. Light from different parts of the Sun passes through the small aperture at slightly different angles and forms an image on the surface behind it. Because this is a projected image, there is no need to look directly at the Sun.

And, rather wonderfully, it worked.

They are hardly contenders for *Astronomy Photographer of the Year*. The images are small, grainy and imperfect.

But there it is: the shape of the partially eclipsed Sun, reproduced by sunlight passing through a hole made in a folded piece of paper with a skewer.

## A useful reminder

There is something I rather like about this.

I began with thoughts of a DSLR, a 600 mm telephoto lens, a tripod and specialist solar filtration. None of that was available in time, but the absence of the ideal apparatus did not prevent the observation.

A pinhole projection has very little resolution and tells me nothing about fine detail on the solar surface. But it was quite sufficient to answer the immediate observational question: *what shape does the Sun have at this stage of the eclipse?*

The result is crude, but it is real.

It is also a useful reminder that sophistication and observational value are not the same thing. Better instruments allow us to see more, measure more and ask more demanding questions, but sometimes an extraordinarily simple apparatus is enough to make the phenomenon visible.

In this case:

**one eclipse, one skewer, and one folded piece of paper.**

Not quite the astrophotography session I had planned.

Possibly a more memorable one.
