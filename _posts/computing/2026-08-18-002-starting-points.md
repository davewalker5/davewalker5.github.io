*Or: how a tiny computer started a very long journey*

I started my computing career with a Sinclair ZX80. It had **1 KB of memory**. Not one megabyte. Not one gigabyte. One *kilobyte*.

It is tempting, looking back from a world of multi-core processors, gigabytes of memory and cloud computing, to think of machines like the ZX80 as impossibly limited.

But that misses the point.

> The ZX80 could do things

It could perform calculations. It could execute programs. It could take an idea expressed clearly enough and turn it into something that a machine could explore.

The difference between then and now is not that computers went from being useless to useful.

It is that they went from being constrained tools to astonishingly powerful ones.

## The first lesson

One of the most useful observations about computers came not from the computer itself, but from my father.

During my PhD, when I was working on mathematical problems involving systems of differential equations, I once commented that I couldn't have done the work without a computer. His response was that I could. It would just be much more convenient with one.

He was right.

A computer does not make the mathematics possible. The equations still exist. The reasoning still has to be done. The model still has to be understood.

What the computer changes is the scale of what is practical.

A calculation that might take hours or days by hand becomes something that can be repeated, explored and refined. A model that might otherwise remain a theoretical exercise becomes something that can be tested against different assumptions.

The computer does not replace the thinking: It extends it.

## The same idea, different scales

Looking back, that ZX80 was the beginning of a continuous thread.

The questions became more ambitious, but the fundamental approach remained the same:

- Describe a problem precisely
- Express it in a form a computer can process
- Explore the results
- Learn something from the behaviour of the system

Mathematical modelling. Computational chemistry. Bioinformatics. All of them are variations on the same theme.

> Take something we want to understand about the world and build a representation that allows us to ask questions

A model is not reality. A simulation is not an experiment. A computational result is not automatically an answer.

But computers allow us to explore possibilities that would otherwise remain inaccessible.

## The strange thing about progress

I have spent my professional life benefiting from computers becoming dramatically more capable.

A modern laptop would have seemed like science fiction when I first encountered the ZX80.

The computational problems I work on now would have been unimaginable on that machine.

And yet the fundamental relationship has not changed.

- The computer is still a tool
- The important part is still the question being asked

Perhaps starting with a machine that had only 1 KB of memory left me with a useful instinct:

> **Complexity should have to justify its cost**

This does not mean that modern systems should somehow return to the limitations of the past. The capabilities we have gained are extraordinary.

But capability and complexity are not the same thing.

The best tools are those that allow us to think more clearly, explore more deeply and ask better questions.

## From shells to genomes

These days I find myself using computers to explore things that would have seemed extraordinary from the perspective of that ZX80.

- I can model the growth of shells using mathematical descriptions of morphology
- I can simulate molecular evolution and reconstruct evolutionary relationships
- I can explore the growth of ancient stromatolites through computational models

The subjects are different, but the underlying activity is remarkably similar.

- A curiosity about the natural world becomes a question
- The question becomes a model
- The model becomes something the computer can explore

And the results lead to new questions.

## Still learning from 1 KB

The irony is that the ZX80 did not just introduce me to computers: It introduced me to a way of thinking.

When resources were scarce, you had to understand what you were asking the machine to do. You had to think carefully about the problem before writing the solution.

Modern computers free us from many of those constraints, and that is an extraordinary gift.

But the underlying discipline remains valuable.

A computer is not impressive because it is powerful: It is impressive because it allows us to turn curiosity into exploration.

Somewhere in my computing history is a Sinclair ZX80 with its **1 KB of memory**.

Nearly half a century later, I am sitting in front of machines millions of times more capable, using them to explore mathematics, biology and the natural world.

The hardware has changed beyond recognition.

The reason for using it has not.

A computer is still a way of asking:

> **"What happens if...?"**
