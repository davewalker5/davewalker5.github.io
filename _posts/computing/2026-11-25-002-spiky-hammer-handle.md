It all started with a file manager.

Following some particularly frustrating experiences with Finder under macOS Golden Gate, I'd eventually reached the point of forking out (pardon the pun) for a third-party file manager, ForkLift. Whilst researching the issues I'd experienced, I came across an article about Golden Gate which opened with the following statement:

> "macOS Golden Gate delivers a lot of what Mac users have been waiting for: a smarter Siri, next-generation Apple Intelligence features, and Liquid Glass improvements, to name a few"

Really?

Here I was, having spent additional money to compensate for shortcomings in one of the most fundamental components of Apple's desktop operating system, yet apparently what I'd been waiting for was an improvement to Liquid Glass.

I must have missed that particular meeting.

## A Tool, Like a Spanner

My introduction to personal computing came in the form of a Sinclair ZX80 and I was already well into my professional career when the World Wide Web came into existence, so I've witnessed an extraordinary transformation in the capabilities and applications of computing technology over the intervening decades.

Throughout that period, however, my fundamental relationship with computers has remained remarkably consistent:

> A computer is a tool

An extraordinarily powerful, versatile and intellectually stimulating tool, admittedly, but a tool nonetheless and, like a spanner, metaphorically speaking, I get it out of the toolbox, use it to accomplish something and put it away again. That something might be developing software, analysing data, investigating a scientific concept, documenting an encounter with wildlife or writing an article such as this one.

In many of those activities, selecting, renaming, moving, copying, organising and locating files is a fairly fundamental requirement. Not particularly exciting, perhaps, but then neither is the ability of a spanner to grip a nut! It's something I rather take for granted when purchasing one.

## A Hammer with a Spiky Handle

None of this should be interpreted as suggesting that I don't appreciate attractive user interfaces because I most certainly do. An attractive, thoughtfully designed interface can make a tool more pleasant, intuitive and efficient to use and there is considerable value in good design and craftsmanship. However, that value is contingent upon the tool fulfilling its fundamental purpose.

Consider, if you will, a beautifully designed hammer with a spiky handle. The craftsmanship might be exquisite, the materials might be of the highest quality and the visual appearance might be stunning. Unfortunately, it hurts when you hammer things!

I can almost imagine the accompanying marketing material:

> Introducing _Liquid Grip_ – a revolutionary reimagining of the traditional hammer handle, featuring dynamic light refraction, precision-engineered contours and an immersive tactile experience

And, somewhere amongst the customer reviews:

> It hurts when I hammer things

The handle of a hammer is, in effect, its user interface and its design should serve the purpose for which the hammer exists with aesthetic considerations complementing, rather than compromising, that purpose.

Perhaps next year's hammer will feature improvements to its revolutionary _Liquid Grip_ technology ... the spikes will be 20% shorter!

## The Unmarketable Virtues of Technology

I do appreciate that there's something of a marketing challenge here. Imagine the announcement of a major new release of macOS with the following headline features:

- Finder works reliably
- Memory management is more efficient
- Long-standing bugs have been resolved
- User interface inconsistencies have been addressed
- Existing functionality has been comprehensively tested
- Background processes consume fewer system resources

I'd find that a genuinely exciting announcement, although I suspect it might present something of a challenge to the people tasked with producing the promotional video.

How, after all, do you visually demonstrate the absence of frustration?

Perhaps a beautifully shot, slow-motion sequence of somebody successfully moving a folder from one location to another, accompanied by stirring orchestral music?

> We think you're going to love it!

## When Nothing Happens

There's something rather wonderful about technology that simply works. When I'm processing underwater footage, developing an application or analysing natural-history records, I want to devote my attention to the task at hand rather than to the behaviour of the operating system that facilitates it.

A productive day during which nothing crashes, nothing behaves unexpectedly and nothing requires a workaround might not sound particularly remarkable but that's precisely what makes it so desirable. There is considerable engineering achievement in making complex technology behave reliably and predictably but the unfortunate thing, from a marketing perspective, is that the better that engineering is, the less noticeable it becomes.

Perhaps the greatest compliment I could pay my operating system would be to spend an entire productive day using it without giving the operating system itself a moment's thought.

And on that note, Apple, could I please have a properly functioning file manager? Because I'm really not terribly concerned about the refractive properties of my toolbar.

And whilst you're at it, could you do something about the spikes on my hammer?
