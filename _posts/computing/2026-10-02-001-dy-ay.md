*Revisiting numerical integration on the RC2014, with a rather unexpected trip down memory lane.*

Sometimes a project takes you somewhere you weren't expecting. In this case, it's taken me back to the beginning of my PhD, a Commodore 64 and a differential equation for which I've always had a particular fondness.

I've recently been extending my RC2014 project with a collection of programs for solving Ordinary Differential Equations (ODEs). What started with a relatively straightforward implementation of Euler's method has grown into something rather more substantial, incorporating multiple numerical integration methods and an adaptive step-size solver.

And I've thoroughly enjoyed it.

Not least because, when I first started doing this kind of numerical work, I was writing programs to solve exactly this sort of problem on a Commodore 64.

## Back to the Commodore 64

When I started, we didn't initially have the IBM PCs that would eventually become available to us. Instead, I was doing numerical computing on a Commodore 64.

_Yes, a Commodore 64._

An eight-bit computer, with a MOS 6510 processor and 64 KB of RAM, being put to work solving mathematical problems.

Of course, modern computers have vastly more processing power and memory than we could possibly have imagined back then. But the underlying mathematics hasn't changed, and neither has the satisfaction of getting a numerical algorithm working.

Which brings me to a differential equation.

## An Old Friend

For reasons that will become apparent, I've always had a particular fondness for this deceptively simple equation:

$$
\frac{dy}{dt}=Ay
$$

Here, _y_ is the dependent variable, _t_ is the independent variable and _A_ is a constant. Depending on the value of _A_, the equation describes exponential growth or exponential decay. But there's another property that makes it particularly attractive when you're learning about numerical methods: it has an analytical solution.

Given the initial condition _y(0)=y<sub>0</sub>_, the solution is:

$$
y(t)=y_0e^{At}
$$

And that's enormously useful.

If you're developing a numerical method to solve a differential equation, it's rather reassuring to have a mathematical expression that tells you what the answer should be. You can experiment with different integration methods and step sizes, calculate numerical approximations, and then compare those approximations with the exact solution. You aren't simply producing numbers and hoping they're correct. You have an independent means of checking them, which is precisely why I started using this particular equation all those years ago.

And it's why I've returned to it for the RC2014.

## Enter the Z80

Fast-forward several decades, and I've found myself writing numerical integration programs again. This time, the machine is an RC2014 Mini II, built around another eight-bit processor, the Zilog Z80, and the programming environment is Microsoft BASIC.

There's something rather satisfying about returning to scientific computing on hardware from broadly the same generation as the equipment I was using at the beginning of my PhD.

The project started with implementations of three numerical integration methods:

- Euler's method
- Euler Predictor-Corrector, also known as Heun's method
- Fourth-order Runge-Kutta (RK4)

Each method approaches the same fundamental problem differently, with corresponding differences in computational effort and numerical accuracy.

- Euler's method is wonderfully straightforward - it uses the derivative at the current point to estimate where the solution will be after the next integration step
- The Predictor-Corrector method improves upon this by estimating the solution at the next point and using that estimate to calculate a corrected result
- RK4 goes further, evaluating the derivative at four points during each integration step and combining the results to produce a more accurate approximation

Naturally, the additional accuracy comes at a computational cost and, when you're working with an eight-bit computer, computational cost is something you can appreciate!

## Making It Adaptive

Having implemented the three integration methods, the next step was to introduce _adaptive step-size integration_. Ordinarily, a fixed-step solver advances through the solution using a predetermined step size in the independent variable, _t_. But this creates an interesting problem.

- Choose a large step size, and the solution may be insufficiently accurate
- Choose a very small step size, and the computer may perform far more calculations than necessary

Adaptive integration attempts to address this problem by adjusting the step size according to an estimate of the numerical error. My implementation uses a technique called *step doubling*.

For each proposed integration step, the solver calculates the result in two different ways.

- First, it performs one full integration step
- Then it repeats the calculation using two half-steps, starting from the same initial point

The difference between the two results provides an estimate of the numerical error. If that difference exceeds the specified tolerance, the solver reduces the step size and tries again.

The technique can be used with each of the three integration methods, so I've structured the implementation to keep the adaptive logic separate from the numerical integration algorithms themselves.

It's all written in Microsoft BASIC, using numbered subroutines and `GOSUB`.

_And it runs on a Z80._

I have to admit that I find this disproportionately entertaining.

## A Picture Is Worth a Thousand Numbers

Of course, producing numerical results is one thing. Being able to examine them is another. The programs provide both tabulated results and a simple text-based graph, displayed through an ordinary serial terminal. For example, consider the equation:

$$
\frac{dy}{dt}=0.5y
$$

With the initial condition _y(0) = 0.5_, the analytical solution is:

$$
y(t)=0.5e^{0.5t}
$$

At _t = 10_, the numerical solution is approximately 74.1968 and the analytical solution gives:

$$
y(10)=0.5e^5\approx74.2066
$$

That's an absolute error of approximately 0.0098, or around 0.013%. Not bad for an eight-bit computer running BASIC!

Of course, that's a single example rather than a comprehensive assessment of the integration algorithm. Different step sizes and different equations will produce different results.

Which raises an interesting question.

## How Good Is Good Enough?

Having implemented the integration methods and adaptive solver, the obvious next step is to investigate their behaviour more systematically and this is where my old friend, the exponential differential equation, really comes into its own.

Because we know the analytical solution, we can measure the actual numerical error rather than relying exclusively on the estimated error produced by the adaptive algorithm. I'm planning to extend the project with an experimental framework that will allow me to compare the three integration methods. In particular, I want to investigate the relationship between numerical accuracy, integration step size and computational effort.

- How many evaluations of the differential equation does each method require?
- How does reducing the step size improve accuracy?
- How does adaptive integration compare with fixed-step integration?
- And how much computational effort is required to achieve a particular level of accuracy?

There's also scope to improve the adaptive controller so that it can increase the step size when appropriate, rather than simply reducing it when the estimated error becomes excessive. The intention is to turn the existing solver into something of a miniature numerical analysis laboratory.

_All running on an RC2014._

Because, apparently, that's what I now consider entertainment.

## Coming Full Circle

There's a rather satisfying circularity to all of this. All those years ago, I was writing numerical algorithms on a Commodore 64, using a simple differential equation with a known analytical solution to help me understand and verify what I was doing. Several decades later, I'm writing numerical algorithms on another eight-bit computer, using precisely the same differential equation.

The hardware is different, the implementations have evolved, and I've acquired a few years of additional experience along the way but the fundamental pleasure is remarkably familiar: It's the pleasure of taking a mathematical problem, translating it into an algorithm, implementing that algorithm and then watching a computer produce the expected results.

And perhaps most importantly, there's the pleasure of experimenting, discovering what works and understanding *why* it works.

I didn't start this RC2014 project because I needed an eight-bit computer to solve differential equations. There are rather more practical ways of doing that.

I started it because I enjoy making things, experimenting with computers and discovering what I can persuade relatively modest hardware to accomplish.

Returning to something I was doing at the beginning of my career has added an unexpected personal dimension.

Forty years, two eight-bit computers and one differential equation.

And I'm still having fun.
