*Or: from 1 KB of memory to a phone powerful enough to run a small research laboratory, and the calculator still just works*

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.calculator %}

This is my first calculator, a Texas Instruments TI-30. It sits upstairs, restored and still perfectly functional and it's currently displaying:

$$
\sqrt{\pi}
$$

Not because this is a particularly important calculation, but because it seemed an appropriate greeting from a small machine that started a very long relationship with computation.

Here is an old calculator, still quietly doing mathematics, while my modern phone is having an existential crisis about storage, which made me realise something rather amusing:

> The TI-30 has never once asked me to delete anything before allowing me to perform a calculation

- It has never required a software update
- It has never needed more storage
- It has never displayed a message telling me that I need another 12 GB of free space before I can use it

Perhaps there is something to learn from this.

## The beginning of a journey

The TI-30 was not my first encounter with computing. That honour belongs to a Sinclair ZX80.

The ZX80 had **1 KB of memory** and was the beginning of a journey that eventually led through mathematical modelling, a PhD, computational chemistry, bioinformatics and a career built around using computers to explore the natural world.

The ZX80 and the TI-30 were very different machines:

- The ZX80 was programmable
- The TI-30 was a dedicated calculator

But they shared something important:

> They were tools

They existed to help a human being think.

## The calculator revolution

It is easy to underestimate what calculators changed.

Before electronic calculators became commonplace, many mathematical tasks involved tables, interpolation, logarithms and careful manual arithmetic.

The calculator did not make mathematics possible: People had been doing mathematics for thousands of years. What it changed was the practical cost of doing mathematics.

- A calculation that was tedious became immediate
- A calculation that interrupted the flow of reasoning became something that could be checked instantly
- A student could spend less time performing arithmetic and more time understanding what the arithmetic meant

The calculator did not replace thinking: It removed friction from thinking.

## My father's observation

During my PhD, while working with mathematical models involving systems of differential equations, I once commented to my father that I could not have done the work without a computer.

His response was that I could.

It would just be more convenient with one.

He was right.

- The equations still existed
- The mathematics still had to be understood
- The model still had to be constructed
- The computer did not provide the insight - it provided the ability to explore

A calculation that would have taken an impractical amount of time by hand became something that could be repeated, modified and investigated.

That distinction has stayed with me.

## The computing continuum

Looking back, the ZX80, the TI-30 and the computers I use today are all part of the same continuum.

The technology has changed beyond recognition but the principle has not.

- A question becomes a mathematical description
- The mathematical description becomes something a machine can process
- The results lead to more questions

This is the same process whether the question is:

- "What does this equation do?"
- "How does a biological system evolve?"
- "How might a shell grow?"
- "What happens under different environmental conditions?"

The machine changes the scale.

The curiosity remains human.

## The paradox of progress

Of course, modern computers are vastly more capable than a ZX80 or a TI-30. My current machines can perform calculations that would have been unimaginable when I first encountered those devices.

They can simulate complex biological systems, analyse huge datasets and explore mathematical spaces that earlier generations could barely imagine. That is extraordinary.

But there is a useful question to ask of any tool:

> **What does the additional complexity buy us?**

- A more powerful computer is valuable because it allows us to do things that were previously impractical
- A more complex system is not automatically a better system

The best tools disappear into the task.

## The calculator that just works

Which brings me back to the TI-30:

- It has one job
- It performs calculations
- It does that job quietly and reliably
- It does not need an account
- It does not need connectivity
- It does not need storage management
- It does not need me to move its backup to an external drive
- It simply waits until I ask it a question

There is something rather elegant about that.

## From calculators to computational science

It would be wrong to conclude that simple tools are always better: The computational power available today is astonishing, and I would not willingly give it up.

The models I can build now, the simulations I can run and the biological questions I can explore would have been impossible on those early machines.

Progress matters.

But perhaps starting with limited machines gave me a useful instinct:

> **A tool should make thinking easier, not become the thing that needs managing**

- The ZX80 taught me that computers could extend curiosity
- The TI-30 taught me that the right tool could remove friction
- Modern computers allow me to explore ideas far beyond anything I could have imagined then

And somewhere upstairs sits a calculator from my school days that still does exactly what it was designed to do.

- No updates
- No storage warnings
- No deleted applications

Just:

Press a button. Get an answer.

Sometimes progress is complicated.

Sometimes the simplest tools still have something to teach us.
