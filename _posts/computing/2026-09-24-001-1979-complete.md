A couple of days ago I ended a Field Notes post about preparing the RC2014 for its new speech synthesiser with:

> The MG005 hardware arrives tomorrow.
> What could possibly go wrong? 😉

Well, it arrived. I built it. And now the RC2014 talks.

More importantly, I think the hardware for this particular RC2014 is finished.

## The 1979 Machine

I hadn't originally set out to recreate a particular historical computer. The RC2014 is not, after all, a replica of one. It is a modern modular computer built around the Z80, designed very much in the spirit of the homebrew and hobby computers of the late 1970s.

But as I've added things to mine, it has gradually acquired an identity of its own and looking at it now, I've realised that it rather nicely encapsulates my idea of the state of hobby computing around 1979.

At its heart is the Z80. Around that I've now got:

- A dedicated character display
- The SID-Ulator for programmable sound and music
- Digital I/O with buttons and LEDs
- The MG005 speech synthesiser
- A small amplifier and speaker

Along with the normal serial terminal, the machine can now accept physical input, control lights, display text, make music and speak and that feels like enough. Not because there aren't more things I *could* add, but because adding them would begin to turn it into a different machine.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.front_panel %}

## Not Quite 1979

There is one fairly obvious chronological cheat.

> The character display is an LCD

That wasn't actually the original plan. I wanted to use the RC2014 bubble LED display, which would have looked gloriously appropriate for the period, but unfortunately that board had been discontinued. So the LCD is less a deliberate attempt to sneak newer technology into 1979 and more a concession to trying to build a 1979-inspired computer nearly half a century later.

Apparently component availability becomes slightly problematic after 47 years!

Who knew?

More importantly, I'm not trying to build a museum-quality recreation of a computer that actually existed in 1979. This is more like a computer that *could belong to that world*. It's my interpretation of the things that made microcomputers exciting to me: a processor, some memory and a collection of bits of hardware that can be persuaded to interact with the outside world.

## A Computer You Can See

Even the case has ended up fitting that philosophy. It isn't actually a computer case:  It's a transparent display case intended for a model car, that I found on Amazon. That turned out to be almost exactly what I wanted, because putting the RC2014 inside an opaque box would rather miss the point.

I want to see it. I want to see the boards, the bus, the LEDs, the wiring and the little amplifier and speaker. Part of the appeal of the RC2014 is that the computer hasn't disappeared inside an appliance.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.top_down %}

You can still look at it and think:

> That's the computer

Looking from above and going from front to back, the current stack is the display, SID-Ulator, a spare slot from which I'm taking power for the small audio amplifier, to the left, Digital I/O and finally the speech synthesiser.

And that's how I think it is going to stay.

## Hardware Complete

There are already other RC2014 hardware projects forming in my head. One is likely to be a self-contained CP/M machine: RC2014, CP/M support, VGA output, a small portable monitor and a keyboard. That could be great fun.

But I specifically *don't* want to turn this RC2014 into that machine.

Even though CP/M itself belongs to the period, a self-contained system with VGA, monitor and keyboard would take this build away from what I wanted it to represent, and particularly away from my own early experiences of computing.

As a separate project, that's absolutely fine.

Likewise, another RC2014 may eventually become a synthesiser: not simply a computer that happens to have a sound card, but a machine built specifically as a musical instrument.

Those ideas don't need to be squeezed into this machine - they can have RC2014s of their own.

That gives me a useful boundary: new hardware ideas that would fundamentally change the character of this machine don't get added to it but become another project.

## Now Make It Do Something

Declaring the hardware finished doesn't mean that I've finished with the machine. Quite the opposite. It means the emphasis shifts back to the question that has increasingly come to define this whole project for me.

Not:

> What can the RC2014 do?

But:

> **What can I make out of it?**

Now I've got several different pieces of hardware that can be used together rather than merely demonstrated individually.

- The SID-Ulator can make music
- The MG005 can speak

Which naturally raises the question of whether I can get the SID-Ulator to play **Daisy Bell** while the speech synthesiser says the words in time with the tune. There is something wonderfully circular about that idea.

As I've mentioned before, "Daisy Bell" was used in early computer speech synthesis work, most famously with an IBM 704 in 1961. Kubrick subsequently turned it into one of the most memorable moments in *2001: A Space Odyssey* when HAL 9000 sings it as his higher functions are being disconnected.

And now, apparently, I'm contemplating getting a Z80 to provide the accompaniment while an SP0256-AL2 attempts the vocals.

I suspect the timing will be terrible.

I suspect the singing will be worse.

I also suspect I'll find it enormously entertaining.

## Finished, But Not Finished

So that's it. The hardware is finished. The RC2014 isn't.

What I've ended up with is not a replica of a particular historical computer, nor was it ever intended to be.

It's something rather more personal: my version of a late-1970s experimental microcomputer, assembled from modern reproductions and modern interpretations of the sorts of hardware that made computers fascinating to me in the first place.

- It has switches and lights
- It can display things
- It can make music
- And now it can talk

That seems like a good place to stop adding hardware and start finding out what I can make all of it do together.

Although if it starts singing **Daisy** without being asked, I may reconsider.
