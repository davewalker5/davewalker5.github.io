With the MG005 speech synthesiser card due to arrive, I’ve been using the emulator to refine the speech support and, more importantly, to get some practice constructing words and sentences from allophones.

Naturally, this very quickly turned into making Charles quote HAL 9000.

There was really no other possible outcome.

The current repertoire includes:

- “The 9000 series has a perfect operational record”
- “I’m sorry, Dave. I’m afraid I can’t do that”
- “Just what do you think you’re doing, Dave?”
- “Dave, my mind is going. I can feel it”
- and, inevitably, “Daisy, Daisy, give me your answer true”

The last one feels particularly appropriate. “Daisy Bell” was famously used in early computer speech synthesis demonstrations, including the IBM 704 in 1961. Kubrick’s use of it for HAL was a deliberate nod to that history, so there is something wonderfully circular about a Z80-based retrocomputer recreating it through an emulator running on a modern laptop while waiting for the real speech hardware to arrive.

There is also something slightly alarming about how quickly a primitive synthetic voice acquires personality when it starts saying HAL lines.

By the time it reaches:

> “Dave, my mind is going. I can feel it”

I actually start to feel slightly sorry for it!

The interpreter’s completely unsympathetic `OK` immediately afterwards does not help.

## A Tiny Rule-Based Speech System

The starting point for generating the allophone sequence for a new phrase is the speech converter that I've written as part of my RC2014 project. Give it a phrase, out pops a simple BASIC program that, when transferred to the RC2014 emulator or the real hardware with an MG005 card, will speak the phrase:

```basic
10 REM MG005 SP0256-AL2 SPEECH
20 FOR I=1 TO 13
30 READ A
40 IF (INP(31) AND 2)=0 THEN GOTO 40
50 OUT 31,A
60 NEXT I
70 END
80 REM PHRASE: Hello Z80
90 REM HH1 EH LL OW PA3 ZZ EH DD1 PA3 EY TT2 IY
100 DATA 27,7,45,53,2,43,7,21,2,20,13,19
110 REM PA1
120 DATA 0
```

Strictly speaking, it's already more than a simple lookup table. It first tokenises the input into words, numbers and punctuation. Known words are handled by explicit pronunciations, numbers are expanded into spoken English, and unfamiliar words fall back to a set of ordered spelling rules.

Those rules scan from left to right, trying groups such as `SH`, `TH`, `EE`, `TION` and `OUGH` before falling back to individual letters.

It even applies a deliberately crude pronunciation rule such as dropping a final `E` from longer words.

So there is already a _primitive_ rule-based speech system in there. The important word is **primitive**. English pronunciation is sufficiently irregular that those rules can only produce an approximation, and the converter cannot reliably infer things such as vowel sounds, stress or the pronunciation of every name.

In practice, that makes the converter my **first-pass generator** rather than the final authority.

## Allophones are not Spelling

The useful part of the exercise has been learning that constructing intelligible speech from _allophones_ is not simply a matter of translating letters into sounds one by one.

An _allophone_ is a particular spoken variant of a basic speech sound. In a speech synthesiser such as the SP0256-AL2, each allophone is stored as a small pre-recorded or encoded sound unit — for example a particular vowel, consonant or pause. Speech is generated by sending a sequence of these allophones to the synthesiser in the right order and timing, so that the individual sound fragments join together into recognisable words and sentences.

To create the sequence, I give the speech converter a sentence, it does the repetitive decomposition work and its rules produce an initial allophone sequence. I then listen to the result in the emulator and adjust it by ear. The workflow is:

```text
generate -> listen -> tweak -> repeat
```

The _tweak_ stage is still very much empirical. Sometimes it means substituting one allophone for another; sometimes it means changing a pause or consonant; and sometimes it means removing a sound that the spelling suggests ought to be there.

A good example is **“gentlemen”**.

My current rendering is:

```text
GG1 EH NN1 TT2 LL MM EH NN1
```

What is interesting is what is *not* there - the second written “e” is effectively omitted. Trying to represent it explicitly produces speech that sounds less like “gentlemen”, while leaving it out produces a much more convincing result. That was an important little lesson.

> The job is not to reproduce the spelling of the word. The job is to reproduce the **sound that a listener recognises as the word**.

Natural speech is full of reduction, compression and sounds that are implied by the transition between neighbouring phonemes rather than clearly articulated in isolation. English spelling is therefore only a starting point.

In other words, the best allophone sequence may look slightly “wrong” while sounding much more right.

## Refining the Speech Engine

There is also a slightly more interesting possibility hiding in all this.

Every time I discover that a particular combination works better — that a vowel should disappear, that one consonant variant works better than another, or that a sound needs stretching or shortening — I am effectively discovering a further pronunciation rule or exception that can be added to the converter's configuration to improve the result.

So the process is becoming one of automatic first approximation followed by empirical refinement. The converter gets me most of the way there; then I listen, tweak the allophones, and feed useful discoveries back into its pronunciation data and rules.

That was not really the original intention, but then quite a lot of this RC2014 project seems to begin with:

> “I wonder if I can make it do this?”

... and ends several commits later with something rather more elaborate.

For the moment, however, Charles is apparently HAL.

The MG005 hardware arrives tomorrow.

What could possibly go wrong? 😉
