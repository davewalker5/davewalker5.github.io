*Or: how having a wisdom tooth extracted led to an unexpected outbreak of octopus-related software engineering*

I had a wisdom tooth extracted.

This resulted in an octopus living on a Z80.

There are several intermediate steps.

## Something quiet to do

Having a tooth extracted isn't generally something I would regard as a particularly productive event.

The sensible advice afterwards is fairly straightforward: take things easy, avoid strenuous activity and give everything a chance to settle down. So I needed something quiet to do. Fortunately, I have an RC2014.

There were some hardware projects waiting to be built, and sitting at a desk with a soldering iron seemed to qualify reasonably well as taking things easy.

So I started building. First came a backplane expansion. Then came an LCD Display Driver. Neither of these things had anything whatever to do with octopuses.

At this point the consequences of the dental work remained reasonably predictable.

That didn't last.

## One thing leads to another

The LCD is a simple character display - two lines of sixteen characters, but it has one particularly entertaining feature: It allows the programmer to define a small number of custom characters using tiny 5 × 8 pixel matrices.

Naturally, I started experimenting. And because I had also been reading Peter Godfrey-Smith's *Other Minds*, somewhere along the way I drew an octopus. Then I drew another slightly different octopus. And another. Displaying the three characters successively made the octopus move.

This was enormously pleasing.

It was also the point at which the consequences of having a wisdom tooth extracted began to get slightly out of hand.

## What if?

There is a question which has been responsible for quite a lot of my RC2014 project:

> **What if...?**

- What if the octopus moved around the display?
- What if he had moods?
- What if the moods emerged from underlying needs?
- What if he was sometimes hungry, bored, tired or irritated?
- What if different runs gave him slightly different personalities?
- What if he remembered what I had just done?
- What if I could interact with him?
- What if I could do that using physical buttons?
- What if he objected when I annoyed him?

By this point the tiny animated LCD character had acquired a name.

> Charles

And, as I wrote in my previous Field Note, Charles has opinions.

## An unplanned program

None of this was on a roadmap.

There was no project brief saying:

> Build a virtual octopus with competing internal needs, pseudo-random personality, short-term memory, state-dependent behaviour, LCD animation and physical interaction

That would have been a very peculiar project brief. Instead, each step suggested the next one.

- The LCD made custom characters possible
- Custom characters made the octopus possible
- The octopus made animation interesting
- Animation made a character imaginable
- A character suggested behaviour
- Behaviour suggested internal state
- Internal state suggested interaction
- Interaction suggested physical controls

And physical controls created another engineering problem.

## Silliness gets serious

Writing to the LCD from BASIC is comparatively slow.

Once Charles could be controlled using the RC2014 Digital I/O card, that created the possibility that a quick button press might happen while the program was busy communicating with the display. The program could miss it entirely.

So the Digital I/O port is sampled repeatedly during the LCD delays. A valid input is captured in a software latch so that it can be processed during the next animation poll even if the physical button has already been released.

The program then has to deal with release detection, debouncing and LED feedback. These are perfectly respectable little hardware and software problems.

I encountered them because I needed to make absolutely certain that my Z80 didn't accidentally miss the button used to annoy an imaginary octopus.

There is something about that which I find deeply satisfying.

## The other way around

We often imagine projects beginning with requirements.

- First decide what the finished thing should do
- Then work out what capabilities are needed
- Then implement them

There is nothing wrong with that. It is how a great deal of useful engineering gets done.

But Charles happened almost exactly the other way around.

> **The capability created the idea**

Before I had the LCD Display Driver, an animated octopus wasn't something I was considering writing for the RC2014.

- Once I discovered what could be done with custom characters, it became possible to imagine one
- Once the octopus moved, it became possible to imagine him having behaviour
- Once Charles had behaviour, physical interaction became interesting

Each new capability changed the range of things I could imagine doing next.

I rather like that way of working.

## Curiosity has momentum

This doesn't mean the project is directionless. One thing isn't simply being abandoned for another unrelated thing. There is continuity.

- Something gets built
- I learn what it can do
- That suggests an experiment
- The experiment reveals another possibility
- That possibility raises a question
- Answering the question sometimes requires another piece of hardware or another piece of software
- And that creates more possibilities

Curiosity develops momentum.

The destination isn't known in advance, but each step follows quite naturally from the one before it.

Looking backwards, the route makes perfect sense but looking forwards, much of it was completely invisible.

## Planning and emergence

I think there is an important distinction here between a project being **unplanned** and a project being **without purpose**.

Charles wasn't planned.

But every step in developing him had a purpose once the preceding step existed.

- I wanted to understand custom characters
- Then I wanted to animate them
- Then I wanted the animation to move
- Then I wanted the creature to behave
- Then I wanted its behaviour to have enough internal consistency to be interesting
- Then I wanted to interact with it physically

At each stage there was a concrete problem to solve.

The fact that I couldn't have written the complete list of problems at the beginning doesn't make solving them any less worthwhile.

If anything, it made me more interested in solving them.

They were problems I had discovered for myself.

## Room for accidents

Perhaps this is one reason hobby projects can be so rewarding: They have room for accidents.

- There is no customer waiting for Charles
- There is no business case for Charles
- There is no delivery milestone requiring an octopus to become feisty by the end of the week

I can spend time on something simply because the question interests me. And if answering one question reveals a much more interesting question, I am free to follow that instead.

That freedom would be disastrous in some professional projects but here it is almost the point.

The RC2014 gives me a wonderfully comprehensible little computer on which to explore whatever happens to catch my attention.

Sometimes that produces useful programs.

Sometimes it produces mathematical experiments.

Sometimes it produces hardware demonstrations.

> And sometimes it produces Charles

## Consequences

So the sequence of events now looks something like this:

- Have a wisdom tooth extracted
- Need something quiet to do
- Build an RC2014 backplane expansion
- Build an LCD Display Driver
- Experiment with custom characters
- Draw an octopus
- Animate the octopus
- Remember the octopuses in *Other Minds*
- Create Charles
- Give Charles needs and moods
- Give Charles personality and memory
- Add physical interaction
- Solve an input-latching problem
- Release Charles
- Write a Field Note about Charles
- Become inordinately pleased with the entire thing

There is now another unbuilt RC2014 card sitting on my desk. It's a sound card. I'm going to build it next and I could make a list of sensible programs to write for it.

I could decide in advance exactly what I intend to learn from it.

I could produce a roadmap.

But recent experience suggests another approach might be more interesting.

- I'll build it
- I'll find out what it can do

And then I'll ask:

> **What if...?**

I had a wisdom tooth extracted.

A few days later, there was an opinionated octopus living on a Z80.

I have absolutely no idea where the sound card will lead ...

**I think that's rather the point**
