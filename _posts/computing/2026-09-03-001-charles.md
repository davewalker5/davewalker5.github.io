*Or: how reading a book about octopuses eventually resulted in one living on my RC2014*

I have just built what may be my favourite program in my entire RC2014 repository.

It is not one of the mathematical programs.

It is not one of the aviation programs.

It is not even particularly useful.

It is an octopus called Charles.

And Charles has opinions.

## Other minds

I've been reading Peter Godfrey-Smith's *Other Minds: The Octopus, the Sea, and the Deep Origins of Consciousness*.

The book explores the evolution of nervous systems and consciousness, with particular attention to cephalopods and the extraordinary evolutionary path that produced animals such as octopuses.

Among the things that fascinated me were accounts of experiments involving captive octopuses.

One of them was Charles.

Charles was one of three octopuses used in an early learning experiment by the psychologist Peter Dews. The animals were presented with a lever which could be operated to obtain food.

Charles was not, apparently, an especially cooperative experimental subject.

Rather than simply learning to operate the apparatus as intended, he applied considerable force to it, eventually bending and breaking the lever. He also repeatedly interfered with a lamp above the tank and had a tendency to direct jets of water out of the tank.

Elsewhere in the book is an account of another captive octopus in New Zealand which apparently took a dislike to a particular member of the laboratory staff. When she passed the tank, the octopus would direct a jet containing around half a gallon of water at the back of her neck.

I liked these octopuses immediately.

Not because I imagine that we can straightforwardly interpret their behaviour in human terms. That would be anthropomorphism of the most obvious kind.

But the combination of curiosity, individuality, unpredictability and apparent bloody-mindedness is enormously appealing.

Charles, in particular, stuck in my mind.

## Meanwhile, back at the RC2014

Entirely separately, I had been adding an LCD Display Driver to my RC2014.

This is a character display rather than a graphical one. It displays two lines of characters and, rather wonderfully, allows a small number of custom characters to be defined by the programmer.

Each character occupies a tiny 5 × 8 matrix of pixels.

Naturally, therefore, I drew an octopus.

Because why wouldn't you?

One octopus became three slightly different octopuses, stored as three custom characters and displaying them successively produced a tiny animated octopus.

This pleased me far more than it reasonably should have done.

And then I made the mistake which has been responsible for a surprising amount of the RC2014 project:

> **I wondered what else I could do with it**

## Charles lives

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.charles %}

Once the octopus moved, it seemed unreasonable for it not to have opinions, so the animated glyph became Charles.

Initially the idea was simply to give him a few states and messages. That escalated.

Charles now has five underlying needs:

* Hunger
* Happiness
* Energy
* Boredom
* Irritation

These change as time passes and in response to interactions.

His current mood is derived from those competing needs. He can be content, bored, hungry, sleepy, cross or feisty depending upon which conditions dominate.

He also acquires a small pseudo-random personality when the program starts, including variations in:

* Feistiness
* Patience
* Appetite
* Sociability

The intention isn't to create artificial intelligence, still less to model real octopus cognition.

It is simply to provide enough interacting state that Charles's behaviour isn't completely predictable.

## Charles remembers

That still wasn't enough.

Charles now remembers the most recent interaction, how many times it has been repeated and how recently he has been annoyed.

This matters because Charles has boundaries.

Pet him and he will normally be happier. Pet him repeatedly and eventually:

> `STOP THAT`

Play with him and he will normally enjoy it, unless he is hungry, exhausted or has simply had enough.

Feed him and he will generally approve of the arrival of crab.

Annoy him and his irritation rises. Annoy him repeatedly and his responses become progressively less charitable.

Eventually, under suitable circumstances:

> `YOU ARE A FISH.`

There is something immensely satisfying about being insulted by an octopus running in BASIC on a Z80.

## Charles does things

Charles also experiences spontaneous events.

- He might notice something interesting
- He might find a tiny crab
- He might disapprove of something for no stated reason
- He might perform a somersault

These events make small changes to his internal state, while pseudo-random variations also affect things such as metabolism and the consequences of interactions.

The randomness is deliberately constrained.

I didn't want Charles simply selecting arbitrary behaviour from a collection of possibilities. His underlying needs and personality remain dominant.

Randomness modifies Charles.

It doesn't replace him.

## Giving Charles a home

The first implementation was entirely text based. That allowed the behavioural model to be developed and, importantly, made it possible to inspect exactly what was happening inside it.

But Charles had started life as an LCD character and he needed to go home.

The LCD version therefore uses the three custom characters as successive animation frames. A single character cell effectively becomes a tiny animated sprite.

Charles moves backwards and forwards across part of the top line of the display with his current mood appears alongside him and his latest comment or reaction on the second line.

There is very little room.

And I think that makes Charles better.

A two-line character display forces his observations to be terse and there is no room for sophisticated dialogue or elaborate graphics.

- Charles moves
- Charles reacts
- Charles judges you

## Charles notices you

Then I added the Digital I/O card. Four physical buttons became:

* Feed
* Play
* Pet
* Annoy

Additional buttons provide diagnostics and a clean exit.

The corresponding LEDs illuminate when an action is accepted.

And this is where a thoroughly silly idea started generating some quite interesting engineering problems.

Writing to the LCD from BASIC is comparatively slow. If the program only examined the Digital I/O port between complete display operations, a short button press could disappear before the program noticed it.

So the input is sampled repeatedly during the LCD delays.

A valid press is captured in a one-byte software latch and processed during the next animation poll, even if the button has already been released.

The program then waits for release, clears the latch and applies a short debounce delay before accepting another action.

In other words, I found myself thinking quite seriously about input sampling, latching, timing and hardware feedback...

...because I wanted to make sure I didn't accidentally miss the button used to annoy an imaginary octopus.

## Play produces engineering

I think this may be my favourite part of the whole exercise.

I didn't set out to investigate input handling while a Z80 BASIC program was communicating with a relatively slow peripheral.

I didn't decide that the RC2014 catalogue required an experiment involving competing internal state, pseudo-random personality, short-term memory and state-dependent responses.

I didn't even decide that I needed an animated character moving around an LCD.

I drew an octopus.

Then I asked:

> **What if...?**

One question produced another.

- What if the octopus moved?
- What if it had moods?
- What if those moods emerged from underlying needs?
- What if individual instances behaved slightly differently?
- What if Charles remembered what I had just done?
- What if I could interact with him using physical buttons?
- What if the LEDs responded?
- What if a quick button press happened while BASIC was busy updating the LCD?

At some point the joke had quietly turned into a fairly substantial program.

That seems to me to be a perfectly good way for engineering to happen.

## Constraints create character

There is another aspect of Charles that I particularly like.

The limitations of the RC2014 and its display aren't merely technical restrictions which the program has to overcome. They have helped determine what Charles is.

- His entire graphical existence fits into a 5 × 8 character cell
- His habitat consists of two lines of sixteen characters
- His vocabulary has to fit on one of them
- The machine is a Z80 with 32K of RAM
- The program is written in BASIC

There is no sophisticated graphics system, animation framework, event system or user-interface toolkit.

And yet none of those things is actually necessary.

- A few pixels moving between three frames are enough to suggest an octopus
- A handful of numbers are enough to give him needs
- A few persistent values are enough to give him memory
- Some carefully constrained randomness is enough to make him unpredictable
- And terse messages are enough to give him considerably more personality than he has any right to possess

The rest happens in the mind of the person watching him.

## Why Charles matters to me

Charles serves no useful purpose and I think that is *very* important.

I've written programs for the RC2014 that calculate great-circle distances, perform aviation calculations, demonstrate algorithms, play games and interact with hardware.

Many of them are considerably more useful than Charles.

Some are probably more technically interesting.

But Charles may nevertheless be my favourite.

He connects several things that I enjoy: reading, programming, electronics, retro-computing, experimentation and a certain amount of silliness.

More importantly, he wasn't planned.

He emerged from curiosity.

- A book led to an idea
- An LCD experiment gave the idea a physical form
- One question led to another

And eventually there was a tiny animated octopus moving around a character display, responding to physical buttons and occasionally expressing strong opinions about me.

Perhaps that is one of the things I value most about this entire RC2014 project.

Retro-computing doesn't have to mean reproducing exactly what computers did forty or fifty years ago.

These machines are wonderfully comprehensible platforms on which to experiment, learn and make things.

Sometimes those things can be useful.

Sometimes they can explore mathematics or computing history.

And sometimes:

> **You can make an octopus live on a Z80 simply because doing so makes you happy**

I think that is reason enough.
