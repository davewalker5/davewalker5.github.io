There is something inherently slightly ridiculous about doing ecological modelling on a Z80. Which, naturally, is part of the attraction.

Over the last few days I have been adding a small collection of ecology programs to my RC2014 — a home-built computer based around the Z80 processor and running Microsoft BASIC. So far it contains three seasonal wildlife models and, most recently, a program for analysing behavioural phases within bat echolocation sequences.

None of these things originated on the RC2014 which is what makes the exercise interesting. They began as much larger pieces of work on my MacBook, were published in the wildlife section of _Field Notes_, subsequently made their way onto a TI-84 Plus CE-T Python calculator as part of my [Pocket Ecology](/wildlife/pocket/) project, and have now somehow ended up running on an 8-bit computer.

There is a progression here and each step asks essentially the same question:

> **How much of the original ecological analysis can I carry with me?**

## From Analysis to Model

The seasonal modelling started with wildlife observations and analysis on my MacBook. The full analysis environment is where I can inspect the data, experiment with different mathematical representations, fit models and decide whether those models are doing anything remotely sensible.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.bluebell_macbook %}

From that work I ended up with three different kinds of seasonal behaviour:

- **Seasonal presence**, for species whose observable activity is confined to a particular part of the year
- **Winter visitors**, whose seasonal presence crosses the year boundary
- **Resident detectability**, for species that remain present throughout the year but become more or less likely to be observed as conditions and behaviour change

The important thing is that the MacBook work produces something much smaller than the original observational dataset - it produces a mathematical model and a set of fitted parameters. Once those exist, I no longer need the entire analysis environment in order to reproduce the modelled behaviour.

That made the next step possible.

## Pocket Ecology

The first deliberately constrained implementation was on the TI-84 Plus CE-T Python calculator. This was part of my [_Pocket Ecology_](/wildlife/pocket/) project: an experiment in seeing how much useful ecological computation could be carried around on a calculator.

For the seasonal models, the calculator does not repeat the original fitting process. Instead, it takes the model and its fitted parameters and solves the resulting ordinary differential equation. The full analytical journey has therefore been reduced to something considerably more compact:

**observations → analysis → fitted model → numerical solution**

The TI-84 only needs the last two parts and that turned out to be enough. The resulting curves still describe the same seasonal ecological ideas, even though the original analysis that produced them has disappeared from the machine.

## Then Came the Z80

Once the models were running on the TI-84, an obvious and slightly mischievous question presented itself.

> Could I make them run on the RC2014?

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.bluebell_rc2014 %}

The answer turned out to be yes. The RC2014 implementations solve the same models using numerical methods written in BASIC, including Euler, predictor-corrector and fourth-order Runge-Kutta integration.

There is even adaptive step sizing.

On a Z80.

This pleases me far more than is probably reasonable.

But there is also a serious point buried beneath the retrocomputing amusement - the model has survived another reduction in computing environment.

- The MacBook is where the ecological question is investigated and the model developed
- The calculator carries the model around
- The RC2014 demonstrates that the essential mathematics can be reduced still further and still produce something recognisably related to the original ecological analysis

## And Then the Bats Arrived

The bat work followed a similar route, although the thing being reduced was different.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.feeding_buzz %}

My original Bat Call Analysis software runs on the MacBook and processes recordings I have made using bat detectors. The desktop software can examine spectrograms, detect individual pulses, take acoustic measurements and export structured data for further analysis.

One of the things I became particularly interested in was pulse timing. A bat pass is not simply a collection of identical sounds - as the animal's behaviour changes, the timing of its echolocation pulses changes too.

- Search behaviour tends to involve relatively wide and stable intervals
- During an approach, the intervals progressively compress
- A feeding buzz produces an extremely dense burst of pulses as the bat attempts to intercept prey

Afterwards, where the recording continues for long enough, the spacing may widen again during an exit phase. Those changes can be represented numerically using measurements such as _PRI_ (Pulse Repetition Interval) and the change in PRI from one pulse to the next**.

And once again, something interesting happens:

- The original audio recording can disappear
- The spectrogram can disappear
- The detailed acoustic measurements can disappear

What remains is a comparatively small sequence of timing values but some of the behaviour is still there.

## Bats in a Calculator

That observation led to another _Pocket Ecology_ experiment.

> Could the TI-84 infer broad behavioural phases using the timing information alone?

It could. The calculator implementation takes PRI and delta-PRI measurements generated from the full desktop workflow and uses them to identify broad regions of:

- SEARCH
- APPROACH
- BUZZ
- EXIT

It can then classify the overall sequence — for example, recognising a sequence containing the characteristic progression into a feeding buzz.

It is deliberately heuristic and I would not mistake it for the full desktop analysis system but that isn't really the point. The interesting part is that after throwing away an enormous amount of information from the original recording, enough structure remains to make a biologically meaningful interpretation.

And, inevitably, I then wondered whether *that* could run on the RC2014.

## It Can

The latest addition to the RC2014 ecology collection is therefore _Bat Behavioural Phase Analysis_. Like the TI-84 version, it does not attempt to process an audio recording. That would be a rather different proposition. Instead, it begins with the PRI and delta-PRI data generated by the full analysis software and works from there.

From those compact timing values, the Z80 identifies behavioural regions and classifies the sequence.

Watching an RC2014 print:

```text
          Phase           Start             End          Length
--------------- --------------- --------------- ---------------
         SEARCH               1              22              22
       APPROACH              23              26               4
           BUZZ              27              33               7
```

followed by:

```text
Classification: FEEDING BUZZ
```

is absurdly satisfying.

- The machine has no idea what a bat is
- It has never heard the recording
- It has never seen the spectrogram

But the numerical structure left behind by the animal's behaviour is sufficient for a small BASIC program to recover something meaningful from it.

## Two Journeys

Putting the two strands together makes the pattern rather clearer.

| Stage                        | Seasonal modelling             | Bat behavioural analysis          |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------ | --------------------------------- |
| **Full analysis**            | MacBook observational analysis | MacBook spectrogram/call analysis |
| **Distilled representation** | fitted parameters + ODE        | PRI + ΔPRI timing data            |
| **Portable implementation**  | TI-84 ODE solver/model         | TI-84 phase inference             |
| **8-bit implementation**     | RC2014 ODE model               | RC2014 phase inference            |

The programs themselves are quite different. One is mathematical modelling while the other is analysis of observational data, but the underlying journey is remarkably similar.

- In both cases, the MacBook is where the rich analysis happens
- The TI-84 asks what can be carried forward once that analysis has been distilled into a much smaller representation
- And the RC2014 asks how far that reduction can be taken

## What Survives?

That has probably become the most interesting part of the exercise for me. It would be easy to regard the RC2014 versions simply as retrocomputing stunts and there is certainly an element of that. Nobody is going to choose a Z80 running BASIC over a modern computer for ecological research because it is the more efficient tool.

But reducing an analysis to the point where it can run on such constrained hardware forces a useful question:

> **What information is actually doing the work?**

For the seasonal models, much of the original observational complexity can ultimately be represented by an equation and a relatively small collection of parameters. For the bat analysis, a surprising amount of behavioural structure survives in pulse timing alone.

The more powerful computer lets me ask the original question and the smaller machines make me decide what parts of the answer really matter.

And there is something rather satisfying about seeing the same ecological idea survive the journey from a modern MacBook, through a pocket calculator, and eventually onto a Z80.

I originally started playing with the RC2014 because old computers are fun.

That remains an entirely sufficient reason.

But somewhere along the way it has become something else as well.

A tiny ecological laboratory ... with an 8-bit processor!
