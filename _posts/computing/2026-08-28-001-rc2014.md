*Or: why a small computer with obvious limitations can still open up a remarkably large space for exploration*

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.rc2014_pc %}

A couple of years ago, I bought and built an RC2014 Mini II: a small computer based on the Z80 microprocessor. I also built its associated digital input/output board, wrote some programs in BASIC and published the results in a GitHub repository.

Then I put it aside.

This wasn't necessarily an intentional decision. There was no dramatic moment when I declared the project complete or concluded that retro-computing no longer interested me. Other things simply occupied my attention and the machine remained on the shelf.

Recently, I picked it up again.

I had forgotten how much pleasure there was in it.

## Not a museum piece

It would be easy to frame this as nostalgia: An older computer user returning fondly to the technology of his youth and lamenting that modern machines are no longer made this way.

That isn't what I mean.

I have no desire to replace my modern computers with a Z80 machine. I use contemporary hardware to write, communicate, analyse data, build models and do things that would have been inconceivable on the computers with which I started.

Modern computing is extraordinary.

The RC2014 is interesting not because the past was better, but because changing the scale of the machine changes my relationship with it.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.rc2014 %}

Here is a computer whose important parts I can see. I assembled it. I can identify the processor, memory and supporting components. Its capabilities are limited enough to be comprehensible and those limitations are part of the attraction.

It brings me closer to the silicon.

## Productive limitations

A modern computer makes an enormous range of things possible. It also hides a great deal of what it is doing.

Layers of hardware, operating systems, frameworks, libraries, services and network infrastructure sit between an idea and its execution. That complexity buys us remarkable capabilities, but it can also make the machine itself feel remote.

The RC2014 presents a much smaller world.

Memory is limited. Processing power is limited. Moving a program onto the machine involves a serial connection and a little patience. The development process is not frictionless and, oddly, that friction adds to the experience.

The constraints make decisions visible. They encourage me to think about what the program actually needs to do and how simply I can express it.

> A productive limitation does not merely make something harder: it makes something important more noticeable

On this machine, I notice the structure of the program. I notice the movement of information. I notice the boundary between writing code, transferring it and running it.

The machine does less, but I see more.

## Programs, not apps

I also find myself reluctant to call the things I write for it “apps”.

> They are programs

This may sound like a purely linguistic distinction, but the words now suggest rather different things to me.

An app is generally expected to remain available, provide an interface, preserve state, receive updates and fit into a larger ecosystem.

These programs are more modest. They start. They perform a task. They finish. Their boundaries are visible. A listing contains the instructions and the machine follows them. What happens between `RUN` and `END` is, at least in principle, understandable.

There is something satisfying about that completeness.

## Making the lights flash

The digital input/output board offers another kind of pleasure.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.led %}

A program changes a value and a physical light responds.

This is hardly at the frontier of computational science. It does not solve a pressing social problem, analyse a genome or produce a sophisticated mathematical model.

It makes the lights flash.

That is sufficient.

The point is not always to produce something useful. Sometimes the purpose of a program is to find out whether an idea works. Sometimes it is to understand the hardware. Sometimes it is simply to make a pattern and enjoy watching it appear.

Learning and exploration are practical purposes of their own.

So is play.

## Building, coding and publishing

The RC2014 gives me an unusually complete relationship with a computer:

- Build the machine
- Write the code
- Run the program
- Observe what happens
- Change something
- Try again
- Publish the result

The publication matters, even if the program itself is small.

Putting the code into a repository turns a private experiment into something another person can inspect, run or adapt. It completes the journey from an idea, through a physical machine, to a shareable result.

The scale may be modest, but the creative loop is complete.

## Continuity without retreat

The RC2014 also reconnects me with my computing roots.

I began with machines on which resources were scarce and programs had to fit within sharply defined limits. Returning to that environment feels familiar, but it is not an attempt to retreat into the past.

I am bringing decades of subsequent experience back to a simpler platform.

The machine belongs to a style of computing I recognise, but I approach it with the knowledge, interests and questions I have now. I know more mathematics, more science and more software than I did when I first encountered a Z80. The interesting possibility is not merely to recreate what I once did, but to discover what I might do differently.

That is continuity rather than repetition.

## A rewarding cognitive state

In an earlier Field Note, I described computers as a way of asking:

> **“What happens if...?”**

The RC2014 is particularly good at inviting that question:

- What happens if I represent the problem this way?
- What happens if I use this output port?
- What happens if I try to fit the idea within these constraints?
- What happens if I make those lights flash in a different sequence?

An idea becomes a specific expression. The expression runs on a machine I assembled. The result is visible and understandable.

That combination produces a deeply rewarding cognitive state: curiosity becomes code and code becomes behaviour.

- It is generative rather than reproductive - I am developing a thought, not merely repeating somebody else's solution
- It is embodied - the activity involves boards, components, cables and switches as well as keys on a keyboard
- It is purposive - even when its purpose is simply to learn, explore and enjoy the process

## The pleasure comes first

There are respectable reasons why this might be a good activity to continue.

It exercises memory and reasoning. It develops skills. It encourages creativity, agency and sustained attention. It provides continuity with an important part of my identity.

All of that may be true.

But there is a simpler reason:

> **Retro-computing makes me happy**

The analysis helps me understand why, but it should not explain the pleasure away.

Not every activity needs to become productive in some external sense. Not every program needs a practical application. Not every flashing LED needs to signify anything more profound than the fact that I made it flash.

The RC2014 is limited, comprehensible, physical and open to exploration.

It turns computing back into something I can hold in my hands.

And, having picked it up again, I am very glad I did.
