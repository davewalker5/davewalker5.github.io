At the end of yesterday's Field Notes post I wrote:

> I suspect the timing will be terrible.
> 
> I suspect the singing will be worse.
>
> I also suspect I'll find it enormously entertaining.

Well. Two out of three isn't bad! The singing is indeed dreadful and I am enormously entertained by it.

But, slightly to my surprise, the timing is actually pretty good.

The RC2014 can now sing **Daisy Bell**.

I use the word *sing* fairly generously.

## Daisy, Daisy...

The idea was simple enough. The RC2014 now has two pieces of hardware that seemed as though they ought to be introduced to one another:

* The SID-Ulator can generate music
* The MG005 speech synthesiser can produce speech

So could I get one to play **Daisy Bell** while the other spoke the words in approximately the right places?

There was only one sensible way to find out. And the answer is:

> Yes.

Very definitely yes.

Whether the result counts as *music* is perhaps a question best left to someone with stronger nerves.

The tune is recognisable. The words are recognisable. More importantly, the two remain sufficiently well synchronised that what comes out of the speaker genuinely sounds like a computer attempting to sing rather than two unrelated pieces of hardware making noises at the same time.

And rather than attempting to describe it any further, here it is.

Which, as far as I'm concerned, makes the experiment a success.

## Not Exactly Dua Lipa

It is not a *good* singer.

The voice is crude, mechanical and gloriously synthetic. The individual phonemes sometimes sound as though they have been persuaded to participate under protest.

The melody is somewhat more confident, but putting the two together produces something that I suspect would make an actual musician wince.

Possibly quite a lot.

Dua Lipa is unlikely to regard the RC2014 as an emerging threat.

And yet I think it is magnificent!

That's partly because making it sound better would, in a strange way, make it less interesting.

A modern computer can play perfectly reproduced music and generate remarkably convincing synthetic speech without any visible effort whatsoever. The machinery disappears.

Here, it doesn't.

- You can hear the limitations
- You can hear the speech synthesiser constructing words from phonemes
- You can hear the SID-Ulator generating the tune
- And you can hear the Z80 trying to keep the whole thing coordinated

The imperfections are part of the evidence that the machine is actually doing something.

## Assembly Required

There are now two versions of the program. The first was written in BASIC and proved the idea. Then came the Assembly version. And that, for me, is where the whole thing becomes particularly satisfying.

There is something wonderfully disproportionate about writing Z80 Assembly simply to make an elderly-style 8-bit computer sing a Victorian popular song extremely badly.

```asm
playback_loop:
    call service_music
    call service_speech

    ld a,(music_done)
    ld b,a
    ld a,(speech_done)
    and b
    jr z,check_timeout

    ld hl,(drain_until)
    ld a,h
    or l
    jr nz,check_drain
    ld hl,(current_tick)
    ld de,DRAIN_TICKS
    add hl,de
    ld (drain_until),hl
```

Every note, pause and spoken fragment has to be coordinated closely enough that the illusion survives. The result might sound crude, but underneath it is a small real-time coordination problem.

The processor has to control two separate output systems and keep them sufficiently aligned that speech and music appear to belong together.

That makes the audible roughness rather misleading. The performance may sound primitive, but the programming isn't.

## And Then There Is C

The Assembly version has another small connection that I hadn't expected when I started the RC2014 project.

> Its tests are written in C

C was one of the first two programming languages I used professionally. The other was FORTRAN. So somewhere underneath this ridiculous little performance is a curious piece of personal computing continuity.

Decades ago I was beginning a professional career writing programs in C and FORTRAN. Now I'm using C to test Assembly code running on a Z80 so that a computer I've built for fun can sing **Daisy Bell** badly.

That feels entirely appropriate. It is difficult to imagine a less commercially useful application of accumulated professional experience.

I mean that as a compliment.

## A Computer Singing Because I Made It

There are much easier ways of producing this result. I could ask almost any modern computer, tablet or phone to play **Daisy Bell** and get something vastly more polished, but that would answer a completely different question.

The question that has increasingly come to define this RC2014 project isn't:

> What can this computer do?

Modern machines win that contest before it begins. The question is:

> **What can I make out of it?**

That distinction matters. The pleasure comes from taking a comparatively simple machine and combining its capabilities in ways that weren't necessarily the reason any individual piece of hardware was created.

- A sound card is one thing
- A speech synthesiser is another
- A Z80 coordinating both so that the machine performs a song is something else again

Not because the end result is impressive by modern standards but because I made the machine do it.

## The Right Kind of Awful

There's another reason I like the result so much.

> It sounds exactly like a computer

Not an imitation of a human voice generated by a computer sufficiently powerful to hide the fact that it is a computer.

A computer.

There is something very characteristic of early speech synthesis in that strange combination of intelligibility and obvious artificiality. You understand the words, but there is never the slightest possibility of forgetting how they are being produced.

And **Daisy Bell** is almost absurdly appropriate for that.

Its association with early computer speech makes it one of those pieces of music that seems to belong to computing history as much as musical history. So hearing it emerge from an RC2014 — melody from one piece of hardware, phonemes from another, all marshalled by a Z80 — feels like a tiny connection to that earlier period of experimentation.

Not a recreation of it, more of an echo.

A rather badly sung echo.

## Hardware Complete. Experiments Beginning.

Yesterday I described this RC2014 as effectively hardware complete and that still feels right. In fact, this experiment confirms why I wanted to stop adding things.

The interesting part now isn't acquiring another board - it's seeing what happens when the existing boards begin interacting.

- The SID-Ulator can make music
- The MG005 can speak

Together, apparently, they can sing ... well, sort of!

- The Digital I/O board adds buttons and lights
- The character display adds another form of output

There is now enough hardware in the machine that combinations become more interesting than individual capabilities. And that's exactly where I wanted to get to.

## Magnificent

I have listened to the Assembly version several times now.

Objectively, it is awful.

Subjectively, I think it is magnificent.

Those two statements are not contradictory.

If I wanted good music, I have plenty of devices capable of producing it. What I wanted from this machine was something else: something visible, understandable, programmable and limited enough that making it do something unexpected still feels like an achievement.

The RC2014 is now singing **Daisy Bell**.

Badly.

In Assembly.

With tests written in C.

And somewhere in all of that is a connection between the computers that first fascinated me, the languages with which I began my professional career, and the computer I'm playing with now simply because doing so makes me happy.

I don't think it's going to win any Grammys.

But that's really not the point.
