*Or: how writing free software for a 32K Z80 computer somehow involves thinking about intellectual property lawyers*

I've recently been expanding my collection of programs for the RC2014.

They are small programs for a small computer: games, mathematical experiments, demonstrations and programs that interact with the digital input/output board. They are also free and open source, published under the MIT licence on GitHub.

This is, in other words, about as far from a commercial software enterprise as it is possible to get.

And yet I have found myself worrying about trademarks and copyright.

**_This is bonkers_**

## Reimplementing ideas

One of the pleasures of retro-computing is revisiting ideas from the early years of personal computing. Games are an obvious example. So are mathematical recreations, simulations and demonstrations. This was part of how many of us learned to program.

- You encountered an idea
- You wondered how it worked
- You wrote your own version

Sometimes you had seen another implementation. Sometimes you had read a description in a book or magazine. Sometimes somebody simply explained the idea.

The interesting part was turning the idea into code.

That remains the interesting part.

I'm not trying to preserve old software as museum exhibits. Nor am I trying to reproduce somebody else's implementation line by line. I'm interested in taking ideas from computing history and asking how I would implement them on this machine.

The irony is that I was doing exactly that **_when much of this history was still current._**

## Then and now

There is therefore something wonderfully absurd about the difference between programming then and programming now:

> **Me, circa 1981:**  
> *"Oh, that's neat. I'll write my own version."*

Compare that with:

> **Me, 2026:**  
> *"Oh, that's neat. I'll write my own version. But first, let me investigate the current ownership history of the name, distinguish trademark from copyright, check whether the original implementation is still commercially exploited, avoid protected audiovisual expression, document provenance, choose a sufficiently generic title, and make sure my README doesn't imply affiliation."*

Meanwhile:

> **Z80:**  
> *"Mate, I've only got 32K."*

Quite.

And, for the record, that 32K lives in a 62256 SRAM chip.

The computer is considerably less complicated than the legal considerations surrounding some of the programs running on it!

## Ideas, expression and names

There are sensible principles behind intellectual property law.

An idea for a game or algorithm is not generally the same thing as the particular code, graphics, sounds or other creative expression used to implement it. Trademark law, meanwhile, is concerned with names and identifiers rather than ownership of the underlying programming idea.

Those distinctions matter.

They are also why I'm perfectly happy to write my own implementation of an old idea while being much more cautious about copying original code, distinctive presentation or potentially protected names.

That seems reasonable.

What feels considerably less reasonable is how far the caution can sometimes have to extend.

A program can have entirely new source code, written by me for completely different hardware, distributed without charge as part of an open-source hobby project, and still leave me wondering whether the historically obvious name is worth using.

The practical answer is often simple:

- Don't use it
- Choose another name
- Add an acknowledgement where appropriate
- Make the independence of the implementation clear
- Carry on programming

None of those things is particularly onerous.

It is the fact that I need to think about them at all that feels absurd.

## A collision of cultures

Part of the problem, I think, is a collision between two very different computing cultures.

Early personal computing encouraged experimentation and reimplementation.

Programs circulated as listings in books and magazines. People typed them in, changed them, broke them, fixed them and wrote their own versions. Familiar ideas were implemented repeatedly because implementing them was itself part of learning how to program.

The boundaries between education, experimentation and play were wonderfully porous.

Modern intellectual property law comes from a different world: one of commercial products, valuable brands, corporate ownership and software capable of generating substantial revenue.

Those protections have legitimate purposes.

But apply that world retrospectively to the informal programming culture of the 1970s and 1980s and some strange situations emerge.

I can find myself being cautious about the title of a tiny program running on a processor designed in the 1970s, on a computer I assembled myself, containing 32K of RAM, whose entire purpose is experimentation, and whose source code I am giving away.

> **The legal framework has become vastly more complicated. The computer hasn't.**

There is something beautifully ridiculous about that.

## Close to the culture

In the previous note in this series I wrote about how the RC2014 brings me *close to the silicon*.

Perhaps this project is also bringing me close to something else: It reconnects me with a particular culture of computing. 

Not because everything about that period was better. It wasn't.

Not because intellectual property should cease to exist. It shouldn't.

But because there was an extraordinarily productive attitude embedded in early personal computing:

> **I wonder how that works. I'll try making one.**

That sentence contains curiosity, learning, experimentation and creativity and it is still one of the things I value most about programming.

When I recreate an old computing idea on the RC2014, the result isn't interesting because I have reproduced a historical artefact perfectly. Rather, it is interesting because I have understood the idea well enough to make my own version work.

That is exactly what I might have done forty-five or more years ago.

The difference is that the younger me probably didn't spend quite so much time wondering what to call it.

## Carry on regardless

So I will continue being cautious.

More importantly, I will continue giving credit where it is due.

That isn't something being reluctantly forced upon me by intellectual property law. It is something I *want* to do. If somebody originated an idea, designed a game or produced an implementation that inspired mine, acknowledging that contribution is part of documenting the history of the program. The fact that I have written my own implementation does not make its intellectual ancestry irrelevant.

There is an important distinction here.

> **Attribution is part of the project. Legal caution is part of protecting it.**

I want to credit the originators of ideas because that seems the right thing to do. I need to be careful about copyright, trademarks and potentially confusing names because publishing software — even free, open-source hobbyist software — carries responsibilities of its own.

Those two motivations happen to point in much the same direction, but they aren't the same thing.

So I will avoid names where there seems any meaningful possibility of confusion. I won't copy somebody else's source code or distinctive creative material. I'll document inspirations and provenance wherever that seems useful.

Those are reasonable habits anyway.

But I reserve the right to find some of the legal gymnastics surrounding them deeply amusing.

Somewhere underneath all the legal history, corporate ownership, trademarks and copyright notices is a Z80 patiently executing instructions one after another.

- It neither knows nor cares what the program is called
- It has 32K
- It has work to do

And I intend to keep giving it some.
