*Or: from a ZX80 with 1 KB to an iPhone with 64 GB, and somehow we're still short of memory.*

I started my computing career with a Sinclair ZX80. It had **1 KB of memory**. Not one megabyte. Not one gigabyte. One *kilobyte*.

This may be relevant to what follows, because spending your formative computing years regarding individual bytes as a precious natural resource leaves you with a lifelong tendency to become slightly agitated when software starts consuming gigabytes as though nobody is paying for them.

Which brings me to my iPhone.

## A perfectly good telephone

I have an iPhone 12 with 64 GB of storage. It is, by contemporary standards, an oldish phone but it is also a perfectly good phone.

The battery works. The screen works. The camera does everything I require of it. It makes calls, receives messages, browses the web and runs the applications I use. I have no compelling reason to replace it.

It also worked perfectly well for my purposes with iOS 18.

Then came iOS 26.

For clarity, this isn't going to be an argument that iOS 26 is objectively terrible, that nobody should use it, or that Apple has somehow forgotten how to write software. Other people may love it. They may find its new features indispensable.

I don't.

Hand on heart, I cannot identify a **single new feature in iOS 26 that is either better suited to my needs than its predecessor or provides a new capability that I personally find useful**.

Not one.

This is important, because it establishes the benefit side of the subsequent cost-benefit analysis: It is zero.

## Some storage would be nice

Eventually my iPhone announced that an update was available. Excellent. I take security updates seriously and want my devices kept current. There was, however, a minor problem. The update required approximately **12 GB of available storage** and apparently I didn't have enough.

Fair enough. Time for a little housekeeping. I removed a few applications. Then a few more. Then rather a lot more.

> **52.89 GB of 64 GB used**

In other words, I had approximately **11.11 GB free**. The operating-system update wanted 12 GB. You may already have spotted the direction in which this story is heading.

## No

The phone still didn't have sufficient space to install the update.

At this point I began to feel that deleting Compass might not be the breakthrough intervention the situation required.

There is, fortunately, another supported method: update the iPhone using Finder on a Mac. Excellent.

I own a MacBook Air. It runs macOS Tahoe, which, incidentally, **I rather like**. This isn't an objection to new Apple operating systems, new interfaces or change in general. Tahoe has the usual occasional glitches of relatively new software, but on the whole I've been very happy with it.

So: connect iPhone to MacBook. Open Finder. Select iPhone. Check for update. And Finder announced:

> Downloading iPhone software update ... x MB of **10.48 GB**

Ten point four eight gigabytes.

Curiously, this was actually less alarming than the message I'd received on the phone itself. The iPhone had told me that it wanted roughly 12 GB of free space before it would install the update. Somewhere in the same message it also mentioned 4.47 GB — exactly what that number represented escaped me, possibly because by this point my attention had become firmly fixed on the words _TWELVE GIGABYTES_.

I had eventually managed to get the phone to 11.11 GB free. Close. But apparently no cigar.

There are perfectly sensible technical reasons why an operating-system update needs temporary working space in addition to the size of the software being installed. Files have to be downloaded, unpacked, staged, verified and old components replaced.

I understand this but I started with a ZX80. It had 1 KB. My sympathy therefore has limits.

## Perhaps I should make a backup

Before allowing a computer to perform major surgery on another computer, I have a quaint habit of making sure I have a backup.

The iPhone can be backed up through Finder.

Unfortunately, I wasn't particularly enthusiastic about dumping a potentially substantial phone backup onto the internal SSD of my MacBook.

No problem. I have a **1 TB external drive**.

There is no Finder button labelled:

> **Backup location...**

That would have made this section disappointingly short.

Instead, iPhone backups are stored in a particular directory beneath the user's Library. A standard Unix solution is therefore to move the backup directory to the external drive and replace it with a symbolic link.

Easy.

Except that I'd apparently done this before.

And the existing symbolic link was wrong.

And my everyday macOS account is deliberately **not an administrator**.

What followed is worth recording in full.

## How to back up a telephone in 2026

1. Take the MacBook off the network
2. Log out of my normal account
3. Log into the administrator account
4. Make my normal account an administrator
5. Reboot the MacBook
6. Log into my now-temporarily-administrative normal account
7. Sort out the symbolic-link mess
8. Log out
9. Log back into the administrator account
10. Make my normal account a non-administrator again
11. Reboot the MacBook
12. Log back into my normal account
13. Connect the 1 TB external drive
14. Finally start backing up the telephone

Remember the ZX80?

> **1 KB.**

## But it's an old phone

There is an obvious response to all this.

The iPhone 12 was released in 2020. Perhaps the hardware is simply getting old. Technology moves on. Operating systems become more capable. Eventually old hardware has to be left behind. I have absolutely no objection to that argument. In fact, I think I would have been considerably happier if Apple had said, in effect:

> Your iPhone 12 has reached the end of the road for major operating-system upgrades. We'll continue providing appropriate security fixes for iOS 18, but iOS 26 requires newer hardware

Fair enough.

The problem is that **Apple offers iOS 26 for the iPhone 12**. That seems to me to make it reasonable to judge the experience of running and maintaining iOS 26 on an iPhone 12.

More specifically, mine is the **64 GB configuration** — a configuration Apple itself sold.

Perhaps the processor is perfectly capable of running iOS 26 while 64 GB of storage is becoming increasingly uncomfortable. If so, that is an interesting distinction. "Supported hardware" is not necessarily the same thing as "pleasantly usable hardware".

But from the user's side, the distinction is rather academic.

- My phone worked
- It still works
- Its hardware still meets my needs

The difficulty is finding enough room to maintain the operating system that officially supports it.

## The security paradox

This is the part I find genuinely troubling.

I **want** to install the update.

Not because there is some exciting new feature I'm desperate to have — we've established the score on that front — but because I want a supported, patched and secure phone.

I completely understand why Apple doesn't want users casually downgrading iPhones to obsolete operating systems with known vulnerabilities. There are excellent security arguments for keeping devices current.

But an update process can become sufficiently burdensome that it creates exactly the behaviour it is intended to prevent.

The desirable sequence is:

> **Security update available → easy update → user installs it → secure phone**

My experience has been closer to:

> **Security update available → insufficient space → delete applications → delete data → still insufficient space → connect to Mac → download 10.48 GB software image → arrange backup → discover broken symbolic link → privilege-changing reboot extravaganza → external 1 TB drive → eventually attempt update**

There comes a point at which an entirely rational user says:

> **"Oh, sod it."**

And doesn't update. The result is a *less* secure phone.

That strikes me as a poor outcome for a system whose restrictions are, at least in significant part, intended to keep users secure.

## How to drive people to Android

I've joked during this process that this is an excellent way to drive people onto Android.

I don't entirely mean it as a joke.

I'm not suddenly claiming that Android has no software bloat, upgrade problems, security compromises or irritating design decisions. That would be a rather different article.

Nor am I particularly desperate to abandon the Apple ecosystem. As I said earlier, I actively like Tahoe on my MacBook Air.

But eventually I'll replace this iPhone and when I do, the question won't merely be:

> **"Do I prefer iOS or Android?"**

It will also be:

> **"Do I want another phone whose manufacturer can render a perfectly serviceable device increasingly inconvenient to maintain through software growth?"**

That experience affects purchasing decisions.

And being told that the solution is effectively to buy a newer iPhone with more storage would not necessarily improve my mood.

## Progress

None of this is to pretend that a ZX80 and an iPhone are remotely comparable machines.

The ZX80 could not make telephone calls, take photographs, navigate by satellite, perform biometric authentication, stream video, communicate securely across the planet or do thousands of other things that I now take completely for granted.

Modern operating systems are staggeringly sophisticated pieces of engineering. But perhaps my ZX80 upbringing left me with one useful prejudice:

> **Complexity should have to justify its cost**

For many people, iOS 26 presumably does. For me, so far, the equation looks rather different.

I have gained no new capability that I personally value and in return, I have deleted more than forty applications, cleared personal data, connected my phone to a MacBook, downloaded a 10.48 GB software package, attached a 1 TB external drive, temporarily altered account privileges and rebooted the computer several times.

All so that I can continue securely using a telephone that was doing everything I wanted before I started.

Somewhere in my computing past sits a Sinclair ZX80 with its **1 KB of memory**.

Nearly half a century later, I am sitting beside a **64 GB telephone connected to a MacBook and a 1 TB external drive**, trying to find enough storage to update its operating system.

Progress is a funny old thing.

**Postscript**: While writing this, the update finally completed. The iPhone remains exactly as useful as it was before I started. The ZX80 remains unimpressed.
