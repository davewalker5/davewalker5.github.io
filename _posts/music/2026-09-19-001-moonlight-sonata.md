# On Passing, and Not Going Gentle

I've been listening to Beethoven's *Piano Sonata No. 14*, the so-called *Moonlight Sonata*.

More specifically, the first movement, *Adagio sostenuto*.

It gets me every time.

There is something about that beautiful, restrained, almost hypnotic music that I find profoundly moving. Increasingly, I think it has something to do with age and an awareness of mortality — my own, and that of the people I love.

It feels like a passing.

Not necessarily a particular death or farewell, but a broader awareness of time passing, of people and experiences that cannot last for ever, and of the inevitability of change and loss.

And yet, curiously, the music feels peaceful.

There is sadness in it, certainly, but also extraordinary beauty and tenderness. It seems possible to contemplate the impermanence of things without being overwhelmed by it.

Well, that's the theory.

In practice, Beethoven can sustain that emotional intensity considerably better than I can. There are occasions when the *Adagio sostenuto* leaves me struggling to maintain my composure.

Which makes what happened next rather amusing.

Having recovered myself, my first thought was of Dylan Thomas:

> Do not go gentle into that good night,<br/>
> Rage, rage against the dying of the light.

Quite an about-face.

One moment I'm contemplating mortality with Beethoven's beautiful, peaceful acceptance. The next, I'm apparently declaring that I'm not going anywhere without a fight.

But on reflection, I'm not sure the two responses are contradictory.

There is a difference between accepting that life is finite and being indifferent to its ending. I can recognise the beauty and poignancy of passing time while remaining thoroughly enthusiastic about the time I have.

Perhaps the awareness of mortality makes life more precious rather than less.

And perhaps that's part of what I find so moving about the music.

The *Adagio sostenuto* doesn't make me want to give up. It reminds me how much there is to value.

Dylan Thomas simply supplies the appropriate response once I've recovered my composure.

I can contemplate the passing of things.

But I'm damned if I'm going gentle.
