A few weeks ago, I met my wife in Reading after work. We had a meal and afterwards stopped for a drink at the stretch by the River Kennet known as “The Beach”.

The bar was playing *Perfect* by Fairground Attraction.

I have always loved *Perfect*. But somewhere in the intervening years I had more or less forgotten about it. I hadn't consciously decided that I no longer wanted to hear it; it had simply slipped out of the music I regularly listened to.

And then there it was.

> *It’s got to be-e-e-e-e-e perfect...*

And, inevitably, it stayed in my head.

There are worse things to have stuck there.

## Rediscovering a song

One of the pleasures of music is discovering something new. Another, rather different pleasure, is unexpectedly rediscovering something you already knew.

There's a curious combination of familiarity and novelty involved. I knew *Perfect*. I remembered the melody and the chorus. Hearing it again wasn't accompanied by any revelation that I'd previously underestimated the song. My reaction was much simpler:

> *Oh yes. I love this.*

But hearing a familiar song after a long absence can also mean hearing it with different ears.

I've listened to a great deal of music since I first encountered Fairground Attraction. Jazz in particular has become an important part of my listening, including singers such as Julie London and Billie Holiday and the wonderfully understated singing of Chet Baker. I've become increasingly interested in small ensembles, relatively sparse arrangements and singers whose personality comes through not through vocal pyrotechnics but through phrasing.

Listening to *Perfect* again, I suddenly heard some of those same qualities.

Which wasn't something I had expected from a British number-one pop single released in 1988.

## A record that doesn't sound very much like 1988

There is, of course, no single sound of 1988. But popular records of the period provide plenty of familiar production signatures: synthesisers, electronic or heavily processed drums, huge reverberant snares, electric basses, layers of production and vocals treated to sound suitably enormous.

Then Fairground Attraction turn up.

*Perfect* sounds almost as though it has wandered in from somewhere else entirely.

The arrangement is light and predominantly acoustic. The rhythm bounces rather than pounds. There's space between the instruments. Nothing is fighting to sound enormous.

And sitting in the middle of it all is Eddi Reader's voice.

That's what caught me again beside the Kennet, and it's what has kept bringing me back to the song since.

Reader doesn't sing *Perfect* as though she's demonstrating what a remarkable singer she is. The performance is much more interesting than that. She plays with the melody.

The obvious example is the song's irresistible hook, the elongated *“be-e-e-e-e-e”* leading into *“perfect”*. Written down, it looks faintly ridiculous. Sung by Reader, it is glorious.

She bends and stretches vowels, swoops between notes and changes the shape of phrases as she sings them. There's something playful about it. It almost sounds spontaneous, as though she's enjoying discovering what she can do with the melody while she's singing it.

Of course, that apparent effortlessness is itself part of the skill.

## Why does it stick?

That also goes some way towards explaining why *Perfect* is such an effective earworm.

The chorus contains a little musical journey that the brain seems determined to complete once it has started. The word *perfect* isn't merely part of the lyric: the whole phrase leading into it has become a recognisable musical object.

Think of the words and you hear the melody.

Hear the first part of the melody and you want the resolution.

And because the arrangement is so light, it isn't an exhausting song to have circulating in your head. It skips along happily.

But in my case there is now something else attached to it.

Music has a remarkable ability to acquire associations with places and events. *Perfect* now means not only whatever memories I already had of the song, but a particular evening in Reading: finishing work, meeting my wife, having dinner, sitting with a drink beside the Kennet and unexpectedly hearing Eddi Reader singing a song I'd forgotten how much I liked.

An old song has acquired a new memory.

Perhaps that's one reason it refused to go away afterwards.

## Fairground Attraction and jazz?

There was only one sensible response to all this.

I bought *The First of a Million Kisses* on vinyl.

And listening beyond *Perfect* produced another surprise. The album sits remarkably comfortably alongside quite a lot of the music I've been listening to recently.

I wouldn't describe Fairground Attraction as a jazz band, and I wouldn't classify *The First of a Million Kisses* as a jazz album. “Acoustic pop” or “folk-pop” probably gets us closer, with elements of folk, skiffle, country, swing and jazz.

But genre isn't quite the interesting thing here.

The interesting thing is the shared aesthetic.

Much of the jazz I enjoy most involves small ensembles and plenty of space. I've developed a particular fondness for singers such as Julie London, Billie Holiday and Chet Baker.

Those are three very different voices. Eddi Reader doesn't particularly sound like any of them either.

What connects them for me is phrasing.

With all of them, the written melody only tells you part of what you're going to hear.

Billie Holiday can move around a melody until it seems inseparable from her particular way of singing it. Julie London can do an extraordinary amount with a breath, a delayed consonant or a phrase placed fractionally behind the beat. Chet Baker's voice can sound almost fragile and technically limited, yet the lack of theatricality is precisely what makes his singing so compelling.

Eddi Reader has a brighter, folkier and much more exuberant voice, but I hear something similar happening.

The accompaniment establishes the song. Reader inhabits it.

She can linger on a syllable, rush another, slide into a note or reshape a vowel. The singer doesn't seem mechanically attached to the beat. There is the feeling of a human being making decisions inside the song.

And that, I think, is what I find captivating about all of these singers.

## The person in the recording

Perhaps that's also a useful way of describing an aspect of my musical taste that I hadn't consciously identified before.

Technical virtuosity can be astonishing, but it isn't necessarily what draws me into a vocal performance.

I like hearing the person.

Julie London doesn't need to overwhelm a song. Chet Baker certainly doesn't. Billie Holiday's greatness isn't reducible to conventional ideas about possessing a beautiful voice. What makes those recordings extraordinary is that the personality of the singer seems present in every phrase.

I'm beginning to think Eddi Reader scratches some of the same musical itch.

That doesn't make *Perfect* jazz. It doesn't need to.

It suggests instead that genre labels sometimes obscure connections that become obvious when we simply listen.

An acoustic pop record from 1988 can sit quite happily in the same listening session as recordings made by jazz musicians decades earlier because they share something more fundamental: melodic songs, relatively sparse arrangements, musicians listening to one another, and a singer given enough room to make a song her own.

Perhaps that also explains why *The First of a Million Kisses* has aged so well.

It wasn't completely tied to the prevailing production vocabulary of its time in the first place.

## Keeping this one

Streaming has made rediscovery almost effortless. A song appears somewhere, I remember it, and seconds later I can be listening to it again.

That's wonderful.

But I still like the point at which a piece of music crosses an invisible boundary from *something I can listen to* to *something I want to keep*.

Hence the copy of *The First of a Million Kisses* now making its way to me from Discogs.

What started with background music in a bar has therefore become something rather more enjoyable: the rediscovery of a song I've apparently loved for decades, the discovery of an album I had never properly explored, and an unexpected connection with other music that has become important to me.

And *Perfect* is still going around in my head.

*It’s got to be-e-e-e-e-e perfect...*

I'm quite happy for it to stay there.
