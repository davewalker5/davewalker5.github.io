After spending part of the morning at Liverpool's World Museum enjoying displays about the inhabitants of the Silurian seas some 420 million years ago, followed by an exploration of the rather more recent history of ancient Egyptian civilisation, my next destination within the museum brought me back to the natural world, to the modern marine environment.

Given that it was only a few weeks since I'd returned from the Maldives, having spent considerable time observing marine wildlife and subsequently rather more time examining underwater footage, identifying species and recording my observations, the aquarium was of particular personal interest and, as with the preceding two galleries, I took notes using my Kindle Scribe, making the most of the opportunity to consider those animals in a wider context.

## The oceans as a connected system

Both the palaeontology and Egyptology displays had lent themselves to the construction of timelines, reflecting the importance of chronology in understanding evolutionary and human history but, in contrast, the aquarium prompted me to construct something more akin to a concept map with the ocean at its centre and branches extending towards its physical characteristics, the habitats it encompasses, the organisms that inhabit it and the ecological relationships that exist between them.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.ocean_notes %}

The five conventionally recognised oceans — Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern and Arctic — are geographical divisions of a continuous global body of salt water that covers approximately 71% of Earth's surface and contains around 97% of its water.

Although continuous, that body is not _uniform_ and temperature, salinity, pressure, light and the availability of nutrients vary enormously, both geographically and with depth. Ocean currents redistribute heat, nutrients and dissolved substances, while the movement of animals also interconnects apparently separate environments. As an example, the tropical waters surrounding Moofushi represent just one part of this system but even within that comparatively small geographical area, the shallow lagoon, the coral reef and the water beyond the reef edge offer quite different conditions for marine life.

My museum notes included fish, jellyfish, corals, mangroves and deep-sea habitats but rather than simply representing a collection of different organisms and environments, these subjects offered several ways of exploring how life responds to the physical conditions of the ocean and how living organisms can themselves modify those conditions.

## The Abyssal Zone and Deep-Sea Environments

The contrast between a shallow tropical reef and the abyssal regions of the ocean could hardly be greater. The abyssal zone generally extends from around 4,000 to 6,000 metres below the surface, well out of the reach of sunlight, where temperatures are generally low and the pressure exerted by the water above is immense.

For many organisms inhabiting these depths, food originates in the upper layers of the ocean that do receive sunlight, reaching the seabed in the form of sinking organic material sometimes described as _marine snow_, an interesting ecological connection between environments separated vertically by several kilometres of water, with biological activity at the surface sustaining communities far beyond the reach of sunlight.

My notes also referenced hydrothermal vents, of particular interest because, in a process called _chemosynthesis_, microorganisms can use energy from chemical reactions involving substances such as hydrogen sulphide to produce organic matter, supporting entire local communities, including animals that obtain nutrition through associations with chemosynthetic bacteria. Although many vent animals still depend indirectly on oxygen produced through photosynthesis elsewhere, the primary production at the vents need not be powered by sunlight.

The brightly illuminated reefs I had been snorkelling over only a few weeks earlier operate on very different principles, yet both environments belong to the same ocean, and both demonstrate the extraordinary variety of ways in which life can exploit available sources of energy.

## Mangroves and coastal ecosystems

Mangroves occupy a particularly interesting position at the interface between land and sea. These salt-tolerant trees and shrubs inhabit tropical and subtropical coastlines, where tidal fluctuations expose them to changing water levels and, often, changing salinity.

Their root systems create complex underwater habitats, offering shelter and feeding opportunities for fish, crustaceans and other organisms, and for some fish species mangrove habitats provide nursery areas from which individuals may subsequently move into seagrass beds or onto coral reefs.

The importance of a mangrove forest therefore extends beyond the organisms permanently resident within it. Its loss can have implications for ecological communities elsewhere along the coast.

Mangroves also influence the physical environment. Their roots can trap sediment and reduce the energy of waves, helping to stabilise shorelines. They can accumulate substantial quantities of carbon, particularly in their waterlogged sediments, making them important examples of coastal blue-carbon ecosystems.

## The Archerfish — Feeding at the Surface

One of the fish I filmed in the aquarium is the _Banded Archerfish, Toxotes jaculatrix_. Its principal habitat is the brackish waters of mangrove estuaries across the Indo-Pacific and it's well known for a rather extraordinary method of obtaining food. By forming a narrow channel using its tongue and the roof of its mouth it can project a jet of water at insects resting on vegetation above the surface, dislodging them so they fall into the water.

{% include fullwidth-movie.html assets=page.movies img=page.banded_archerfish %}

This is quite some feat, since a successful strike requires the fish to account for the apparent displacement of its target caused by refraction at the water's surface.

I wasn't able to record that behaviour in the aquarium, but the species provides a great illustration of an ecological relationship spanning two environments with an insect on a branch above the water becoming part of the diet of a fish swimming beneath it.

The water's surface is a very obvious physical boundary. Ecologically, however, it is rather less definitive.

For further information, see the [Australian Museum's account of the banded archerfish](https://australian.museum/learn/animals/fishes/banded-archerfish-toxotes-jaculatrix/) and [FishBase](https://www.fishbase.se/summary/Toxotes-jaculatrix.html).

## Fish, Jellyfish and Corals

Fish encompass an extraordinary diversity of evolutionary forms and ecological roles, with the evolutionary lineages leading to cartilaginous and bony fish diverging more than 400 million years ago, a timescale that takes us back to the geological timescales I'd been contemplating earlier that morning, in the palaeontology gallery.

The cartilaginous fishes, collectively known as _Chondrichthyes_, encompass two principal living evolutionary lineages,  the _Holocephali_, represented by the chimaeras or ratfish, and the _Elasmobranchii_, encompassing sharks, rays and skates. Their skeletons are composed predominantly of cartilage and, unlike many bony fishes, they lack a swim bladder.

The diversity within the elasmobranchs was brought home to us during our recent visit to the Maldives, where we encountered animals ranging from the streamlined blacktip reef sharks and bottom-dwelling tawny nurse sharks to the extraordinary wing-like forms of the reef manta rays.

The bony fishes, or _Osteichthyes_, encompass two principal evolutionary lineages, the ray-finned fishes or _Actinopterygii_, to which the overwhelming majority of living fish species belong, and the lobe-finned fishes, the _Sarcopterygii_. I'd already encountered a member of the latter in the form of the coelacanth, _Latimeria chalumnae_, in the palaeontology gallery earlier in the visit.

The paired fins of lobe-finned fishes are characterised by muscular lobes containing an internal arrangement of bones, some of which are homologous with the bones found in the limbs of terrestrial vertebrates. This arrangement is of considerable evolutionary significance, as the modification of paired fins into limbs capable of supporting the body's weight was one of the major transitions in vertebrate history, ultimately enabling the colonisation of the land by vertebrate animals. The history of life in the oceans is part of our own evolutionary history and, in evolutionary terms, we ourselves belong to the lobe-finned vertebrate lineage, although we have long since lost anything recognisable as a fin!

Jellyfish offer a very different example of marine adaptation, with bodies composed predominantly of water, and an apparently simple anatomy lacking many of the structures familiar from vertebrate animals. Yet their stinging cells, sensory structures and ability to move through the water enable them to function as effective predators and their ancestry extends deep into the history of animal life.

Corals belong to the same phylum, the _Cnidaria_, as jellyfish, despite their very different appearance and way of life. The individual coral polyp is an animal, and in many reef-building species it lives in association with photosynthetic microorganisms that supply much of its energy. The calcium carbonate skeletons produced by successive generations of polyps contribute to the formation of the reef itself and the consequence is a habitat whose physical structure is largely a product of biological activity. Its crevices, branches and cavities create opportunities for organisms with very different requirements.

Although coral reefs occupy only a small proportion of the ocean floor, less than 1%, they support a remarkably rich diversity of marine life, approximately 25% of marine animal species.

## Pyjama Cardinalfish — Life on the Reef

The second fish I filmed was the wonderfully named _Pyjama Cardinalfish, Sphaeramia nematoptera_.

{% include fullwidth-movie.html assets=page.movies img=page.pyjama_cardinalfish %}

This small reef fish occurs in the western Pacific portion of the Indo-Pacific region and can be found in sheltered lagoons and bays, gathering among branching corals during the day before dispersing to feed at night.

Cardinalfishes are interesting for their reproductive biology and in this species the male incubates the developing eggs within his mouth, a form of parental care known as _paternal mouthbrooding_, in which the mouth provides a protected environment for the developing young.

For further information, see [FishBase — _Sphaeramia nematoptera_](https://www.fishbase.se/summary/Sphaeramia-nematoptera.html).

## An Interlude

After several hundred million years of geological history and a few thousand years of ancient Egyptian civilisation, it seemed sensible to return briefly to the present day.

Lunch at _All Bar One_ in the form of a chicken Caesar salad was the ideal opportunity, accompanied by what appeared to be an unusually generous sedimentary deposit of Parmesan cheese with further excavation revealing evidence of lettuce and, eventually, chicken!

Suitably fortified, it was time to return to the museum for further exploration of the universe.

Some things, after all, are best contemplated on a full stomach.
