Following an exploration of the palaeontology displays at Liverpool's World Museum, spending part of the morning contemplating marine organisms that inhabited the Earth more than 400 million years ago, the Ancient Egypt galleries provided a dramatic change of subject and of timescale as I was now considering the development of a civilisation whose history could be measured in thousands, rather than millions, of years.

That seems comparatively recent in the context of geological time but, in the context of human history, ancient Egypt represents an extraordinarily long-lived civilisation. One of the things that interested me was the extent to which what we tend to regard collectively as ancient Egypt encompasses very different periods of history, with considerable changes in political authority, religious belief and economic life.

As with the palaeontology displays, I took notes using my Kindle Scribe and, once again, found a connection between the exhibits and a software project I'd undertaken some years earlier.

## A Chronology of Egyptian Civilisation

I constructed a timeline in my notes, beginning with early agricultural settlements in the Nile Valley and continuing through the principal periods of Egyptian history. I'm familiar with many of the individual names and events associated with ancient Egypt but placing them in chronological order provides an interesting perspective, particularly on the intervals of time separating them.

The timeline included the emergence of settled communities, early burial practices, the development of royal authority, dynastic unification, pyramid construction, periods of political fragmentation and reunification, and eventual foreign rule.

### Development of the Egyptian State

During the fifth and fourth millennia BCE, in the period we refer to as _Predynastic_, agricultural communities developed along the Nile. Amongst the evidence for the lives of these communities are their burial practices, including the placement of pottery and provisions alongside the dead. Such objects are particularly interesting because they provide evidence not simply of material culture but, potentially, of beliefs concerning death and an afterlife, although the precise nature of those beliefs can be difficult to reconstruct in the absence of written records.

The late Predynastic and Early Dynastic periods saw the consolidation of political authority and the emergence of the Egyptian state with the conventional date for the unification of Upper and Lower Egypt being around 3100 BCE, though its development may have been a process rather than a single event. Representations of rulers and royal regalia, including the crowns associated with Upper and Lower Egypt, provide evidence of the development and expression of royal authority.

### The Old Kingdom and the Pyramids

The Old Kingdom is perhaps the period most readily associated with popular images of ancient Egypt, encompassing the construction of the Step Pyramid associated with Djoser at Saqqara and, subsequently, the great pyramid complexes at Giza.

The pyramids are interesting not simply as architectural achievements but for what their construction implies about Egyptian society. The quarrying, transport and assembly of enormous quantities of stone required the organisation of labour and resources on a considerable scale, and the monuments themselves reflect the relationship between royal authority and religious belief.

The displays also touched upon mummification, the preservation of the body and the provision of objects for the dead forming part of a much wider set of beliefs and practices concerning the afterlife, practices that themselves changed over the course of Egyptian history.

### Fragmentation and Reunification

That history was far from an uninterrupted period of political stability and the conventionally named First, Second and Third _Intermediate Periods_ involved differing forms of political fragmentation, competing centres of authority and, in some instances, the rule of dynasties of foreign origin.

The Middle and New Kingdoms, meanwhile, saw renewed political consolidation, monumental construction and changes in Egypt's relations with its neighbours. The division of Egyptian history into kingdoms and intermediate periods provides a useful framework for understanding these changes but doesn't imply that Egyptian cultural life simply ceased during periods of political fragmentation.

One particularly interesting episode is the reign of Akhenaten during the New Kingdom. His promotion of the Aten, represented by the solar disc, and the establishment of a new royal capital at Amarna marked a departure from established religious and political practices and the subsequent restoration of traditional cults during the reign of Tutankhamun, accompanied by the abandonment of Amarna, demonstrates how rapidly some of those changes could be reversed.

### Egypt Under Foreign Rule

The later part of the chronology included Persian rule, Alexander the Great's conquest of Egypt in 332 BCE, the subsequent establishment of the Ptolemaic dynasty and, finally, the death of Cleopatra VII and the incorporation of Egypt into the Roman world in 30 BCE.

Putting these events alongside the construction of the pyramids is particularly revealing. The Great Pyramid at Giza was already roughly two and a half thousand years old by the time of Cleopatra, meaning that the interval between its construction and her lifetime was greater than that between her lifetime and our own.

We tend to group the pyramids and Cleopatra together under the heading of ancient Egypt, yet the timescale separating them is substantial in its own right.

## The Nile and the Black Land

Underlying much of this history was a geographical feature of fundamental importance to Egyptian civilisation: the Nile.

Prior to the regulation of the river in modern times, its annual inundation deposited fertile sediment across parts of the floodplain, providing the basis for agriculture within an otherwise arid landscape. The contrast between the cultivated land bordering the river and the surrounding desert was fundamental to the geography of Egyptian settlement and the term _Kemet_, commonly interpreted as _the Black Land_, is traditionally associated with the dark, fertile soil of the Nile Valley that provided a contrast to the surrounding desert, often referred to as _the Red Land_.

The Nile was far more than simply a source of water. It provided a means of transport and communication between communities, its flood cycle influenced agricultural activities and the availability of agricultural surpluses helped sustain people engaged in activities other than the production of food.

Interestingly, the inundation wasn't an unqualified benefit as the extent of flooding varied, and both inadequate and excessive floods could have serious consequences for agricultural production. The relationship between the river and Egyptian society was therefore one of dependence upon a natural process whose behaviour was not entirely predictable.

Given my interests in natural history, I find this relationship between the physical environment and the development of human societies particularly interesting. The chronology of Egyptian civilisation is, to a considerable extent, the history of a society developing within the opportunities and constraints presented by its environment.

## Exchange, Trade and the Emergence of Coinage

One question I recorded for later investigation, based on the content of one of the displays, was whether Alexander's conquest introduced coinage into Egypt. Arriving home and investigating further revealed that the historical picture is rather more complicated.

Coins were already known in Egypt through foreign contacts before Alexander's arrival and some coinage was produced before the Macedonian conquest, though for much of ancient Egyptian history economic transactions involved the exchange and redistribution of goods rather than the use of coinage. Grain, for instance, could form part of the remuneration of workers, while metals could serve as measures of value without necessarily changing hands in every transaction.

Underpinning this was a system of standardised weights, one of the principal units being the _deben_, which by the New Kingdom was equivalent to approximately 91 grams and could be subdivided into ten smaller units known as _qedet_. Although these units could be used to measure physical quantities of metal, they also provided a means of expressing the relative economic value of other commodities.

An interesting example survives in an inscription on a fragment of pottery, known as an _ostracon_, from Deir el-Medina, the settlement inhabited by workers responsible for constructing royal tombs. It records the purchase of a coffin valued at 25.5 deben of copper, for which payment was made in the form of two goats, a pig, two sycamore logs and 13.5 deben of copper. The remaining 12 deben represented the assessed value of the animals and timber.

This distinction is worth making because the absence of coinage doesn't imply the absence of a sophisticated system for assigning and comparing the value of goods. In this particular transaction, copper served both as a _unit of account_, providing a common measure of economic value, and as a _medium of exchange_, forming part of the actual payment. 

The use of coins expanded considerably under the Ptolemies, though earlier forms of payment and exchange continued alongside it, and Egypt had established mechanisms for monetary valuation long before minted coins came into widespread use.

It's tempting when constructing a historical timeline to associate a particular change with a particular date or event but in this case Alexander's conquest provides a convenient chronological reference point rather than a simple dividing line between two entirely different economic systems.

## An Encounter with Hieroglyphics

Ancient Egypt is another subject that has previously inspired a software project, in this case a _Hieroglyphics Transliterator_.

One of the particularly interesting aspects of Egyptian hieroglyphic writing is that the signs don't all perform the same function. Some represent individual consonants or combinations of consonants, others can represent entire words, and others, known as _determinatives_, help clarify the meaning of a word without contributing to its pronunciation. Vowels were generally not explicitly represented. The script is therefore considerably more complex than a simple substitution of hieroglyphic signs for the letters of our own alphabet.

The direction in which hieroglyphics are read is also interesting as they may be arranged horizontally or vertically and in horizontal inscriptions the writing may run from left to right or from right to left, with signs representing people and animals generally facing towards the beginning of the text.

The _Hieroglyphics Transliterator_ originated from my own interest in ancient Egyptian writing and was developed largely for fun at a time when I was learning about hieroglyphics. The process of developing the application provided another means of exploring a subject that interested me, encouraging me to investigate the individual signs, their phonetic associations and some of the conventions governing their use.

The transliteration engine uses a correspondence table, loaded from a CSV file, to associate individual characters with hieroglyphic signs. A word is processed letter by letter, with unsupported characters omitted from the transliteration. This is a deliberately simplified approach rather than an attempt to translate modern text into ancient Egyptian, and it doesn't attempt to reproduce the grammatical or orthographic conventions of the ancient language.

What it does provide, though, is an interactive environment in which to explore selected hieroglyphic signs. Entering familiar words and phrases provides an accessible introduction to otherwise unfamiliar symbols, helping to establish associations between signs and their approximate phonetic values.

And, frankly, there's also something rather satisfying about seeing a familiar word represented using symbols drawn from a writing system developed thousands of years ago! That visual transformation can itself prompt curiosity about the origins of the signs, the objects they depict and the ways in which those representations came to be associated with the sounds of a spoken language.

## What Does 'Ancient' Really Mean?

One reflection from the Egyptology displays stands out above the others. We describe ancient Egypt as an _ancient_ civilisation, and rightly so in the context of recorded human history. Its roots extend back several millennia, with increasingly complex communities developing in the Nile Valley long before the emergence of the dynastic state and the appearance of writing.

Yet the preceding palaeontology exhibition had taken me back to the Silurian seas of approximately _420 million years ago_. To put those very different timescales into perspective, if we were to compress the interval between those Silurian seas and the present day into a single year, the approximately 5,000 years since the emergence of the Egyptian dynastic state would occupy little more than six minutes at the end of the final day.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.geological_timescale %}

The word _ancient_ takes on a very different meaning depending upon whether we're considering human history or geological time and, while that's a distinction I'm well aware of in principle, encountering the two collections within the same museum brought it into particularly sharp focus.

There is another connection with the palaeontology displays, though, relating to the nature of the evidence from which we reconstruct the past. The emergence of writing gave subsequent historians access to a new kind of evidence, supplementing that provided by objects, structures, human remains and the environments in which people lived. Written records are particularly valuable but, like the objects recovered by archaeologists, they offer an incomplete and sometimes selective account of the societies that produced them.

Earlier in the day I'd been considering the _Chirotherium_ footprints preserved in Triassic sandstone and how they provided evidence of the activity of animals whose bodies were not necessarily preserved. The evidence available to Egyptologists is very different, but there is a principle common to both disciplines, in that what survives is only a portion of what actually happened.

Whether we're investigating life in the Silurian seas or the development of civilisation in the Nile Valley, the task is to construct a defensible account of the past from the evidence that remains. The two exhibitions offered very different perspectives on history, but also a rather satisfying connection between them.
