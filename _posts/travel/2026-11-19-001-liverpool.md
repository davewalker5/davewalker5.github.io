I came to Liverpool intending to visit museums, revisit a city I love, explore a little and enjoy myself. I hadn't anticipated spending part of an afternoon in a pub contemplating the passing of forty-three years. But then the memorable and significant moments of a trip aren't always on the itinerary.

## A Little Musical History

I've loved the Beatles since I was about thirteen. Technically, they weren't quite my generation. I was only five when the band split up in 1970 but I discovered their music during my early teens, and they've been a constant musical presence ever since, so I've always regarded them as _my_ era.

I love their early recordings, their extraordinary development as musicians and songwriters, and particularly their later work. _Let It Be_ and _Hey Jude_ remain among my favourite songs, while George Harrison's _All Things Must Pass_ is a particular favourite from the post-Beatles years. Somewhere along the way, I've accumulated most of their albums on vinyl.

So, with a whole day available during a return visit to Liverpool, I decided to visit both _The Beatles Story_ and _The Liverpool Beatles Museum_. The first visit was thoroughly enjoyable. Immersing myself in the history of music that has accompanied so much of my life was an experience in itself.

Afterwards, with no particular rush and no appetite for lunch, I decided to find somewhere for a small glass of wine before visiting the second museum. Simple enough.

## In Search of a Proper Pub

My route took me from the docks through the modern city-centre shopping centre. It's impressive, with contemporary architecture, fashionable restaurants and smart-looking shops and bars yet, for reasons I couldn't immediately explain, it left me completely cold. None of the places appealed.

What I wanted, I eventually realised, was an old-fashioned pub. Nothing particularly sophisticated. Just somewhere with a bit of character, a proper wooden table and a comfortable atmosphere. So the intended glass of wine became a pint of Guinness, and I found myself sitting in _McSwiggin's_. Then _There She Goes_ by The La's came on over the sound system.

Another Liverpool band. Of course it did. Sometimes a city seems determined to provide its own soundtrack and sitting there, pint in hand, I found myself unexpectedly reflective, nostalgic and, rather to my surprise, quite emotional. Not unhappy. Not distressed. Just unusually moved.

Which made me wonder why.

## The City and the Student

Forty-three years earlier, in 1983, I had arrived in Liverpool at the age of eighteen to study Chemistry for my first degree. I'd left home and was beginning my adult life but like most eighteen-year-olds, I had only the vaguest idea of what lay ahead. There would be people to meet, places to visit, relationships to build, decisions to make and experiences I couldn't possibly have anticipated.

Liverpool became part of those formative years and now, more than four decades later, here I was again. What struck me was how much I still loved the place. Liverpool has history, character and a distinctive identity. It's a relatively compact city, with a friendliness and warmth that I remember from my student days.

The city has changed, naturally, and so have I, but that affection has remained. Perhaps that was part of what I had been looking for in escaping the modern shopping centre - I wasn't necessarily searching for somewhere unchanged since the 1980s because cities can't and likely shouldn't remain frozen in time, but I did want somewhere that felt connected to the Liverpool I remembered.

A traditional pub seemed to fit the bill rather better than a fashionable cocktail bar. Particularly with a pint of Guinness!

## Music, Memory and Identity

Music has a remarkable ability to connect us with earlier periods of our lives. A familiar song can bring back a particular time, place or feeling with astonishing immediacy, sometimes without our consciously trying to remember anything at all. Psychologists have identified something called the _reminiscence bump_, the tendency for people to recall a disproportionately large number of autobiographical memories from adolescence and early adulthood.

Those are, of course, the very periods when we begin establishing our identities, developing independent interests and making some of the decisions that shape our adult lives and for me, the Beatles connect directly with my teenage years, Liverpool connects with the beginning of adulthood and here I was, revisiting both at once.

I suspect that's why the morning had such an unexpected emotional impact. The music and the city weren't simply reminding me of individual events - they were connecting me with earlier versions of myself:

- The thirteen-year-old discovering the Beatles
- The eighteen-year-old arriving in Liverpool
- The old man sitting in a pub in 2026, still loving the same music and the same city

There's something rather satisfying about that continuity.

## The Peculiar Business of Passing Time

It's easy to assume that nostalgia is primarily about missing the past, or wishing things could return to the way they once were but that wasn't what I was experiencing. I wasn't wishing myself back to 1983 - by and large, I've had a happy and interesting life, full of experiences I couldn't have imagined when I first arrived in Liverpool. I've travelled, pursued interests, learned new things and developed projects that have taken me in all sorts of unexpected directions and, crucially, I'm still doing those things.

Indeed, one of the pleasures of this particular visit has been exploring the _World Museum_ and finding unexpected connections between its exhibits and my own interests in natural history, evolutionary science and computational modelling. I arrived in Liverpool all those years ago to study science and more than four decades later, I'm still fascinated by it.

Some things, apparently, don't change very much!

Nevertheless, there's something undeniably poignant about looking back across forty-three years. Not because those years have been unhappy, or because I regret how I've spent them, quite the opposite. _Poignancy_ is a particular mixture of happiness and sadness, an appreciation of something meaningful, accompanied by an awareness that time passes and experiences cannot be repeated in quite the same way, and that seems a reasonable description of what I felt. A kind of affection for the life I've lived, tempered by the realisation of how quickly the years have passed.

## Still the Same Person?

Of course, I'm not the person I was at eighteen yet, in a curious way, I've never entirely stopped feeling like him. Somewhere inside my head, that eighteen-year-old is still very much present. I've accumulated forty-three years of experiences, knowledge and, hopefully, a little wisdom, but my internal sense of _being me_ doesn't seem to have aged in quite the same way as the rest of me.

It's a peculiar sensation. I can still feel remarkably close to the young man who arrived in Liverpool in 1983, as though the intervening decades have changed almost everything about my life without fundamentally changing the person experiencing it. Then, of course, I look in the mirror and find a sixty-one-year-old looking back at me.

Which can come as something of a surprise! 😉

Experience changes us. Our understanding of the world develops, our priorities shift, and life inevitably takes us in directions we hadn't anticipated but it would be wrong to suggest that _everything_ changes. Some interests and affections become remarkably durable:

- The music I discovered as a teenager still gives me enormous pleasure
- The scientific curiosity that brought me to Liverpool remains as strong as ever
- The city that welcomed an eighteen-year-old Chemistry student in 1983 can still make him feel completely at home in 2026

Forty-three years is a substantial stretch of anyone's life, long enough for extraordinary changes, but also long enough to discover which things have remained constant, and sometimes it takes something as simple as a familiar city, a song and a pint of Guinness to make those connections suddenly apparent.

## Back to the Itinerary

Eventually, of course, there was another Beatles museum to visit. The afternoon wasn't intended as an exercise in autobiography, and there was plenty more of Liverpool to enjoy but I'm rather glad I took that unexpected detour into _McSwiggin's_. It reminded me that travel isn't always about discovering somewhere new - sometimes it's about returning to somewhere familiar and discovering that your relationship with it has acquired another forty-three years of meaning.

The city has changed. I've changed. The affection remains. And the music still sounds wonderful.

_Now, where was that second museum?_
