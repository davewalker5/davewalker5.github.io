One of the things I enjoy about visiting different parts of the country, and indeed the world, is exploring their local history and culture and, of course, food is as much a part of that culture as architecture, language, music or anything we might encounter in a museum.

So, during our visit to Liverpool in November 2026, there was one particular culinary experience on the agenda - eating _Scouse_. I was familiar with the dish and its association with Liverpool but I was curious about its origins and how a relatively simple stew came to lend its name to the inhabitants of an entire city.

## A Dish with Maritime Origins

Scouse takes its name from _lobscouse_, a dish associated with sailors throughout Northern Europe. The word appears in English records as early as the beginning of the eighteenth century, although its ultimate linguistic origins are uncertain. <sup>[2,4]</sup> Early versions of lobscouse typically consisted of salted meat, vegetables and ship's biscuit, a practical, inexpensive and sustaining meal that could be prepared using ingredients suited to life aboard ship. <sup>[2,4]</sup>

Related dishes survive in several northern European countries and while they differ in their ingredients and preparation, their names reflect a shared culinary and linguistic heritage <sup>[2]</sup>:

- __Norway__ – _Lapskaus_
- __Denmark__ – _Skipperlabskovs_
- __Germany__ – _Labskaus_
- __Sweden__ – _Lapskojs_

It's not terribly surprising that a dish with maritime associations should have become established in Liverpool. As the city developed into one of Britain's most important trading ports, its docks became a meeting place for people, languages, customs and culinary traditions from across the world and amongst the things that travelled aboard those ships were ideas about what to have for dinner!

## From the Docks to the Domestic Kitchen

Over time, Scouse became established as a staple of Liverpool's working-class households, moving beyond its maritime origins and developing into a recognisably local dish. <sup>[2]</sup>

There is documentary evidence of its popularity towards the end of the eighteenth century and Sir Frederick Morton Eden's _The State of the Poor_, published in 1797, records the purchase of substantial quantities of beef, potatoes and onions specifically for the preparation of Scouse at Liverpool's poorhouse. <sup>[2]</sup>

The traditional Liverpool version generally consists of beef or lamb, potatoes, carrots and onions, slowly cooked together to produce a hearty stew but, as with many traditional regional dishes, there is no single definitive recipe, and individual households have developed their own variations. <sup>[5]</sup>

It was particularly well suited to households with limited financial resources, combining relatively inexpensive ingredients in a single pot to produce a substantial meal for an entire family. When meat was unaffordable, a version could be prepared without it, a dish that became known as _Blind Scouse_, a reminder of the economic circumstances in which the dish developed. Today, meat-free Scouse also provides a basis for vegetarian and vegan adaptations. <sup>[5]</sup>

Traditionally, Scouse is served with bread and pickled red cabbage or beetroot, the acidity of the pickled vegetables providing a contrast to the richness of the stew. <sup>[5]</sup>

## How Liverpool's Inhabitants Became Scousers

Perhaps the most interesting aspect of the history of Scouse is the way in which the name of the dish became associated with the people who ate it, the association between the dish and Liverpool becoming sufficiently strong that the terms _Scouse_ and _Scouser_ came to be applied to the city's inhabitants. The development of the nickname was gradual, and it was not necessarily intended as a compliment, as there is evidence that it was used somewhat disparagingly by outsiders, reflecting the association of the dish with poverty and working-class life. <sup>[2]</sup>

The term also circulated amongst members of the armed forces, becoming more widely known during the first half of the twentieth century, particularly around the time of the Second World War, and by the latter half of the twentieth century, _Scouser_ had become an established expression of Liverpool identity, and _Scouse_ had also become the name by which the city's distinctive accent and dialect were widely known. <sup>[2,3,4]</sup>

The timeline, from Professor Tony Crowley's research <sup>[2]</sup>, is shown below:

| Period             | Development                                                                                                         |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Early 18th century | Lobscouse recorded as the name of a dish associated with sailors                                                    |
| Late 18th century  | Scouse established as a dish eaten in Liverpool                                                                     |
| 19th century       | Dicky Sam was the predominant nickname for an inhabitant of Liverpool                                               |
| Late 19th century  | Scouse emerged as a nickname for Liverpudlians, with somewhat derogatory associations                               |
| Early 20th century | The nickname circulated in Army and Navy slang                                                                      |
| 1920s–1930s        | Scouse began to displace Dicky Sam, although it faced competition from another nickname, Wacker                     |
| Second World War   | Use of the nickname became more widespread                                                                          |
| 1950s              | Scouser increasingly adopted by Liverpudlians themselves, and Scouse emerged as a name for their distinctive speech |
| 1960s              | Increasing prominence of Scouse language and identity in popular culture                                            |
| 1970s              | General adoption of Scouser as the predominant nickname for Liverpool's inhabitants                                 |

The progression in meaning is interesting:

- A dish eaten by sailors
- A staple of Liverpool's working-class households
- A nickname for the inhabitants of Liverpool
- A name for the city's distinctive accent and dialect
- An expression of cultural identity and local pride

A relatively humble stew has given its name not only to the people of a city but also to the way in which they speak.

## Ma Boyle's Alehouse and Eatery

For our own encounter with Scouse, we chose Ma Boyle's Alehouse and Eatery, tucked away in Tower Gardens, close to Liverpool's waterfront and the Pier Head. <sup>[1]</sup>

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.ma_boyles_scouse %}

Ma Boyle's has a considerable history of its own. Established by Kay Boyle in 1860, it began life as a coffee shop before subsequently becoming an oyster bar and, eventually, the alehouse and eatery that exists today and has occupied its present location in the Tower Building since 1974. <sup>[1]</sup>

Given the maritime origins of Scouse, there is something particularly appropriate about eating it in an establishment with such a long history, situated so close to the waterfront through which generations of sailors passed, and Ma Boyle's takes its Scouse seriously enough to have an entire menu dedicated to it!

Alongside the Classic Scouse, the menu offered several variations:

- __Classic Scouse__ – slow-cooked beef with potatoes, carrots, onions, swede and celeriac in a red wine gravy.
- __Classic Guinness Scouse__ – beef marinated in Guinness, accompanied by vegetables in a Guinness gravy.
- __Spicy Scouse__ – a variation incorporating chorizo, beef, sweet potato, paprika, chillies and jalapeños.
- __Blind Scouse__ – a vegan interpretation of the traditional meat-free dish.

Each of the four varieties was also available as a pie, either under a puff pastry lid or topped with spring onion mash in the style of a cottage pie, an interesting example of how a traditional regional dish can continue to evolve without losing its association with the place and culture from which it emerged.

## Our Scouse Experience

It was the perfect venue for our introduction to Liverpool's signature dish. Served with homemade pickled red cabbage and buttered sourdough bread, and accompanied by a half of Guinness, it was a wonderfully appropriate meal for a cold November night.

There's something particularly satisfying about this kind of food. It doesn't rely on elaborate presentation or exotic ingredients - its appeal lies in the combination of familiar ingredients, slowly cooked together, producing something hearty and comforting and, as perhaps the best indication of how much we enjoyed it, we went back to Ma Boyle's for Scouse a second time during our visit!

Our Liverpool trip encompassed museums, the city's musical heritage, its architecture and its waterfront, but our visits to Ma Boyle's were very much a part of the experience.

## More Than Just a Stew

Food can offer a fascinating insight into the history of a place. The history of Scouse touches upon international maritime trade, the movement of people and ideas, the economic realities of working-class life, the evolution of language and the emergence of a distinctive regional identity.

What began as a practical meal for sailors became a staple of domestic life in Liverpool and a dish once associated with economic necessity subsequently became something celebrated as part of the city's cultural heritage. There is even an annual _Global Scouse Day_, celebrated on 28 February, dedicated to the dish and its association with Liverpool. <sup>[6]</sup>

I rather like the idea that something as seemingly ordinary as a bowl of stew can carry so much history and, more, that exploring the history and culture of a city doesn't necessarily require a visit to a museum.

Sometimes all it requires is a bowl of Scouse, a piece of bread and a half of Guinness.
