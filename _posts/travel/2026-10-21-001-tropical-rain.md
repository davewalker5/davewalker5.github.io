It's raining. Not the tentative, apologetic sort of rain we so often get at home, but proper tropical rain. The sort that arrives with conviction, hammering on the roof, drumming on the decking and turning the lagoon into a thousand little splashes. And it's wonderful.

There's something deeply satisfying about sitting comfortably under shelter while the weather puts on a performance. The sound of rain on a roof has always appealed to me, but here, in the Maldives, it seems to have an entirely different character. There's nothing restrained about it. When it rains, it *really* rains.

This evening, curiosity got the better of me.

I wondered what the weather system producing all this noise actually looked like. Not from underneath it, which was where I happened to be sitting, but from above. So I went looking for a live weather map. And there it was. _Zoom Earth_ showed the clouds gathered over the atoll. _Meteoblue_ showed the rain. Both presented their information with the quiet confidence of modern meteorological technology.

I was able to confirm their accuracy with an unusually direct form of field observation: looking outside!

Both were absolutely right.

There's something faintly ridiculous, and rather delightful, about using satellite imagery and sophisticated weather models to confirm that it's raining when the evidence is already hammering on the roof above your head. But that's not really why I was looking. What fascinated me was the change of perspective. Here I was, on a tiny island in the Indian Ocean, listening to rain falling just a few feet away. Yet on the screen in front of me I could see the much larger weather system of which that rain was a tiny part.

Two views of precisely the same event. One experienced through sound, sight and sensation; the other observed remotely, through instruments and technology. Neither diminished the other. If anything, each made the other more interesting.

We spend so much of our time using technology to insulate ourselves from the world, or to distract ourselves from what's immediately around us. It's rather nice when it works the other way, helping us appreciate an experience we're already enjoying.

So I've put the weather maps aside for now.

The rain is still falling, the roof is still rattling under the downpour, and somewhere beyond the island the waves are still breaking against the reef.

I think I'll just sit here and listen.

Sometimes the best thing to do with the weather is simply enjoy it.
