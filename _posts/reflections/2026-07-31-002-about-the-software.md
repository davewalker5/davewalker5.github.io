For much of my working life, software was the thing I made.

I began as a developer and eventually became a software architect. Building systems, thinking about structure, explaining designs and helping other people turn requirements into working software were all central parts of my career.

Like many people working in modern software development, I was also expected to maintain a visible body of work and more often than not that meant GitHub.

I was perfectly happy to populate it with well-engineered projects, but I was determined not to fill it with throwaway demonstrations, fashionable technologies or applications I had no genuine interest in.

So I built software connected to things I already cared about.

- Aircraft
- Wildlife observations
- Photography
- Data analysis
- Mathematics

Those projects were real, useful and personally meaningful. But while I was still working, they remained primarily software projects.

I continued the underlying practices — observing aircraft, recording wildlife, taking photographs and following questions — but there was rarely enough time to develop the software into anything much larger.

Since retiring, that has changed dramatically.

## More than having time

It would be easy to say that retirement simply gave me more time and, of course, it did. But I increasingly think the more important change was that the software no longer had to justify itself professionally.

- It did not need to demonstrate competence
- It did not need to use a particular framework
- It did not need to resemble the kind of application someone might expect to find in a professional portfolio

It only needed to help me investigate something I genuinely wanted to understand and that changed the centre of gravity of the work. Instead of asking:

> What application should I build?

I could ask:

> What am I curious about, and what kind of tool would help me investigate it properly?

The distinction sounds small but, in practice, it has transformed almost everything.

## The question comes first

Many of the projects that now form Field Notes began with questions rather than technologies.

- Could shell growth be modelled procedurally in a way that made differences in form easier to understand?
- What assumptions shape the results of a molecular-clock reconstruction?
- How might stromatolite-like layered growth emerge from repeated accretion and burial?
- How could I catalogue fossil specimens while keeping provenance, uncertainty and interpretation visible?
- What patterns emerge in the airspace above my home when individual ADS-B observations are accumulated over time?

The software followed from those questions. Sometimes the right tool was a Streamlit application. Sometimes it was a Jupyter notebook. Sometimes it was a SQLite database, an interactive visualisation, a simulator or a set of command-line utilities.

The technology mattered, and I still cared about building it well. But it was no longer the reason the project existed.

## The architect did not disappear

None of this means that I stopped thinking like a software architect. Quite the opposite. I still care about:

- Clear separation of concerns
- Understandable data models
- Reproducible workflows
- Provenance
- Maintainability
- Explicit assumptions
- Distinguishing observation from interpretation
- Choosing the simplest infrastructure that solves the problem properly

Those habits remain deeply ingrained.

Instead, what changed was where I applied them.

During my career, the systems belonged to organisations, customers or professional programmes of work. Now I could apply the same discipline to my own questions. The architecture was no longer serving a product roadmap: It was serving curiosity.

## Different subjects, the same practice

For a long time I thought of my interests as largely separate.

- Wildlife was one thing
- Aircraft were another
- Fossils, microscopy, shell morphology, evolutionary modelling and sporting data all appeared to belong to different parts of my life

Increasingly, I think that is the wrong way to look at them. The subjects differ, but the practice is remarkably consistent:

1. Observe carefully
2. Keep good records
3. Distinguish evidence from interpretation
4. Build better tools where necessary
5. Look for patterns
6. Revise the question when the evidence suggests something more interesting
7. Share the result

That is the thread connecting much of Field Notes.

The software is part of that process, but it is not the whole of it.

## Making observation visible

One of the most satisfying parts of this work is that software can make something visible that would otherwise remain difficult to perceive.

- A fossil catalogue can reveal the shape and gaps of a collection
- A morphospace explorer can make relationships between shell forms visually apparent
- A simulation can expose the consequences of assumptions that would be hard to reason about in the abstract
- A density plot built from thousands of ADS-B positions can reveal the structure of the airspace above a particular place

That last example brought the wider change into especially sharp focus.

I was looking at the first historical reports from the ADS-B Tracker when I realised that the project was no longer only about following individual aircraft.

I still enjoy doing that enormously. Aircraft remain large, noisy and fascinating machines, and part of me will probably always be the child who wanted to become a pilot.

But the accumulated data was beginning to show something larger.

The aircraft were revealing the character of the airspace around my home.

The software had become an observational instrument.

## Curiosity with discipline

Retirement has allowed my curiosity and imagination to range far more freely than they could while I was working.

That freedom is enormously enjoyable but I also know that I would be dissatisfied if I did not pursue those interests properly and, for me, that means being careful about evidence.

- It means recording where information came from
- It means making uncertainty visible
- It means avoiding claims that the data cannot support
- It means retaining raw observations where possible and keeping them distinct from later interpretation

The subject may be a hobby but the method still matters.

That combination — curiosity allowed to run widely, but pursued with discipline — has become one of the defining pleasures of Field Notes.

## Sharing the work

Originally, I think part of my motivation for publishing work came from a sense of impermanence and making something public felt like making it a little more durable.

That motivation has gradually become less important and I no longer think much about leaving something permanent behind. What matters more is making the work available.

I would be delighted if someone encountered a post, notebook, model or visualisation and became curious in turn.

- They might look at an aircraft-density plot and wonder what the airspace above their own home looks like
- They might see a shell model and begin thinking about how growth produces form
- They might read about a fossil specimen and follow a geological question of their own

I will probably never know whether that happens. But if I do not put the work out into the world, it cannot happen at all. Sharing is therefore not an afterthought or a way of measuring success through audience size.

It is an invitation.

## A different relationship with software

Looking back, the software projects I built during my career were not unrelated to what Field Notes has become.

They contained the beginnings of it:

- I was already choosing subjects I cared about
- I was already trying to build things carefully
- I was already combining observation, data and software where I could

What retirement provided was the freedom for those strands to reinforce one another.

- The curiosity was already there
- The engineering skill was already there
- The observational practice was already there

They simply no longer had to compete with the demands of a working life.

And that has led me to a sentence that now feels central to the whole project:

> **Software stopped being the thing I made and became one of the ways I investigate the things that interest me.**

That does not make the software less important.

It gives it a richer purpose.

It is no longer merely the product of the work.

It is one of the ways I pay attention.
