When I visited the Oxford University Museum of Natural History a few weeks ago, I thought I was simply spending an enjoyable afternoon looking at fossils. I had no idea that one museum visit would lead to months of reading, modelling, programming, sketching, collecting and writing.

The catalyst was surprisingly small: One of the display labels mentioned that ammonite shells follow a logarithmic spiral. That was enough. Not enough to understand logarithmic spirals, of course, but enough to make me curious.

> _I wanted to know why_

That question eventually became a shell growth modelling project. Building mathematical models proved to be a remarkably effective way of understanding the geometry, because every assumption had to be made explicit. By the time the project was complete I felt that I understood shell morphology far better than I had from simply reading about it.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.morphospace %}

But curiosity rarely stops where you expect.

The same museum visit made me curious about stromatolites. Those strange layered structures prompted another question: how do microbial mats actually build something that survives in the fossil record? That became a completely different modelling project, exploring biological growth, sediment deposition and environmental variation.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.stromatolite %}

Meanwhile, reading about early life naturally led to Stephen Jay Gould’s _Wonderful Life_. Gould’s discussion of the Cambrian explosion and the Burgess Shale fossils opened another door. Rather than simply reading about animals such as _Marrella_, _Yohoia_ and _Opabinia_, I found myself sketching fossils from published photographs. Drawing them forced me to slow down and pay attention to their anatomy in a way that simply looking at illustrations never could.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.yohoia %}

Those questions about evolution then led somewhere I hadn’t anticipated at all.

> How do scientists reconstruct evolutionary relationships?

That question grew into a molecular clock simulation project, exploring sequence evolution, substitution models and phylogenetic reconstruction. Once again, building the software was less about producing a tool than about understanding the underlying ideas well enough to implement them.

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.relaxedclock %}

At some point I also realised that I wanted to own a few representative fossils—not as ornaments, but as specimens connected to the things I was learning. That, in turn, prompted the development of Fossil Tracker to catalogue and document them properly.

Looking back, none of these projects was planned. Each simply answered a question raised by the previous one. That strikes me as an important lesson.

We often think of learning as a straight path:

- Observe something
- Learn about it
- Move on

In practice, at least for me, it seems to work more like this:

- Observation leads to a question
- Answering that question raises several more
- Some of those become projects
- Those projects change the way I observe the next time
- The cycle repeats

The result is that I’ve found myself drifting steadily backwards through Earth’s history. A visit that I imagined would mostly reinforce my long-standing fascination with dinosaurs instead led me towards stromatolites, the Ediacaran, the Cambrian explosion, Burgess Shale animals and the origins of complex life.

Dinosaurs haven’t become any less interesting. Quite the opposite. They’re now part of a much larger story.

The next time I stand in front of Stan, the museum’s magnificent Tyrannosaurus rex, I suspect I’ll see something rather different. Not simply one of the largest terrestrial predators ever to have lived, but the product of an evolutionary history stretching back billions of years through countless ancestors, innovations and contingencies.

None of that was part of the original plan.

But perhaps that’s the point: Curiosity rarely takes us where we intended to go. It usually takes us somewhere worth visiting.
