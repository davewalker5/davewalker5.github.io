# Beyond the Encounter

The journey described in the preceding chapters began with the straightforward intention of enjoying the marine wildlife of the Maldives, recording some of the encounters and, where possible, identifying the species we had seen.

Four snorkelling excursions around Moofushi produced approximately 50 GB of underwater video, from which a collection of representative images was extracted, examined and catalogued. Three new software applications emerged from the process, each addressing a different question prompted by the recordings, while an existing natural history application provided the means of turning the resulting identifications into structured observations.

The immediate outcome is a personal natural history record: Observations associated with dates and locations, supported by photographic and video evidence, with a practical route back to the original source material. The records can be examined, revised, organised and subjected to descriptive analysis.

## The Potential for Citizen Science

The project has something in common with citizen science, although I would hesitate to describe the recordings themselves as the product of a scientific study. They were not collected under a coordinated research programme or according to a predefined survey methodology. The snorkelling excursions were recreational activities, and the cameras recorded whatever happened to be encountered.

What developed afterwards, however, shares several important practices with structured natural history recording:

- Observations are associated with identifiable dates and locations
- Species identifications are retained alongside their supporting photographic evidence
- Uncertain identifications can remain unresolved rather than being forced into a species catalogue
- The original recordings are preserved, allowing identification decisions to be reconsidered
- Repeated identifications are consolidated into species sightings without treating every marker as a different animal
- The resulting observations can be organised, examined and reported

These practices make the resulting information more useful, understandable and potentially shareable than an unstructured collection of photographs or an unsupported species list.

They do not, however, retrospectively turn the recordings into a scientific survey. The consolidated records can establish that particular species were observed at particular places and times, but they cannot establish population sizes, relative abundance across the reef or ecological changes over time.

## Beyond the Evidence

Looking back, one of the most important aspects of the project has been the effort to distinguish what the recordings demonstrate from what might be tempting to infer from them.

Image enhancement cannot recover information that was never recorded, repeated identifications cannot establish numbers of individual animals, and a catalogue of observed species cannot describe the complete ecological community of a reef, but recognising those limitations does not diminish the value of the resulting observations. Instead, it establishes the boundaries within which they can be meaningfully interpreted.

There is another perspective from which to consider the journey, though - the software was never the original reason for being in the water! The starting point was the experience of encountering wild animals in their own environment, watching reef fish move through the coral, seeing rays pass through the water and spending time in the company of tawny nurse sharks. Those encounters were memorable before any footage was recorded or processed, any species identified or any database record created.

The subsequent work did not make them more significant as personal experiences. Rather, it provided a way to revisit them with greater attention, examine details that might otherwise have gone unnoticed and preserve some of what was learned.

Curiosity led to closer observation, closer observation raised questions, some of which prompted software development while others required careful identification and a disciplined approach to recording. Along the way, the process created opportunities to learn about the animals themselves, about working with underwater recordings and about the distinction between an observation and the conclusions that can reasonably be drawn from it.

The result is not a scientific survey of Moofushi's reefs. It is a documented collection of encounters, organised in a way that makes their observational content accessible and their limitations explicit. It has demonstrated that recreational encounters can become useful, structured and traceable natural history observations without pretending to be something more.

Perhaps the most satisfying conclusion is that none of this diminishes the original experience because knowing more about the animals, understanding how the recordings were interpreted and acknowledging what remains uncertain only adds to the appreciation of having encountered them.
