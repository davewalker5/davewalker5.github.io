# Wildlife Through the 2026 Heatwaves

The spring and summer of 2026 were exceptionally warm in the UK. Significant heat arrived during May and was followed by further periods of unusually high temperature through June, July and August. By the end of the season, meteorological summer 2026 had provisionally become the warmest recorded in the UK series beginning in 1884.

Against that background, the wildlife records for Abingdon offered an opportunity to ask a simple question:

> **Did the exceptional heat leave a visible signature in the wildlife I encountered?**

This is not a controlled ecological survey. The records describe encounters made during ordinary observation, and changes in the number of records cannot by themselves demonstrate changes in population. What they can show is whether familiar species became more or less conspicuous within the landscape — and whether 2026 looked different from 2025 to the same observer.

The species included in the study are a subset of those from the [Wildlife Through the Year In Abingdon](/wildlife/seasonal/) work, selected both because I generally record enough data for them for the results to be meaningful and because they're amongst the species that I, _personally_, take note of in my local area.

## A Quieter Summer?

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.all %}

For all bird observations, the monthly comparison is:

| Month   |    2025 |    2026 |    Change |
| ------- | ------: | ------: | --------: |
| Jan     |     437 |     371 |      −15% |
| Feb     |     339 |     259 |      −24% |
| Mar     |     368 |     366 |       ~0% |
| Apr     |     430 |     348 |      −19% |
| **May** | **428** | **342** |  **−20%** |
| **Jun** | **231** | **161** |  **−30%** |
| **Jul** | **283** | **257** |   **−9%** |
| **Aug** |  **90** | **184** | **+104%** |
| Sep     |     188 |     155 |      −18% |

Across the selected bird species, the broad impression during 2026 was of fewer encounters.

That impression is visible in the records. Across all bird observations, monthly totals were lower than in 2025 through much of the spring and early summer. May fell from 428 observations to 342, June from 231 to 161, and July from 283 to 257.

June produced the clearest difference, with observations approximately 30% below the corresponding month in 2025.

The timing is suggestive. The first significant heat episode occurred during late May, followed by another major period of heat during June. The records therefore show their strongest early-summer reduction at approximately the same time that unusually hot conditions became a persistent feature of the season.

The comparison needs some caution, however. Observation totals were already somewhat lower during parts of early 2026, before the principal heatwaves began. The records therefore do not support attributing the whole difference between the two years to heat.

It may therefore be more useful to think of 2026 as a year in which encounter rates were already somewhat lower, with a particularly pronounced additional divergence emerging during early summer. June stands out in that respect.

Nor did the reduction continue uniformly through the summer.

August was strikingly different: 184 bird observations were recorded in 2026 compared with only 90 in August 2025. Whatever happened during the hot summer, it was not simply a progressive disappearance of birds from the records.

## Fewer Birds, or Birds Less Visible?

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.all_birds %}

This distinction is important.

These records measure **encounters**, not population. A bird which remains present but changes where it spends its time, when it forages, how much it sings, or how readily it enters open ground can become much less visible without becoming less numerous.

In ecological terms this is partly a question of detectability: how likely a species which is present is to be encountered and recorded.

The following is the per-species data:

| Species       |   2025 |   2026 |   Change |
| ------------- | -----: | -----: | -------: |
| Wren          |     27 |      9 | **−67%** |
| Great Tit     |     26 |      9 | **−65%** |
| Blue Tit      |     22 |     10 | **−55%** |
| Dunnock       |     11 |      6 | **−45%** |
| Song Thrush   |     14 |      8 | **−43%** |
| House Sparrow |     51 |     34 | **−33%** |
| Robin         |     47 |     34 | **−28%** |
| Swallow       |     24 |     18 | **−25%** |
| Swift         |     28 |     22 | **−21%** |
| Blackbird     |     76 |     70 |      −8% |
| Woodpigeon    |    113 |    117 |  **+4%** |
| **Magpie**    | **69** | **95** | **+38%** |

Several familiar resident birds became markedly less conspicuous during the heat-affected months from May to September.

Wren observations fell by approximately 67% compared with the same period in 2025. Great Tit fell by 65%, Blue Tit by 55%, Dunnock by 45%, House Sparrow by 33%, and Robin by 28%.

Blackbird was much closer to the previous year, falling by only around 8%.

The records cannot determine how much of these differences represents population change and how much represents changed behaviour or detectability. Their clearest conclusion is therefore not that there were fewer birds, but that **many familiar birds were encountered less frequently**.

For an observational record, that difference matters.

## The Magpie Exception

Not everything became less visible.

Magpies were one of the most conspicuous exceptions — something which was noticeable in the field before examining the figures.

Between May and September, Magpie observations increased from 69 in 2025 to 95 in 2026, an increase of approximately 38%. From April onwards, every monthly total was higher than during the corresponding month of 2025.

Woodpigeons were similarly resistant to the broader decline. Their May–September totals were effectively unchanged, increasing slightly from 113 to 117 observations.

The differences between species are perhaps more informative than an overall reduction would have been. The summer did not simply suppress wildlife observations equally. Some species became much less conspicuous while others remained prominent — and Magpies became more so.

## Swallows and Swifts

The two summer migrants included in the comparison also appeared less frequently overall.

Swallow observations between May and September fell from 24 in 2025 to 18 in 2026, a reduction of 25%. The difference was particularly noticeable early in the season: May fell from 11 observations to 5, while June fell from 5 to only 1.

Swifts show a more complicated pattern. Overall observations fell from 28 to 22, but this conceals considerable variation between months. May actually increased from 5 to 7 observations and July from 10 to 13, while June fell sharply from 11 to 2. No Swifts were recorded during August 2026.

The migrant records therefore support the impression of a relatively poor year overall, but not a simple relationship with the local heatwaves. Migration, conditions elsewhere on the migration route, breeding success and the enormous distances over which these birds can forage all complicate the picture.

Here again, the observations are better understood as a record of **visibility within the local landscape** than as a direct measure of population.

## The Flowers Changed Too

{% include fullwidth-image.html assets=page.assets img=page.all_flora %}

The wildflower records provide an interesting comparison because they show a broadly similar change emerging during early summer:

| Species            | 2025 | 2026 |   Change |
| ------------------ | ---: | ---: | -------: |
| Red Campion        |   22 |    8 | **−64%** |
| Common Cleavers    |   25 |   16 | **−36%** |
| Red Dead-nettle    |   20 |    8 | **−60%** |
| Buttercup          |   38 |   30 | **−21%** |
| Rosebay Willowherb |   27 |   20 | **−26%** |
| Daisy              |   67 |   63 |  **−6%** |
| Dandelion          |   50 |   41 | **−18%** |
| Cuckoo-pint        |   13 |    7 | **−46%** |
| Common Poppy       |   37 |   52 | **+41%** |


April 2026 actually produced more wildflower observations than April 2025, while May was almost identical between the two years. In June, however, observations fell from 308 in 2025 to 180 in 2026 — a reduction of approximately 42%.

The response was again species-specific.

Red Campion, Red Dead-nettle and Buttercup all became substantially less prominent in the records, while Common Poppy moved in the opposite direction and was recorded more frequently during June and July 2026.

As with the birds, there is no single uniform response. Different species contributed differently to the changing appearance of the landscape.

## Visibility Rather Than Abundance

The strongest conclusion from these records is therefore also a deliberately cautious one.

Against a background of somewhat lower encounter rates earlier in 2026, the exceptional heat coincided with a particularly marked change in the wildlife being encountered around Abingdon during early summer, especially June. Many familiar resident birds became less conspicuous, Swallows and Swifts were recorded less frequently overall, and several flowering plants showed substantial reductions in observations.

But these records do not constitute population counts.

What changed most clearly was **visibility**.

The distinction is particularly apparent in a year when some species declined sharply in the records while others remained stable or increased, and when overall bird observations rebounded strongly during August.

Rather than showing a landscape emptied of wildlife by the heat, the records suggest something subtler: **the character of the observable landscape changed**.

Some of its familiar inhabitants became quieter or harder to encounter. Others remained conspicuous. A few — particularly the Magpies — seemed almost to take possession of the space left behind.

As an observational record of one unusually hot year, that may ultimately be the most interesting result.
